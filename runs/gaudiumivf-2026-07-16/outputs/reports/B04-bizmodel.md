## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description
Gaudium runs a chain of fertility (IVF) clinics built on a "hub-and-spoke" model — 7 full-service
labs (hubs) backed by 28 smaller referral centres (spokes) across 9 states — and increasingly
resells medicines through an in-house pharmacy subsidiary alongside the core IVF service (RHP
p.163, 166-167; Inv. Pres. Q4&FY26 slide 15).

### 1B. Money flow chain, by revenue stream

| Stream | Input | What the company does | What it delivers | Who pays | How they pay |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IVF Treatment (64% of FY26 revenue) | Couple/individual with infertility, doctor + embryologist time, lab consumables, leased clinic space | Ovarian stimulation, egg retrieval, fertilisation/ICSI, embryo culture, embryo transfer (RHP p.168-170) | A completed treatment cycle (OPU and/or ET), success measured as a positive pregnancy test | The patient (self-pay; not insurance-covered per company's own SWOT — RHP p.178, "Threats: elective procedures not always covered by insurance") | ~70% of package collected at Ovum Pick-Up, remainder staged over the cycle; company also runs a "flexible credit scheme" that lets some of this slip into instalments (RHP p.167 process flow; Deven Choksey risk factors, p.7-8) |
| Hospital (4% of FY26 revenue) | 15-bed day-care facility at the Janakpuri (Delhi) hub only | Delivery packages, routine post-conception monitoring months 4-9 (RHP p.166) | Childbirth/delivery care, follow-up support | Patient | Package priced by room category, paid directly |
| Pharmacy (31% of FY26 revenue, and the fastest-growing line — see flag below) | Medicines and consumables sourced by wholly-owned subsidiary Gaudium International Pvt Ltd | Supplies drugs/consumables to patients during and after treatment across all 7 centres (RHP p.166) | Prescribed medicines | Patient | Point-of-sale, retail-style |

**FLAG — mix shift is the single biggest business-model fact here.** In three years the pharmacy
trading subsidiary has gone from an afterthought (0.5% of revenue, FY23) to nearly a third of
consolidated revenue (31%, FY26), while the core IVF service line has shrunk from over 90% to
about 64% of the mix. This is not a neutral change: pharmacy is a lower-margin, lower-moat
resale business layered onto a specialist clinical brand. It shows up directly in margins — Q4FY26
**standalone** (largely ex-pharmacy) EBITDA margin was 50.81% vs **consolidated** Q4FY26 40.10%
(Inv. Pres. Q4&FY26 slide 27 vs slide 29) — a ~1,070bp gap attributable mainly to the pharmacy
subsidiary. Consolidated EBITDA margin has fallen from 45.36% (FY23) to 36.13% (FY26) even as
revenue nearly tripled (RHP p.171; Inv. Pres. Q4&FY26 slide 25). Any bull case built on "premium
fertility brand economics" needs to net out how much of consolidated revenue and margin is really
IVF versus a low-differentiation drug-resale business.

**FLAG — international/forex tension.** Marketing materials claim a "Global Patient Base... treated
patients from 30+ countries" and cite medical-tourism cost advantages (70-80% cheaper than the
US) as a growth driver (Inv. Pres. Q4&FY26 slide 18; Deven Choksey p.5). But the RHP's own
patient-country table shows international patients at **1.03% of total boarded patients in FY23,
1.44% in FY24, 6.65% in FY25, and 5.78% in H1 FY26** (12, 32, 104 and 76 patients respectively,
against 1,167 / 2,229 / 1,563 / 1,315 total boarded — RHP p.169-170). And the statutory Annual
Report FY2024-25 discloses **foreign exchange earnings as NIL for both FY2024-25 and FY2023-24**
(AR FY24-25, Board's Report, Conservation of Energy/Foreign Exchange section). The "25-30%
international mix" figure referenced in this stage's brief is **not supported by either document** —
treat it as an unverified market claim, not evidence. It is possible international patients pay
domestically in INR (through relatives/agents) rather than remitted forex, which would explain the
NIL forex-earnings line without contradicting a somewhat higher true patient count than the RHP
table shows for the most recent period — but the RHP's own count data caps plausible international
patient share in the low-to-mid single digits by volume, not 25-30%. This is a management question
(see 4D).

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version
Imagine a chain of specialist baby-making clinics, like a chain of very high-skill dental clinics but
for fertility. A family walks in, pays for a package (like paying an instalment plan for a big
purchase), and over a few weeks the clinic's doctors and lab technicians try to help them conceive.
The clinic doesn't own its buildings — it rents everything, so it's a light business to expand,
like a franchise. But there's a catch: about a third of what the company now calls "revenue" isn't
the fertility treatment at all — it's the in-house pharmacy simply reselling medicines, more like a
chemist shop bolted onto the hospital. And unlike a chemist shop that gets paid cash on the counter,
the company lets a lot of its fertility patients pay in instalments, so the cash comes in much
slower than the revenue is booked — nearly six months' worth is sitting as "money owed" rather than
"money in the bank."

**Overall:** the moat is concentrated in an industry-wide regulatory barrier plus an emerging,
governance-flagged IP asset (GAAT) and a personal-brand/founder-clinician reputation. There is no
durable scale or network-effect advantage versus the PE-backed national chains, several of which
are 5-25x Gaudium's revenue.

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage
**Growth stage, early consolidation.** The Indian IVF market is projected to grow from USD 1.32bn
(2024) to USD 4.54bn (2034E), a 13.13% CAGR, versus a 7.10% global CAGR (Inv. Pres. Q4&FY26 slide
11; Deven Choksey p.4). Penetration is very low: ~27.5 million infertile couples in India versus
~300,000 IVF cycles performed annually — under 2% of addressable demand is being treated, or "1 in
50 couples" (Inv. Pres. Q4&FY26 slide 11). The industry is "transitioning from fragmented
standalone clinics to organized platforms" with regulatory tightening (ART Act) "accelerating the
shift toward compliant, scalable players" (same source). Gaudium is a small-scale player racing
larger, better-capitalised, PE-backed incumbents in this consolidation window.

## SECTION 3: FINANCIAL METRICS THAT MATTER FOR THIS BUSINESS MODEL

### 3A. Ignore-these, track-these

| Commonly tracked ratio | Why it is misleading/irrelevant here |
|---|---|
| Fixed Asset Turnover / capex-intensity ratios | Business is asset-light (all leased premises — RHP p.175); most non-current assets are Right-of-Use, Goodwill and Intangibles, not owned productive PP&E, so classic manufacturing-style turnover ratios don't map cleanly |
| Consolidated Inventory Turnover | Blends a pure fertility-service business (near-zero inventory) with a drug-resale trading subsidiary; a single consolidated number obscures which segment is driving it |
| Reported Net Profit Margin, viewed alone | Can mask weak cash conversion: CFO/PAT ratio was only 0.45x in FY25 and 0.33x in FY24, i.e. reported profit is running well ahead of actual cash collected, due to the flexible credit scheme and staged revenue recognition (Deven Choksey risk factors p.8) |

### 4D. Highest-value management questions

1. Pharmacy trading revenue has risen from under 1% to 31% of consolidated revenue in three years
   while EBITDA margin has fallen from 45% to 36%. What is the medium-term target mix and margin

   a bounded target mix with a defined margin commitment. **Worries:** no ceiling or margin
   commitment, framed purely as incremental revenue.
2. Trade receivable days rose from 7 to 170 (FY23-FY25) on the flexible credit scheme; management
   says it has become "selective" from FY25, yet FY26 receivables still grew faster than revenue

   target with evidence of deceleration. **Worries:** vague language, continued outpacing.

   planned, what is the concrete hiring pipeline, and what happens to volume if embryologist
   capacity is not scaled in time? **Reassures:** a hiring plan tied to the capex schedule.
   **Worries:** no concrete plan against an aggressive rollout.
4. RHP patient-country data shows international patients at roughly 1-7% of total patients by
   count (FY23-H1FY26), and the Annual Report shows NIL forex earnings for two straight years —
   how does this reconcile with medical-tourism positioning in investor materials, and how exactly
   are international patients billed? **Reassures:** a transparent reconciliation. **Worries:** no
   clear answer, continued reliance on the tourism narrative without disclosure support.
5. The ₹250 lakh GAAT-related payment to promoter Dr Manika Khanna was first booked as "Advance to
   Supplier" (FY24) and reclassified as development cost (FY25). What internal-control changes
   prevent recurrence, and is GAAT intended to be commercialised/licensed or does it remain an
   internal promoter-linked tool? **Reassures:** clear remediation and an IP roadmap. **Worries:**
   no control changes, ambiguous ownership/commercial path.
6. Contingent tax liabilities of ₹4,499.34 lakh (survey alleging suppressed income via "parallel
   fee ledgers") sit against roughly 76% of pre-IPO net worth. What is the realistic worst-case
   cash outflow and timeline, and is there a binding promoter indemnity? **Reassures:** a bounded
   worst case and a binding indemnity. **Worries:** open-ended exposure, informal assurance only.
7. Reported EBITDA margin (40.48%, FY25) is the highest among the disclosed IVF peer set despite
   Gaudium being the smallest by scale (3,476 cycles vs Indira's 42,622). What specifically drives
   this — mix, accounting policy, cost allocation — and is it sustainable as 19 new centres ramp up
   (which typically dilute margins during the build-out phase)? **Reassures:** a specific,
   persistent structural driver. **Worries:** margin gap attributable to small base/one-offs,
   likely to compress on scale-up.
