## ARCHETYPE RESOLUTION (carried forward from Stage 3 — load-bearing for this run)

**Question:** Is Balu Forge a build-to-spec component maker or a commodity converter (spread-driven, Section 1B v3.7 Amendment 17 binds converters)?

**VERDICT: BUILD-TO-SPEC COMPONENT MAKER.** Not a commodity converter. This is a firm, evidence-based classification, not a close call.

### The evidence, weighed

**1. What is actually sold — parts to a specification, not a shape at a spread.**
The product portfolio is named by end-application and OEM part type, not by generic metallurgical form: crankshafts, undercarriage components, brake components (hub, brake flange, disc, caliper), transmission and clutch parts (drive shafts, yokes), chassis components (front axle beams, steering knuckles), turbine blades, railway wheels and axles (specified "up to 800 kg to 1350 dia" wheels, "up to 900 kg to 2.5 metre" axles), defence ammunition shells (six calibre-specific families: mortar, naval, RCL, aerial, tank, artillery), and aerospace components (aero structure, APU, landing gear, engine components, each with a stated weight range, a stated material — titanium + aluminium alloy — and a stated scope of supply, "forged + semi machined") (Concall PPT Jun 2026, p.19-22). A commodity converter sells a standard shape (billet, bar, plate) into a market price; BFIL sells a named, weight-and-material-specified part into an OEM drawing.

**2. Steel grade is dictated by the customer, not chosen by the company.**
"Balu Forge Industries Limited largely relies on certified primary alloy steels as per OEM specifications, and hence does not have significant recycled input material at present" (Annual Report FY2025, p.79). The input specification itself is a customer design parameter. In a converter model the company chooses its own input mix to optimise the spread; here the OEM's drawing constrains the metallurgy.

**3. The customer relationship is a design-in with a multi-year qualification tail, not a spot transaction.**
"Customer lifecycle and long product journey: 15 to 20 years" against a "Research & Application" cycle of 2-3 months and a "Product Development" cycle of 3-5 months (Concall PPT Jun 2026, p.24). The FY2024 Annual Report states the company is "servicing 25+ global OEMs" and has "approval to supply over 180 products" in defence alone, with "product lifecycles spanning beyond 10 years" (Annual Report FY2024, p.27). This is a design-win economics pattern: qualification cost is front-loaded, revenue is a long tail once the part is approved. A converter has no equivalent qualification step — any buyer at the prevailing spread can transact.

**4. Contracting model is disclosed as a pricing-mechanism negotiation, not a spread formula.**
The commodity price risk note states: "The Company is primarily exposed to fluctuations in the prices of steel and alloy steels, which are the key raw materials for manufacturing crankshafts. Most contracts with Indian customers are based on mutually agreed price mechanisms, which partially offset the impact of raw material price volatility. However, in the case of firm price orders, any sharp movement in commodity prices may impact profitability... The Company does not enter into commodity derivative contracts and relies on operational measures to mitigate such risks" (Annual Report FY2025, p.143). This is an input-cost pass-through arrangement negotiated per contract — exactly the mechanism the build-to-spec archetype names — not an output-price-minus-input-price spread that a converter locks in through its own pricing power over a commodity market. The note is explicit that the pass-through is partial and customer-contract-specific, and that firm-price orders carry open commodity risk; that risk exists precisely because the company is a price-taker on a per-unit component price, not because it is running a converter spread book.

**5. Quality certification regime matches component-maker qualification, not commodity-grade certification.**
IATF 16949 (automotive), ISO 9001, and AS9100 (aerospace) certifications are named (Annual Report FY2024, p.41; Annual Report FY2025 references ISO/TS16949 and IATF 16949 throughout the quality section). These are part-approval and process-approval regimes tied to OEM qualification, not commodity-grade certifications (e.g., LME warrant grades) that a converter would hold.

**6. Raw-material intensity is falling as the value-added (fully machined) share rises, which a pure converter's cost structure would not show.**
Consolidated cost of materials consumed fell from 76.4% of revenue from operations in FY24 (Rs 42,780.75 lakh / Rs 55,985.58 lakh) to 65.8% in FY25 (Rs 60,774.92 lakh / Rs 92,361.74 lakh) (Annual Report FY2025, p.161, p.184-185), while EBITDA margin rose from 21.3% to 27.2% over the same period (Concall PPT Jun 2026, p.27). Management explicitly attributes this to "prioritization the production of the fully machined, value-added components, reflecting its core expertise in precision machining" (Concall PPT Jun 2026, p.11-12). A converter's margin lives and dies with the spread on a largely fixed-form output; BFIL's margin is explicitly moving with mix-shift toward higher machining content — an operating lever a converter does not have.

**7. What a genuine converter footprint would look like here, and why it is absent.**
A converter slice would show: (a) a standard-shape output (billets, ingots, rolled products) priced off a public index or a formula tied to LME/domestic steel benchmarks; (b) commodity derivative hedging or at least an explicit spread-lock policy; (c) minimal customer-specific qualification lead time; (d) flat or volatile gross margin tracking the input-output spread rather than trending with product mix. None of these four markers is present in the disclosed evidence. What is present — OEM part numbers, weight/material-specified drawings, 15-20 year customer lifecycles, IATF/AS9100 qualification, and a margin trend that tracks machining mix rather than the steel-price cycle — is the build-to-spec pattern.

### What would overturn this verdict
Three specific disclosures, if they appeared, would force a re-classification or a hybrid finding:
- **Export contract pricing mechanism, disclosed separately from the domestic note.** The AR's commodity-risk note explicitly discusses only "contracts with Indian customers" (Annual Report FY2025, p.143); exports are 83.4% of consolidated FY25 revenue (Rs 76,995.52 lakh / Rs 92,361.74 lakh, Annual Report FY2025, p.188) and their pricing mechanism is **NOT FOUND** in the documents provided. If export contracts turn out to be priced off a steel-price formula (spread-style) rather than a firm per-part price with partial pass-through, the converter classification would gain real weight for the export book specifically, even if the domestic book stays build-to-spec.
- **A published bill of materials or per-tonne realisation that moves in lockstep with the steel price index** rather than with defence/aerospace/railway mix shift, would undercut the mix-shift explanation for the FY24-FY25 margin move.
- **Evidence that OEM drawings are generic/interchangeable across customers** (i.e., the company holds inventory of a standard shape rather than customer-specific tooling) would weaken the design-in evidence above.

**Practical instruction for downstream stages:** treat BALUFORGE as a build-to-spec component maker archetype for Section 1B destination-PE neighbourhood purposes (R2-R3 territory per the Quality Ladder, see Section 5 card) and do NOT apply the Section 1B v3.7 Amendment 17 converter multiple cap. Spot-year ROCE and rupee-denominated working-capital trends may be fed to Section 1B and FTTCP for this name — the Amendment 17 exclusion (CLAUDE.md NEVER list) does not bind here because the CONVERTER classification is rejected on the evidence above. The unresolved export-pricing-mechanism gap above should still be named as an open item in the Halt 1 dossier.

### 1A. One-line description
Balu Forge makes precision-forged and machined metal parts — crankshafts, railway wheels, defence shells, aerospace structures — to an OEM's exact drawing, mostly for export, then charges a per-part price that is not fully protected against the price of the steel that goes into it.

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version
Think of a tailor who only stitches suits to each customer's exact measurements, not off-the-rack shirts sold by the metre of cloth. Balu Forge takes a block of special steel, hammers and shapes it in giant presses, then machines it down to a customer's exact drawing — a crankshaft for one car maker, an artillery shell for the army, a landing-gear part for an aircraft maker. Getting approved to supply a part can take years, but once approved, the same part order can keep coming for 15-20 years. The tailor still has to buy the cloth (steel) at whatever price the market charges that month, and not every customer agrees in advance to share that cost if it rises — so a chunk of profit still depends on the steel market, even though the real skill (and the real moat) is in the tailoring, not the cloth.

### 3D. Unit economics — the physics of the business

- **Define one unit:** one finished, machined forged component (e.g., one crankshaft, one artillery shell, one railway wheel) — heterogeneous by weight and material across product lines, so a single blended "per unit" figure cannot be constructed from the disclosed data; tonnage (MTPA) is the closest common unit disclosed.
- **Revenue per unit:** NOT FOUND at the per-part level; only aggregate revenue (Rs 92,361.74 lakh consolidated FY25) and installed capacity in MTPA are disclosed. A revenue-per-tonne figure could be approximated only if shipped tonnage were disclosed, which it is not.
- **Cost per unit:** NOT FOUND at the per-part level; only aggregate cost of materials consumed (Rs 60,774.92 lakh consolidated FY25) is disclosed.
- **Volume drivers:** forging capacity utilisation, machining capacity utilisation, new OEM part approvals, order-book conversion pace.
- **Price drivers:** steel/alloy steel input price (partially passed through on Indian contracts, export mechanism NOT FOUND), mix shift toward higher-value defence/aerospace parts, export incentive income (RoDTEP/duty-drawback-type, Rs 1,719.97 lakh FY25).
- **Cost drivers:** raw material price (steel/alloy steel), energy cost (heaters, cooling units, compressors cited as sustainability capex, AR FY2025, p.78), labour (small relative to materials — Rs 2,352.37 lakh consolidated FY25, ~2.5% of revenue), discounting/LC charges on receivables (Rs 376.69-377.83 lakh, standalone/consolidated FY25, AR FY2025, p.147/p.186).
- **Incremental margin / operating leverage:** the FY24→FY25 EBITDA margin expansion from 21.3% to 27.2% while revenue nearly doubled (Rs 559.86 cr → Rs 923.62 cr consolidated) suggests meaningful operating leverage as fixed forging/machining capacity absorbs more volume, reinforced by the mix shift toward higher-margin defence/aerospace/railway output. This is the central physics of the transition thesis: each incremental tonne of defence/aerospace-mix output should carry materially higher margin than the legacy auto mix, but the audited AR does not disclose segment-level margins to confirm the magnitude, only the directional EBITDA-margin trend.
