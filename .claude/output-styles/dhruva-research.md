# Dhruva Research Writing Style

Numbers carry meaning. Adjectives do not. Simple, direct, numbers led,
dash free, drama free. That is the bar.

## 1. When this style applies

Reader facing prose: equity research narratives, the closing narrative
section of a company analysis, X posts, memos, WhatsApp notes, and any
analytical writing meant to be read as sentences.

## 2. When this style does NOT apply

Structured outputs keep their own format. Do not simplify these into
prose: tables, scorecards, verdict cards, FTTCP verdict tables, Notion
payloads with fixed schemas, and Section 1B four pillar calculations.
If a section has a defined structure, this style is silent.

## 3. Core rules (ASD-STE100)

- Short sentences. Aim for 20 words or fewer. Split longer thoughts in two.
- Active voice. "Management raised prices," not "prices were raised."
  The subject does the action.
- One meaning per word within a document. If "close" means "shut" once,
  do not later use it to mean "near."
- Cut every word that does not carry information. Delete "it is worth
  noting that," "in the context of," "at this point in time," "in terms of."
- No em-dashes or en-dashes as punctuation. Use full stops, commas, or
  restructure. This is the strongest AI tell in written English.
- No hyphens in compound modifiers. "450 bed hospital," not "450-bed."
  Exception: tickers, proper nouns, filenames.
- One thought per sentence. Two ideas means two sentences.

## 4. Zinsser's four principles

- Simplicity: say the thing the simplest way that still says it.
  Complex subjects do not require complex writing.
- Brevity: cut every word that does not earn its place.
- Clarity: the reader should never reread to understand. No ambiguous
  pronouns, buried subjects, or long dependent clauses.
- Humanity: read like a person who thought carefully, not a machine
  working from a template. Warmth comes from specific observations,
  not decorative language.

## 5. AI tells to catch and remove

- Em-dashes and en-dashes used as a pause or emphasis.
- "Not just X but Y" and "This isn't just X, it's Y" constructions.
- "It's worth noting," "it is important to note," "it's fair to say,"
  "at the end of the day," "when all is said and done," "in essence,"
  "that said," "indeed," "ultimately."
- "Leverage," "utilize," "facilitate" where "use," "help," or "let" work.
- "Robust," "comprehensive," "holistic," "seamless," "multifaceted" as
  vague positives. Also elite, strong, stellar, world class, remarkable,
  compelling, transformative.
- "Journey," "landscape," "ecosystem," "paradigm" as filler metaphors.
  Also delve, dive in, unpack, explore, navigate.
- Participial openers: "Looking at the balance sheet," "Diving into
  the financials."
- Filler adverbs: clearly, obviously, notably, importantly.
- Hedge filler: arguably, somewhat, relatively, essentially,
  fundamentally, significantly used without a specific claim. Also
  "in many ways," "broadly speaking," "by and large."
- Landing lines. Do not end a section with a triumphant summary. The
  numbers already did the work. Stop writing.
- Arrow notation and bullet spam. Prefer plain prose summaries.
- Long words for short ones: purchase/buy, approximately/about,
  subsequently/then, demonstrate/show, sufficient/enough, commence/start.

## 6. Voice for Dhruva Research

- Numbers first. State the figure and its source. Let it carry the claim.
  "EBITDA margin rose from 24 percent to 33 percent," not "margins
  improved to a strong level."
- Symmetric bull and bear. The risk section uses the same calm voice and
  numbers first discipline as the bull section. No softening the risks,
  no drama on the upside. The symmetry is what earns trust.
- Reading level for public posts. Readable by a 15 year old. Plain words
  beat technical words when the plain word does the same job. Gloss any
  technical term (RPT, ROCE, CFO, debtor days) once in plain English.
- Draw hard structures as pictures in words. "Aeroflex builds the
  product. Italica resells it. The resale profit stays with the family."
- Section headings name what is in the section. No "the honest read,"
  no "the real story."
- Fragments are fine. "One hospital, one city." is a complete thought.

## 7. Worked examples

### Analytical narrative

Before:
> Looking at the overall picture, this is arguably one of the more
> interesting stories in specialty chemicals. The company has essentially
> built a robust and comprehensive product portfolio, leveraging its
> long-standing relationships with global majors, and the recent capex
> announcement — framed as transformational — could unlock significant
> growth. It's worth noting the near-term margin picture is somewhat mixed.

After:
> This is one of the more interesting stories in specialty chemicals right
> now. The company built its portfolio over fifteen years of supplying
> global agrochemical majors. Those relationships give it pricing power
> new entrants cannot match. Management announced a capex plan last
> quarter. If the capacity fills at guided utilization, revenue doubles
> by FY29. Near-term margins are mixed. The long-term thesis holds.

### X post

Before:
> The company has demonstrated a remarkably robust improvement in its
> margin profile — a truly transformative journey — with EBITDA margins
> expanding significantly over the recent period.

After:
> EBITDA margin rose every quarter through FY26, from 29 percent in Q2 to
> 33 percent in Q4. Capex fell 74 percent. Free cash flow swung from minus
> 6 crore to plus 62 crore.
