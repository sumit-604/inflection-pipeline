## PART 2: UNPROMPTED CROSS-READ

### 2A. Demand environment
All three peers report strong, broad-based demand across the high-voltage/grid ecosystem
in the same period Yash reports its own growth: QPOWER's consolidated revenue grew
112% YoY (Q2FY26), then crossed Rs1,000cr for FY26 (+157% YoY), with order book
rising from Rs830cr (Nov-2025) to Rs1,400cr (May-2026) to Rs1,945cr (Aug-2026).
POWERINDIA posted its highest-ever order backlog (Rs29,412.6cr, Nov-2025 call, p.5-6)
and 23%+ revenue growth. VILAS grew volumes 41-64% YoY across its calls and reports
no transformer-industry slowdown ("I do not see any slowdown in transformer
industries... at present, lots of people are searching the new vendors because the existing
transformer manufacturers are not able to give them the transformers," Nov-2025 call,
p.7). This is broad, independent confirmation that the demand environment Yash describes
is real and industry-wide, not a story specific to Yash's own telling — though it is a
confirmation at the level of the broader HV/transformer ecosystem, not bushings narrowly.

### 2B. Pricing and input costs
All three peers describe raw-material volatility (copper, aluminium at QPOWER; CRGO
steel at VILAS) passed through with a lag rather than absorbed, consistent with Yash's own
margin narrative of price recovery with a delay. VILAS supplies the one genuinely
different data point: CRGO steel prices, which had been in shortage and rising, REVERSED
and corrected 15-20% once Chinese mills obtained BIS approval and entered the market
(May-2026 call, p.5-6) — a live example of an import-substitution/shortage dynamic
flipping direction inside the same broad industry Yash operates in. This is a useful caution
against assuming Yash's own import-substitution-driven margin story is a one-way,
permanent tailwind.

### 2C. Capex cycle
This is an industry-wide capacity race, not a lone expander. QPOWER is building/expanding
Sangli, a new HVDC magnet-wire facility, Mehru capacity, an Endoks PCS facility in Turkey,
and just acquired Winwin Speciality Insulators (Rs50-60cr) with a further Rs50cr planned
capex there. POWERINDIA's board approved an additional Rs2,000cr investment including a
"greenfield large power transformer facility" (Jun-2026 call, p.5-6) on top of an already-stated
Rs750cr/year capex programme. VILAS tripled its CRGO lamination capacity (12,000 to
36,000 MTPA) via Unit 3, added nanocrystalline core and radiator lines, is entering copper
conductors, and has now started a bushing venture. Every peer in the set is expanding
simultaneously — this reads as a genuine capacity race across the whole transformer/HV
component value chain, which both supports Yash's "multi-year opportunity" framing and
raises the standard base-rate risk that an industry-wide capacity race eventually pressures
margins for the slower or smaller expanders (see Part 5 hypothesis below).

### 2D. Competitive mentions
The single highest-value finding in this stage. VILAS's May-2026 call (H2/FY26 results)
names Yash Highvoltage directly, twice, in ways Yash's own concalls never surface:
1. An analyst benchmarking question: "how do we will be like, benchmarking ourselves
   against the incumbents like Hitachi Energy and even like Yash Highvoltage?" (p.14) —
   a neutral confirmation that the market already regards Yash as a bushing incumbent
   worth benchmarking against.
2. Far more significant: VILAS discloses that its new high-voltage bushing venture (OIP
   now, RIP later, 12kV-400kV range) is being built with "Mr. Ramachandran who has found
   [founded] the Yash Highvoltage, that founder promoter is joining with us, we have made
   an agreement with them and he is on Board and developing all this product" (p.14), and
   later, in more detail: "Being an entrepreneur, he has set up the two plants, one is for the
   earlier he was in Crompton, then he has set up the Yash Highvoltage and now we have
   joined hands with him to set up the new plant... we have appointed Mr. Ramachandran as
   [the] CEO and it is his duty to develop and product development, marketing and
   everything" (p.15).

This is a person the transcript identifies as Yash Highvoltage's founder-promoter, now
running a new competing bushing venture, part-owned by a Yash peer, explicitly targeting
OIP now and RIP later, across the SAME 12kV-400kV range Yash itself operates in and is
extending toward (Yash's own extension target is 550kV). This was never named or
addressed on any of Yash's own three concalls read in Stage 5, despite the VILAS
announcement (12-May-2026) landing two days before Yash's own Call-3 (14-May-2026).
This is flagged below as a MEDIUM-HIGH severity red flag: an insider-knowledge
competitor, backed by a listed peer's balance sheet, entering Yash's own product range,
and Yash management has had at least one subsequent public forum (no later Yash call
exists in this corpus) in which to address it and has not.

Separately, and more routinely, QPOWER names Yash Highvoltage in its own Q3FY26 call
(Feb-2026, p.3) simply to describe the Sukrut Electric joint-venture structure accurately
("50% stake in Sukrut Electric Company Private Limited converting into a joint venture of
the company along with Yash Highvoltage Limited") — a neutral, confirming mention with
no adverse content.

### 2E. Risks peers discuss that Yash does not
- **Liquidated-damages (LD) exposure on late delivery.** QPOWER repeatedly frames LD
  penalties as a live, costed risk in the same high-voltage/utility-customer ecosystem Yash
  sells into: "when you start opening up the floodgates of orders, you end up in LDs, like
  what my colleagues in Mehru did in the recent past. They lost more than INR 2.5 crores in
  one single order in LDs" (Nov-2025 call, p.10). Yash's own three concalls never discuss LD
  exposure on its own order book, despite discussing order-book growth and execution
  visibility repeatedly.
- **Customer affordability ceiling limiting pricing power even amid shortage.**
  POWERINDIA names this explicitly: "in the domestic market, despite the supply and
  demand, there's always an affordability challenge... it's not that whatever price you want,
  you can get it" (Nov-2025 call, p.19). Yash's own narrative treats its supply-constrained
  position as close to unqualified pricing power; no Yash call names a demand-side ceiling.
- **Import-substitution/BIS-renewal policy risk cutting both ways.** VILAS names a
  specific, dated regulatory dependency: Chinese CRGO mills' BIS approvals expiring on a
  rolling basis, with price direction (up or down) hinging on whether each approval is
  renewed (May-2026 call, p.6-7). Yash's own RIP/RIS import-substitution narrative never
  discusses an equivalent renewal/policy dependency for its own Swiss core supply or for
  future domestic entrants (including, now, VILAS's own bushing venture).
- **A new, insider-founded domestic competitor entering the same product range** (see
  2D above) is, by construction, a risk Yash's own concalls cannot yet have addressed on
  the corpus available, but its absence from Yash's most recent public forum despite the
  timing overlap is itself notable.

**The single most consequential finding, not a "contradiction" in the strict sense but the
highest-value item this stage produced:** VILAS Transcore's May-2026 disclosure that Yash
Highvoltage's own founder-promoter is now the CEO of a new, listed-peer-backed bushing

own three concalls despite timing that made it knowable before Yash's most recent call.

**The strongest independent confirmation:** two unrelated peers (QPOWER, repeatedly

**Overall: the peer set complicates more than it either supports or undercuts Yash's
narrative.** The demand and supply-tightness story is independently real — this is not an
invented growth narrative. But the peer set also surfaces two things Yash's own calls never
raise: a live, credible new domestic competitor with founder-level insider knowledge of
Yash's own business, and at least one concrete industry example (VILAS's CRGO
experience) of a "protected," import-substitution-driven transformer-component margin
compressing fast once a policy or capacity condition changed. Both cut against treating
Yash's supply-constrained position as durable without qualification, while leaving the core
growth-and-shortage thesis itself independently better evidenced than before this stage.

## PART 5: CROSS-PEER HYPOTHESIS (mandatory closing step)

Across all three peers, a single pattern recurs that none of them states outright: every
peer in this set is simultaneously (a) reporting a supply-constrained, seller's-market
environment for its core high-voltage product, AND (b) using that same environment as
the stated justification for entering an ADJACENT, only loosely related product category
via new capex or a new entity — QPOWER into GIS/BESS/PCS and now insulators
(beyond its core reactor/instrument-transformer business), VILAS into copper conductors
and now bushings (beyond its core CRGO lamination business), POWERINDIA into a
greenfield large power transformer facility and BESS localisation (beyond its core
grid-equipment business.

The testable hypothesis: the entire small/mid-cap Indian high-voltage-component
ecosystem is using a genuine, real core-product shortage as balance-sheet and
credibility cover to fund diversification into adjacent, unproven product lines, faster than
the shortage itself would justify on its own economics. If this is right, the observable
signature over the next 4-6 quarters should be: (1) capex intensity across this peer set
rising faster than core-segment order-book growth, and (2) new-segment margins
(Mehru's GIS, VILAS's bushing venture, POWERINDIA's BESS) print materially below
each company's own core-segment margin for several quarters before any of them
narrows the gap — exactly the pattern QPOWER's own numbers already show for Mehru
(15-18% vs. 25%+ core) and that VILAS itself flags for its new bushing venture ("we are a
baby in it, we cannot benchmark anywhere"). Yash Highvoltage's own greenfield RIP/RIS
plant, capex-escalated ~70% against original guidance (per Stage 5 findings) while its
core Savli business remains the near-term revenue engine, fits the same signature. If this
pattern is real, it reframes the sector-wide capacity race in Part 2C: it is not simply
capacity racing to meet demand, but diversification racing ahead of proof, funded by a
shortage story that is itself real but is being stretched further than its own economics
currently support.
