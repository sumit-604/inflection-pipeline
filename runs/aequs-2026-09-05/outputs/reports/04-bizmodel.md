## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description

Aequs takes a customer's approved drawing for an aircraft part or a
consumer product, machines, forges, molds and finishes it inside its own
vertically integrated Indian manufacturing "ecosystem," and ships the
certified, ready-to-fit unit back to a global aerospace OEM or a global
consumer brand under a long-term supply agreement (AR p.5, p.17-21).

### 1B. Money flow chain, by revenue stream

**Aerospace** (engine systems, structures, actuation systems, turnings,
landing systems, interiors and cargo, assemblies — 5,654 aerospace SKUs at
FY26 year end, AR p.15, p.19, p.43):
[Customer-owned drawing + qualification approval] → [Aequs forges/machines/
surface-treats/assembles the part inside the Belagavi SEZ, plus France and
US sites] → [delivers a certified, drawing-conformant part or assembly] →
[Airbus, Boeing, Safran, Collins Aerospace, Honeywell, SAAB, GKN Aerospace,
Eaton, Bombardier — OEMs and Tier-1 integrators, AR p.121, p.18] → [paid per
unit shipped under a multi-year program contract, trade receivables 0-90
days, AR p.294 Note 36].

**Consumer** (toys, cookware/consumer durables, consumer electronics
enclosures, AR p.20):
[Brand-owner design/spec] → [Aequs molds/stamps/machines/assembles the
product at Koppal (toys) and Hubballi (durables, electronics)] → [delivers
finished or semi-finished consumer goods] → [Hasbro, Mattel, Spin Master,
Tramontina, Wonderchef, Reliance Retail and "one of the largest global
consumer electronics players" (unnamed in filings), AR p.121, p.20] → [paid
per unit shipped, same 0-90 day credit terms basis disclosed at the
consolidated level, AR p.294].

| Element | Aerospace | Consumer |
|---|---|---|

**The capital-employed asymmetry that matters most:** at FY26 year end,
Consumer (15% of revenue) held Rs 1,286.9 cr of segment assets versus
Aerospace's (85% of revenue) Rs 1,378.3 cr — almost the same asset base for
roughly one-sixth the revenue and a -42.5% EBITDA margin (AR p.293 Note 36).
Implied segment asset turnover: Aerospace 0.76x, Consumer 0.14x (derived
from AR p.292-293 revenue and segment-asset figures). This is the single
number that most concretely captures the "capex into a loss-making
vertical" risk named in the load-bearing facts.

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version

Imagine a tea-stall that also fixes watches. The tea side is the main
business — steady customers, good margins, people keep coming back because
your tea is reliable and hard to copy exactly (AR calls this a "15-year
average relationship" with top customers). The watch-repair side is new —
you spent a lot of money on tools and a workbench, but not enough customers
have come yet, so that corner of the shop is losing money every day. You
are betting that once more watch customers show up, the tools you already
bought will suddenly make that corner profitable too, without buying much
more. That is Aequs: a profitable aerospace tea-stall (26.9% margin, AR
p.292) funding a loss-making consumer watch-repair corner (-42.5% margin,
same page) that management says will break even by Q4 FY27 (AR p.9-10;
Inv. Pres. slide 6).

## SECTION 2: INDUSTRY DYNAMICS & COMPETITIVE POSITION

### 2A. Five forces

| Force | Reading | Helps/Hurts/Neutral |

| Competition count | Global Tier-1/2 aerospace machining/forging suppliers plus named India peers Azad Engineering, Dynamatic Technologies, Unimech Aerospace (Step 1 brief, weighed not anchored); AR itself lists Dynamatic Technologies and Triumph Group among Aequs' own **customers** (AR p.18), meaning Aequs also sits as a Tier-2 supplier to some entities that are also viewed as India peers elsewhere | Neutral — moderate named competition, but the supply-chain layering is more complex than a simple peer list |
| Entry barriers | Multi-year qualification cycles, NADCAP/AS9100/Airbus-Boeing facility approvals, SEZ policy access, capex-heavy tooling (10,000-ton press) (AR p.17-18, p.43) | Helps |
| Supplier power | Import dependence on aluminium/titanium/steel/nickel alloys is a named threat (AR p.40); critical surface-treatment and forging capability sits inside JVs (Magellan Aerospace, Aubert & Duval) that Aequs does not wholly own (AR p.292-296) | Hurts |
| Customer power and concentration | Top 4 anonymised customers = 58% of FY26 revenue, all Aerospace (AR p.294 Note 36); top-5 customer groups = 63% of Q1 FY27 revenue (Inv. Pres. slide 26); 15 total OEM customer groups globally (Inv. Pres. slide 26) | Hurts |
| Substitutes | Low once qualified into a specific Aerospace program (re-qualification is slow and costly); high for Consumer (toys/cookware/EMS are more easily re-sourced to other contract manufacturers) | Mixed — helps Aerospace, hurts Consumer |

### 2B. Competitive positioning

Named peers (Step 1 brief selection, weighed as company memory, not
anchored to the AR): Azad Engineering (AZAD), Dynamatic Technologies
(DYNAMATECH), Unimech Aerospace (UNIMECH) — all precision aero-component
makers for global OEMs. The AR itself does not disclose market share or
name direct competitors; it frames Aequs as "the only precision component
manufacturer in India operating from a single special economic zone with
fully vertically integrated manufacturing capabilities" in Aerospace (AR
p.42). No comparable claim of uniqueness is made for Consumer. Aequs' two
JV partners — Magellan Aerospace (surface treatment, API JV) and Aubert &
Duval (forging, SQuAD JV) — are simultaneously capability partners and,
outside the JV, global competitors in their own right (AR p.22, p.292-296);
this dual relationship is a structural nuance worth tracking, not a clean
competitor map.

### 2C. Moat assessment (eight standard moat types)

| Moat type | Present? | Evidence | Durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network effects | No | Not applicable to a contract manufacturer | N/A |
| Switching costs | Yes — high | Multi-year qualification per part, single/dual sourcing, 15-year average top-3 customer tenure, 6x Airbus D2P award 2016-2023 (Inv. Pres. slide 4, slide 26) | Durable |
| Cost advantages | Yes — moderate | India labour arbitrage plus single-SEZ co-location cuts part-travel distance from ~5,000km to <500m (AR p.17, p.21) | Moderate — replicable in time by other India entrants |
| Intangible assets/brand | No (Aerospace is an invisible B2B supplier); No (Consumer has no owned consumer brand) | Certifications function more like a regulatory moat than a brand moat | N/A as brand; see regulatory row |
| Efficient scale | Yes — moderate | Only a few players can justify a fully integrated single-SEZ aerospace ecosystem investment in India at this scale (AR p.17-18, p.42) | Moderate, narrowing as India capacity builds |
| Regulatory/certification barrier | Yes — moderate-high | NADCAP, AS9100, first Airbus/Boeing-approved India surface-treatment facility (AR p.18, p.43) | Durable, but held partly via JV, not wholly owned |
| Data/technology moat | No | Not disclosed as a differentiator | N/A |
| Customer captivity (Consumer) | No/low | Toy/cookware/EMS-style contracts, more fungible than Aerospace | Not durable |

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage

Global aerospace: mid-cycle growth, $340.04bn (CY2025) to $356.93bn
(CY2026), 5% CAGR, projected $445.64bn by CY2030 at 5.70% CAGR (AR p.38).
India aerospace precision manufacturing: early-growth/emerging, riding
Make in India and OEM supply-chain diversification (AR p.36, p.40).
Aequs sits as an emerging-scale participant transitioning from a
qualification-stage entrant toward a trusted Tier-1/2 position (order book
crossing USD 1bn, Inv. Pres. slide 7). Consumer end-markets (global toys
6.62% CAGR to CY2035; global cookware 7.40% CAGR to CY2034; global EMS
7.10% CAGR to CY2034, AR p.41) are themselves mature, moderate-growth
industries; Aequs itself is at an early pilot-to-commercial-scale phase
within them (Consumer Electronics "progressed from pilot production to
commercial scale" only in FY26, AR p.12).

## SECTION 3: FINANCIAL METRICS THAT MATTER FOR THIS BUSINESS MODEL

### 3A. Ignore-these-track-these

| Commonly tracked ratio | Why misleading/irrelevant here |
|---|---|
| Consolidated ROCE / ROE / net profit margin | Blends a profitable ~27% EBITDA-margin, ~21.7% segment-ROCE Aerospace engine with a -42.5% EBITDA-margin, -24.8% segment-ROCE Consumer build-out (AR p.292; Inv. Pres. slide 12). Consolidated ROCE was just 1.56% FY26 / 0.92% FY25 (AR p.46 Key Financial Ratios) — this understates Aerospace's real earning power and overstates near-term whole-company risk. Track segment ROCE/EBITDA margin instead. |
| Trailing consolidated P/E | Company is net-loss-making at the consolidated level every year disclosed (PAT -Rs 113.3 cr FY26, -Rs 102.4 cr FY25, AR p.46). No meaningful denominator. |
| Headline consolidated revenue growth alone | Consumer's +84% FY26 growth (AR p.292) is off a small base and inflates the growth optic; the value-driving growth is Aerospace's +27% at expanding margin. Track segment growth and margin together. |
| Pre-IPO leverage trend | Debt-Equity fell from 0.99x (FY25) to 0.23x (FY26) purely because Dec-2025 IPO proceeds reset the equity base (AR p.46). The historical trend is not informative; track the post-IPO base and its trajectory against the ~USD 350-450mn FY27-31 capex plan (AR p.9-10). |
| Consolidated inventory/receivables turnover | Blends a long qualification-cycle Aerospace working-capital rhythm with Consumer's shorter, more seasonal cycle. Segment-level working-capital days are NOT FOUND (not disclosed by segment anywhere in the AR or the presentation) — track the consolidated cash-flow statement and segment revenue/EBITDA instead (AR p.231-232). |

## SECTION 4: RISKS, VALUATION APPROACH & MONITORING

### 4A. Business-model-specific risks

| Category | Risk | First financial line item to deteriorate |
|---|---|---|

### 4B. Valuation method applicability

| Method | Applicability | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sum-of-the-parts (segment EV/EBITDA for Aerospace + asset/replacement-cost anchor for pre-breakeven Consumer) | **PRIMARY** | Aerospace (85% of revenue, 26.9% EBITDA margin, ~21.7% segment ROCE) and Consumer (15% of revenue, -42.5% EBITDA margin) sit at genuinely different profitability and life-cycle stages (AR p.292; Inv. | DCF to FY31 Vision-2031 targets | **SECONDARY** | Long qualification cycles and the multi-year capex plan (~USD 350-450mn FY27-31, AR p.9) make forward cash flow, not trailing earnings, the right lens; management has published its own FY31 revenue (4-6x)/margin (18-22%)/ROCE (~20%) targets to anchor assumptions (AR p.10; Inv. Pres. slide 6) |
| Order-book-to-EV cross-check (Aerospace) | **TERTIARY** | USD 1,004mn order book (Inv. Pres. slide 7) gives an independent revenue-visibility sanity check on the Aerospace segment distinct from any multiple choice |
| Consolidated EV/EBITDA, trailing P/E, dividend discount, PEG, consolidated P/B | **NOT APPLICABLE** | Consolidated EV/EBITDA blends the two segments the same way SOTP is built to avoid; trailing P/E fails on negative consolidated PAT (AR p.46); no dividend has been paid or policy set for one (AR p.113 area, dividend policy referenced but no distribution made); PEG fails on a negative/volatile near-term earnings denominator; consolidated P/B is distorted by the fresh Dec-2025 IPO equity infusion not yet earnings-tested (AR p.8, p.46) |

Cycle stage that matters for valuation: Aerospace sits early-to-mid in a
multi-year global aircraft-build-rate up-cycle plus an India in-sourcing
wave (AR p.36-38); Consumer sits at the trough of its own capex-ramp
J-curve, with capacity commissioned and utilisation/fixed-cost absorption
still climbing toward the Q4 FY27 breakeven milestone (AR p.9-10, p.12).
Valuation must separate these two cycle positions rather than apply one
multiple to the blended entity.

### 4C. Quarterly monitoring checklist

1. Aerospace order book (USD mn) and QoQ change — good: steady increase; trouble: decline or flat 2+ quarters (Inv. Pres. slide 7).
2. Aerospace segment revenue growth YoY — good: within/above the historical +27% pace; trouble: sustained deceleration below ~15% (AR p.292; Inv. Pres. slide 11).
3. Aerospace segment EBITDA margin — good: holds at/above the 26.9% FY26 level; trouble: compresses back toward FY25's 19.4% (AR p.292).
4. Consumer segment revenue growth and mix % of total — good: rising, utilisation-led; trouble: growth without utilisation gain (Inv. Pres. slide 7, slide 10).
5. Consumer segment EBITDA margin trend toward Q4 FY27 breakeven — good: loss narrowing every quarter; trouble: loss flat or widening (AR p.9-10; Inv. Pres. slide 11).
6. Consolidated PAT and progress to the H1 FY28 breakeven milestone — good: narrowing on schedule; trouble: milestone slipping (AR p.9-10; Inv. Pres. slide 6, slide 8).
7. Operating cash flow vs capex — good: gap narrowing; trouble: CFO stays deeply negative while capex continues unabated (AR p.231-232; Inv. Pres. slide 15).
8. Debt-Equity / Net Debt-Equity — good: stays near FY26's post-IPO 0.23x; trouble: re-leveraging materially (AR p.46; Inv. Pres. slide 12).
9. Capacity utilisation %, Aerospace and Consumer separately — good: rising toward guided levels; trouble: stalled utilisation (AR p.7; Inv. Pres. slide 7).
10. Fixed Asset Turnover — good: stabilising/recovering; trouble: continued decline (Inv. Pres. slide 12).
11. Customer concentration (top 4/5) — good: stable or diversifying; trouble: rising further (AR p.294; Inv. Pres. slide 26).
12. Segment ROCE, Aerospace vs Consumer — good: Aerospace holds ~20%+, Consumer inflects upward; trouble: Aerospace compresses or Consumer stays deeply negative (Inv. Pres. slide 12).
13. New program wins (Safran and other Tier-1 agreements) progressing to delivery — good: on-schedule industrialisation; trouble: delays/cancellations (Inv. Pres. slide 7-8; concall, not in this corpus).
14. Net Working Capital days — good: stable near 120-125 days or improving; trouble: rising materially (Inv. Pres. slide 12; footnote flags FY26 annual disclosure uses full-year revenue, not directly comparable to the annualised quarterly figure).
15. JV dependency signals (API, SQuAD JV results) — good: JV profitability stable/growing; trouble: JV losses or capacity constraints feeding through (AR Note 37, p.292-296).
