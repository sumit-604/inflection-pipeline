## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description

Syngene rents out its scientists, labs and factories to global drug companies who
would rather pay Syngene to do the research and make the medicine than build their
own capacity (AR2026 p.181, "Syngene Overview").

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version

Imagine a chai stall that does not just serve tea, it also rents out its whole kitchen
to five-star hotels that do not want to build their own kitchen in this town. Some
hotels sign a five-year lease for a dedicated corner of the kitchen (that is the
Dedicated Centres, like BMS). Other customers just walk in and order one dish at a
time, and if a rival chai stall down the road is cheaper, they walk there instead
(that is open Discovery work). Syngene also built a new, bigger kitchen to cook one
very large order for one big hotel chain, Zoetis. That hotel chain cut its order
almost to zero, and now Syngene is stuck with a half-empty new kitchen, still paying
rent and staff, while it hunts for new big-order customers.

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage

Global CRO/CDMO outsourcing: structurally growing, mid-cycle, temporarily
soft-near-term. AR2026 (p.186-188) names five concurrent forces: biotech funding
lag (6-12 months to convert into CRO revenue), the BIOSECURE Act (a structural,
multi-year tailwind for non-China CRDMOs), China+1 diversification, new-modality
growth (ADC/peptide/oligonucleotide), and AI adoption as a productivity lever that
could also compress billable hours. Syngene's own position within that cycle:
recovering from a company-specific demand shock (Zoetis) layered onto an industry-wide
soft patch, not a company at the top of its cycle.

### 3D. Unit economics: the physics of the business

Syngene runs two different unit economics inside one P&L, which the single-segment
disclosure obscures. Both are given because the model is genuinely hybrid.

**Unit 1 -- Research Services: the billed scientist-hour/FTE**

- Unit: one FTE-year of a scientist's billed time (or one fee-for-service project).
- Revenue per unit: not separately disclosed; approximated only by blended revenue
  per employee = Rs 3,739 cr / 8,373 headcount = approximately Rs 44.7 lakh per
  employee per year (AR2026 p.185 headcount; p.197 revenue; computed, not a filed
  ratio).
- Cost per unit: dominated by employee benefits (32.9% of total revenue FY26, AR2026
  p.198), which are largely fixed once a scientist is hired.
- Volume driver: number of active clients and FTE headcount deployed against them
  (~400 clients, AR2026 p.181).
- Price driver: FFS rate cards and FTE billing rates; open discovery work has been
  losing on price to competitors (Concall_Jul_2026 p.9, "commoditized service
  offerings").
- Cost driver: scientist wage inflation, attrition/rehiring cost, facility overhead.
- Incremental margin / operating leverage: high. Once a lab/dedicated centre is
  staffed, an incremental billable hour carries close to the contract margin; the
  flip side is that under-utilised staff (e.g., research services facing "attrition")
  keep drawing full-time cost with no matching revenue, which is exactly what pulled
  FY26 margin from 30.0% to 26.0% (AR2026 p.198).

**Unit 2 -- CDMO: the manufactured batch/kg or litre of bioreactor capacity**

- Unit: one manufacturing campaign/batch of drug substance or drug product (small
  molecule) or one bioreactor run (large molecule).
- Revenue per unit: NOT FOUND IN DOCUMENT (no per-batch or per-litre pricing
  disclosed).
- Volume driver: number of active manufacturing programmes and, critically, offtake
  volume from large committed customers; Zoetis alone drove "nearly $50 million" of
  the FY26/FY27 revenue swing (Concall_Jul_2026 p.9).
- Price driver: contract-negotiated manufacturing fees, largely fixed per program
  once signed.
- Cost driver: raw material/consumables (25% of revenue FY26, AR2026 p.198),
  depreciation on newly capitalised biologics capacity (Unit 3, Bayview), which rose
  4.7% YoY to Rs 4,529 mn in FY26 even as revenue barely moved (AR2026 p.198).
- Incremental margin / operating leverage: very high and symmetric. This is the
  crux of the current thesis question: fixed-cost biologics capacity built for one
  customer generates strongly negative operating leverage when that customer's
  offtake disappears (Zoetis), and would generate strongly positive operating
  leverage if new customers fill the same capacity. Management's own words capture
  this precisely: "we then were left with spare capacity and we had to fill in a lot
  of the new capacity that we had built in anticipation of diversifying" (Kiran
  Mazumdar-Shaw, Concall_Jul_2026 p.7).
