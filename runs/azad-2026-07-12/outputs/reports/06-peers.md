## PART 2: UNPROMPTED CROSS-READ

**2A. Demand environment.** Broad, consistent peer consensus of strong secular demand: MTAR across fuel cells (record order inflows, AEP $2.65bn deal), nuclear (Kaiga 5&6, refurbishment reactors, expected PLI scheme), and aerospace (AMCA participation); Unimech across nuclear tenders (~INR800cr bid pipeline), semiconductor (52-week order visibility building), and post-tariff aero-tooling recovery; PTCIL (2023, structurally) citing 42,000-aircraft/$6.3tn 20-year civil aviation demand and Aatmanirbhar Bharat defence localization. This broadly supports Azad's framing of a structural, multi-sector Indian aerospace/defence/energy upcycle, though none of it is gas-turbine-specific corroboration (see Claim 3).

**2B. Pricing and input costs.** PTCIL (2023) reports aerospace-casting realization per kg rising from ~INR850 to ~INR1,480 over six years and EBITDA/kg more than doubling — an industry-level structural margin-expansion story for precision aerospace metal components, consistent in direction with Azad's own margin trajectory, though from a different sub-segment (castings, not forged/machined airfoils) and dated. MTAR discusses fuel-cell gross margin as structurally lower than domestic/nuclear gross margin (Q3 FY26 call, PDF p.7: "gross margins in the fuel cells is lower, in case of domestic, it is higher") — a segment-mix effect, not a raw-material story. No peer directly discusses titanium/superalloy price trends in the FY26 window (see Claim 2).

**2C. Capex cycle.** This is unambiguously an **industry-wide capacity race**, not a lone-expander situation. MTAR, PTCIL, and Unimech are all deploying capital well ahead of current utilization, in some cases (PTCIL) far more aggressively than Azad's own ~15x-type build-ahead pattern implied by 8 factories against current revenue. This context somewhat normalizes Azad's capex intensity but does not validate its execution or returns — none of the peers' capex programs are evaluated here for their own success.

**2D. Competitive mentions.** No peer transcript mentions Azad Engineering by name or by unmistakable description across all 10 transcripts. This silence is worth noting: MTAR, PTCIL, and Unimech all operate in adjacent-but-distinct sub-segments (fuel cells/nuclear/space; titanium castings/materials; tooling/GSE/nuclear subsystems/bellows) rather than Azad's specific niche (large hot-section forged and machined gas-turbine/aero-engine airfoils for MHI, Siemens Energy, Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce). The absence of competitive mention is consistent with genuine niche differentiation, but cannot on its own confirm Azad's "sole qualified supplier" framing (Claim 1) — it is equally consistent with peers simply not operating in a comparable enough space to reference Azad either way.

**2E. Risks peers discuss that Azad does not (per B05).**
- **Tariff risk as an acute, quantified, multi-quarter earnings drag.** Unimech's revenue and guidance were materially cut by US tariffs for two consecutive quarters (Nov 2025, Feb 2026 calls) before resolution — treated as a serious live risk with dollar/percentage impact disclosed. Per B05, Azad's own management stance was "tariffs will not affect deliveries or margins" (delivered as promised) — a much more dismissive framing than what peers actually experienced in the same window, even though Azad's outcome was ultimately fine.
- **Customer concentration, explicitly quantified.** MTAR discloses Bloom Energy's percentage of Clean Energy segment revenue and dollar quarterly run-rate in granular detail every call. Unimech discloses that two OEM groups represent ~93% of Hobel Bellows' revenue (May 2026 call, PDF p.10). Per B05's own red flag list, Azad has never quantified its customer concentration despite ~81% of FY26 revenue from a three-OEM-dominated Energy/O&G segment — peers are markedly more transparent on this specific metric.
- **Geopolitical/regional-conflict receivables risk.** MTAR explicitly attributed a WC-days spike to delayed Israeli-customer receivables during "the ongoing conflict in that region" (Aug 2025 call, PDF p.6) — a specific, quantified geopolitical AR risk not referenced in Azad's concalls per B05.
- **Customer-driven BOM/design cost-reduction risk.** MTAR flags that its largest customer (Bloom) is actively working to reduce bill-of-materials costs via component substitution (e.g., replacing Inconel with lower-cost materials), which could compress realizations even if MTAR's own value-add is protected (Nov 2025 and Feb 2026 calls) — a customer-driven repricing risk not mentioned in Azad's disclosures.
- **Succession/ownership risk surfacing in M&A.** Unimech's Hobel Bellows acquisition rationale included the seller's lack of succession plan — an industry-specific due-diligence risk category with no direct Azad analogue but illustrative of the kind of governance risk peers surface candidly that Azad's concalls (per B05) tend not to volunteer.

## PART 4: TRIANGULATION SUMMARY

- **Claims verified: 2 of 7** (Claim 4 — elongated working-capital cycles during capacity ramp; Claim 7 — capex intensity/dedicated single-customer factories are sector-typical, not an outlier).
- **Claims partially verified: 2 of 7** (Claim 3 — data-center demand supercycle, confirmed in adjacent technologies (fuel cells, gensets) but not gas-turbine-specific; Claim 6 — US-India tariff relief, directly and specifically confirmed by Unimech but only partial, not complete removal of the cost overhang).
- **Claims unverifiable: 3 of 7** (Claim 1 — sole-supplier exclusivity for gas/nuclear-turbine airfoils; Claim 2 — margin gains specifically from domestic super-alloy/titanium sourcing, complicated by a stale but direct peer statement that qualified metal sourcing remained Western as of Feb 2024; Claim 5 — share gains against Howmet/Precision Castparts, no peer evidence either direction).
- **Claims contradicted: 0 of 7** outright, though Claim 2 carries a material complicating data point (Dynamatic, Feb 2024) that runs counter to an implicit premise of Azad's claim, and is flagged as such rather than forced into a CONTRADICTED bucket given the 2-year timing mismatch.

**Single most consequential complicating finding.** Dynamatic Technologies (Feb 2024) stated flatly that qualified titanium/superalloy metal sources for Indian aerospace manufacturing remained Western, not domestic, "even today." No current-period peer (MTAR, across four FY26 calls) offers any commentary on domestic vs. imported super-alloy/titanium sourcing as a margin driver at all. This means Claim 2 — margin gains from domestic sourcing — is unsupported by the peer set in either direction for the FY26 period, and the one directly relevant data point available runs against the premise. This should be treated as an evidence gap in Azad's own disclosures (per B05) rather than a verified industry trend.

**Single strongest independent confirmation.** MTAR and Unimech, independently and across seven combined FY26 quarterly transcripts, both show working-capital days rising materially (MTAR: 229→267→260 days with negative interim OCF; Unimech: ~90–100→150–160 days) specifically during phases of capacity ramp into longer-gestation segments (nuclear, in both cases), before eventually normalizing. This directly and substantively informs Azad's own negative-FY26-OCF-against-positive-PAT situation: the pattern is genuinely sector-normal for Indian precision-manufacturing companies expanding into nuclear/long-cycle segments, not obviously anomalous to Azad. It does not, however, resolve the INDETERMINATE cash-conversion classification on its own — Azad has not disclosed the quantified WC-day figures or resolution timeline that both peers provided.

**Overall assessment.** The peer set complicates more than it cleanly supports or undercuts Azad's management narrative. It supports the macro backdrop (structural demand growth, an industry-wide capacity race, and — via Unimech — real and dated confirmation of tariff relief) and it normalizes the two claims most central to the cash-conversion and capex-credibility questions (working-capital elongation and capex intensity), which is genuinely useful independent evidence in Azad's favor on execution risk. But it does not corroborate Azad's most differentiating, moat-defining claims — sole-supplier exclusivity, margin gains specifically from domestic metal sourcing, and share gains against Howmet/Precision Castparts — because no peer operates closely enough in Azad's specific hot-section-airfoil niche to speak to them, and the one directly relevant historical data point (Dynamatic, 2024) runs mildly against the domestic-sourcing premise. Symmetrically: peers are also more transparent than Azad appears to be (per B05) on customer concentration and tariff risk quantification, which is a modest incremental data point for the missing-risks analysis rather than a narrative-level contradiction.
