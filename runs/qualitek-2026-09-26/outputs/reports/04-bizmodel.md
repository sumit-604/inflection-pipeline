## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description

Qualitek runs accredited labs that test other companies' products against required
standards, and it sells that test as a report a factory needs before it can sell, export,
or certify what it makes (AR "Who We Are", [page 7]).

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version

Think of Qualitek as a government-certified exam board, except the "students" are factory
products, not people. A car-parts maker, a food company, or a drug maker brings Qualitek a
sample. Qualitek's accredited lab runs the required tests and hands back a signed report
that says "this passes." Without that report, the factory cannot sell the product,
especially abroad. Qualitek does not make anything itself; it sells trust, backed by a
government-recognised badge (accreditation) that took years to earn. The catch: Qualitek
has been buying other test-report shops (ITCPL, LabOps) to grow fast, using debt and new
shares, and the cash from customers is coming in slower than the bills for the new labs are
going out.

| Commonly tracked ratio | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|

### 3D. Unit economics — the physics of the business

| Element | Reading |
|---|---|
| One unit | One accredited test/inspection assignment (heterogeneous across verticals: an EV battery validation and a food-residue test are priced very differently, so this is a directional unit, not a uniform SKU) |

## Analyst note (business-model framing only; not a valuation or governance verdict)

The business model itself is straightforward and evidenced: Qualitek sells accredited
technical judgment, priced per assignment, in an industry with genuine structural tailwinds
(AR "Our Market", [page 10]). The model's own physics explain the AVERAGE Gate 0
classification and the flagged cash-conversion problem without needing a governance story:
gross margin is rising because more of the cost sits in fixed lab overhead and skilled
labour, not materials, and that fixed-cost structure has not yet delivered the operating
leverage it should, because utilisation of the newly built and newly acquired capacity is
still low (AR's own stated FY27 priority: "increasing utilisation of existing laboratory
capacity", [page 73]). This stage cannot resolve whether that lag is GROWTH-INDUCED
(temporary, capex-ramp related) or STRUCTURAL (a permanent feature of buying labs faster
than they can be integrated), because no receivables-by-customer-type or utilisation-rate
disclosure exists in this corpus (input gap, carried forward to LBF2). The revenue-mix and
customer-concentration numbers used above are the best evidence available but sit on two
different clocks: the vertical mix is FY26 (Inv. Pres.), while customer concentration is
FY23 pre-IPO, standalone-only (DRHP) — a live gap for later stages to close, not something
this stage can paper over with an estimate.
