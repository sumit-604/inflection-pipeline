## PART 1: CLAIM-BY-CLAIM VERIFICATION

### Q1. No-overcapacity argument (142GW ALMM cell capacity, ~70-75GW effective, vs
~65-70GW DC demand from government tenders)

**Verdict: PARTIALLY VERIFIED, with a magnitude and terminology discrepancy that itself is a
finding.**

Peer evidence:
- Waaree (30-Apr-2026 call, p.20): "the real available supply for the sector asking for DCR is
  not... 160 gigawatt (ALMM I approved module). It is rather (ALMM) II approved solar cell
  capacities integrated with module capacity... relevant capacity to note is 30 gigawatt today...
  While the demand, like in last financial year, 50-gigawatt, 55 gigawatts of module were
  consumed to install 44.6-gigawatt worth of AC site of solar installations" (Abhishek Pareek).
  Waaree's own "relevant" ALMM-II-compliant cell capacity figure (30GW) is far below INA's
  claimed 70-75GW effective figure for the same regulatory basis.
- Waaree (22-Jan-2026 call, p.19): analyst Shashank Jha asks directly, "we can say that the
  overcapacity is just a myth that is flowing in the industry, right?" — the moderator cuts the
  call before a direct answer, but CEO Amit Paithankar's closing remark affirms demand strength
  ("India is a big country. And the demand is going to be very-very strong") without disputing
  the framing. Directionally aligned with INA's no-overcapacity claim but not a clean answer.
- Premier (29-Oct-2025 call, p.21-22): "current cell capacity in India is about 27 gigawatt and
  the current run rate in terms of production is about 16, 17 gigawatts per annum... our
  expectation is that by the end of 2027 we should be able to have a total capacity of about 80
  gigawatts" (Vinay Rustagi).
- Premier (23-Jan-2026 call, p.14-15): "if you look at certain reports... as early as January '25...
  Kotak... estimated FY26, 54 gigawatt of cell capacity would be up and running in India. But as
  we speak today, we are way below 30 gigawatt" and "total announcements by something like 45
  to 50 companies... add up to something like 200 gigawatts" nameplate, of which actual
  production will be "far, far short" (Chiranjeev Saluja / Vinay Rustagi).
- Websol (30-Jan-2026 call, p.19): "The latest numbers which I read were 24 GW of ALMM
  approved cell capacity, 145 GW of modules capacity" (Sanjana Khaitan). Websol's 145GW
  MODULE figure sits very close to INA's "142GW ALMM cell capacity" claim — a strong signal
  that INA's figure may describe ALMM-approved MODULE capacity, not cell capacity as INA
  labelled it. Websol's own cell-capacity figure (24GW) is an order of magnitude below INA's
  "142GW ALMM cell capacity."
- Premier (Aug-2026 call, p.6-7, Q1 FY27): direct CONTRADICTION on the non-DCR side —
  "non-DCR business is not profitable as we speak... there is about 250 odd gigawatt of module
  lines and the entire demand in India is about 60 gigawatt, out of which almost about 30
  gigawatt is DCR... there is a serious situation over there" (Chiranjeev Saluja). This is an
  explicit peer acknowledgement of real, severe non-DCR module overcapacity, in direct tension
  with a blanket "no overcapacity" reading of INA's claim.

Peers silent: none of the three peers frames the argument using INA's exact figures (142GW /
70-75GW / 65-70GW); each uses its own capacity/demand framing.

Net read: peers consistently support the narrower claim that ALMM-II-compliant, ramped-up CELL
capacity remains scarce relative to demand (Waaree ~30GW relevant vs ~50-55GW module demand;
Premier ~27-30GW actual vs nameplate ~200GW announced; Websol ~24GW ALMM cell approved).
That much of INA's "no overcapacity" argument holds directionally. But INA's own headline
number (142GW ALMM "cell" capacity) is implausible against every peer's cell-specific figure and
matches Websol's MODULE capacity figure almost exactly — this looks like a cell/module
conflation in INA's corpus, not a verified industry fact. Separately, Premier's Q1 FY27 call
flatly states non-DCR module capacity is oversupplied and "not profitable," a segment INA's
argument does not carve out. VERDICT: the underlying cell-scarcity thesis is corroborated; the
specific 142GW/70-75GW figures and the blanket "no overcapacity" framing are not, and likely
mislabel module capacity as cell capacity.

### Q3. Per-GW capex and commissioning timeline for peer cell lines of similar scale/technology

**Verdict: PARTIALLY VERIFIED — direction plausible, INA's per-GW figure looks low against
the nearest comparable peer benchmark.**

Peer evidence:
- Websol (30-Jan-2026 call, p.6-7): "Phase-3... cost of around Rs. 1,600 crores to Rs. 1,700 crores
  for an integrated 2 GW Topcon cell and module manufacturing facility" (Sanjana Khaitan) — a
  greenfield, similarly-scaled (small/mid-cap), same-technology (TOPCon) project. That is
  ~Rs800-850cr/GW for a combined cell+module line. INA's guide is ~Rs1,500cr for 4.5GW of
  CELL-ONLY TOPCon capacity (~Rs333cr/GW), a materially lower per-GW figure even after
  allowing that Websol's number bundles module capacity (which typically adds cost). This is a
  meaningful, testable gap: INA's own greenfield cell capex guidance sits well below the nearest
  disclosed peer benchmark for the same technology and scale class.
- Websol (28-Apr-2026 call, p.5-6): a 150MW TOPCon brownfield upgrade (existing Mono-PERC
  line) costs Rs250-270cr — high per-GW (~Rs1,700-1,800cr/GW) because it is a small partial
  upgrade, not representative of at-scale greenfield economics; not directly comparable.
- Premier (29-Oct-2025 call, p.13): brownfield debottleneck from 4.8GW to 7GW TOPCon cost
  "about INR502 crores" incremental for 2.2GW (~Rs228cr/GW) — cheap because it reuses
  existing land/utilities ("23% saving" vs greenfield); explicitly NOT comparable to a standalone
  greenfield project.
- On timeline: Premier's brownfield lines ramp in "4 to 6 months" (29-Oct-2025 call, p.12-13);
  Websol's TOPCon upgrade targets "less than one year" commercial start with "2 months" to full
  ramp (28-Apr-2026 call, p.5); Waaree's new lines take "4 to 6 months" per line (22-Jan-2026
  call, p.13). INA's internally inconsistent COD guidance (Q3 FY27 vs Q4 FY27 within the same
  call, per B05) sits within this general 4-12 month ramp window once commissioned, but INA's
  inconsistency on WHICH quarter is a company-specific credibility problem, not something peers
  illuminate either way.

Net read: no peer discloses a cell-only greenfield TOPCon capex figure at a truly comparable
scale to INA's 4.5GW plan; the closest available peer benchmark (Websol, integrated cell+module)
implies a materially higher per-GW cost than INA guides, which is a caution flag for INA's
capex discipline, not a confirmation.

### Q4. Cell-driven PAT margin premium magnitude and margin compression timeline

**Verdict: PARTIALLY VERIFIED — direction strongly confirmed by multiple peers; INA's specific
magnitude (+400-500bps, ~20% by Q4 FY27) and 4-5 year compression timeline are not
independently corroborated at that precision.**

Peer evidence:
- Waaree (30-Jul-2026 call, p.17): "compared to module only suppliers in India, let's say, module
  plus cell integrated supply, the margin profile generally is between 35% to 41%" (Abhishek
  Pareek) — a very large premium for cell integration, directionally consistent with (and larger
  in magnitude than) INA's claimed uplift.
- Websol shows the premium in reverse: as module share of revenue rose, blended EBITDA
  margin fell from 47% (Q1 FY26) to 34% (Q1 FY27) even as absolute EBITDA grew 21% (11-Aug-
  2026 call, p.3-4, Sohan Lal Agarwal / Sanjana Khaitan) — explicit confirmation that
  cell-heavy mix carries materially higher margin than module-heavy mix.
- Premier (Aug-2026 call, p.6-7): confirms non-DCR module margin is compressed to
  unprofitable levels ("not profitable as we speak") while DCR/cell margins remain "healthy,
  industry-leading" — consistent direction.
- On compression timeline: Waaree guides "19%-20% margin consistent for a decade long at
  least" (30-Apr-2026 call, p.28, Abhishek Pareek) — i.e. a much LONGER sustain horizon than
  compression; Websol foresees "some moderation... in time to come" but "definitely 2-3 years we
  foresee that margins should remain at the level that they are" (30-Jan-2026 call, p.5, Sanjana
  Khaitan) — a 2-3 year sustain window, roughly in the same range as INA's 4-5 year framing but
  not an exact match and phrased as gradual moderation, not a scheduled step-down.

Net read: the qualitative mechanism (cell/DCR integration lifts margin materially over
module-only/non-DCR) is corroborated by all three peers independently — this is one of the
strongest cross-peer confirmations in this report. The specific bps figure and multi-year
compression schedule INA's CFO gave are INA-specific and cannot be checked against peer
numbers with precision.

### Q6. Order book reporting basis and declining order book — sector churn or company signal?

**Verdict: PARTIALLY VERIFIED — order-book volatility is a normal sector feature, but the
SECTOR NORM in this period was sustained growth, making INA's unreconciled multi-quarter
decline comparatively unusual.**

Peer evidence:
- Waaree's order book fell from ~Rs60,000cr (22-Jan-2026 call, p.3, "highest-ever order book of
  Rs.60,000 crores") to Rs53,000cr one quarter later (30-Apr-2026 call, p.2-3), a Rs7,000cr
  sequential decline explicitly acknowledged and explained by shipments outpacing new
  inflow and ALMM-II-related C&I decision deferrals (30-Apr-2026 call, p.16-17, Praveen Sahay
  Q&A, Abhishek Pareek). It then recovered sharply to Rs61,500cr the following quarter
  (30-Jul-2026 call, p.2, Jignesh Rathod). This is a real, disclosed, one-quarter dip followed by
  recovery — supporting the "normal churn" reading in isolation.
- However, the broader sector pattern across the period is GROWTH, not decline: Premier's
  order book rose from a Q2 FY26 base, to Rs14,010cr (up 66% YoY) by its Q4 FY26 call
  (May-2026, p.3), to Rs15,000cr by its Q1 FY27 call (Aug-2026, p.2). Websol's order book rose
  steadily: Rs1,150cr (Jan-2026, p.4) → Rs1,161cr (Apr-2026, p.4) → Rs1,278cr (Aug-2026, p.4).
  None of the three peers shows a MULTI-QUARTER, unreconciled decline of the kind B05 flags
  for INA (2.1-2.2GW down to 1.6-1.8GW across two quarters, reported on three non-comparable
  bases).

Net read: single-quarter order-book dips do happen in this sector and are not automatically a
red flag (Waaree's case). But the sector's dominant pattern in this window was sustained order
book growth. INA's sustained, unreconciled, cross-basis decline is not something any peer's
experience normalizes; it reads as more consistent with a company-specific demand or
disclosure-quality issue than routine sector churn.

### Q7. Shift from advance payment to 45-day LC terms driving receivables growth

**Verdict: PARTIALLY VERIFIED on the mechanism; CONTRADICTED on the cash-flow
consequence — this is the single highest-value finding in this report.**

Peer evidence:
- Waaree (22-Jan-2026 call, p.10): orders are considered "firm" only against an advance,
  "anywhere between 5% to even 15%" (Sonal Shrivastava) — an advance-payment model, no LC
  mentioned.
- Waaree (30-Apr-2026 call, p.24): explicitly asked about working-capital-cycle deterioration
  (45 to ~90 days), management attributes it to inventory build and dilution of a STEADY
  advance base against higher sales volume — "the advance from customers has remained steady
  and strong, even the levels are also similar... this will have an effect in the number of days of
  advance from customers" (Abhishek Pareek). No LC terms mentioned anywhere across four
  Waaree calls.
- Premier: no mention of letter-of-credit terms across any of its four calls (searched
  systematically); order book is described as "signed, confirmed with advances received" (23-
  Jan-2026 call, p.7-8, Chiranjeev Saluja) — advance-payment model, consistent with Waaree.
- Websol (28-Apr-2026 call, p.19-20): DIRECT CONFIRMATION of the mechanism —
  "the increase reflects also the higher business volumes and the more diversified customer
  base. Importantly, the portion of these receivables is backed by letter of credit from prime
  banks, which mitigate credit risk. So, the increase [is]... in line with the industry benchmark
  also" (Amrit Daga, answering a direct question on rising debtors and inventory). Management
  further clarifies Websol does not bid government tenders directly but instead "tak[es] the
  letter of credits from these players" who do (p.20). No 45-day tenor is specified by Websol.

The decisive contrast: despite using LC-backed receivables, Websol's FY26 operating cash flow
was Rs255cr against PAT of Rs303cr — 84% cash conversion (28-Apr-2026 call, p.4, Sanjana
Khaitan) — and the company ended the year net-cash-surplus, repaying its entire remaining
IREDA term loan from internal accruals in Q1 FY27 (11-Aug-2026 call, p.2-3, Sohan Lal Agarwal).
This is the opposite of INA's outcome: INA's FY26 operating cash flow was NEGATIVE Rs73.13cr
against PAT of ~Rs201cr (per B05/company financials), i.e. roughly -36% cash conversion,
alongside 39.5% of receivables aged beyond six months.

Net read: one of three peers (Websol) confirms letter-of-credit-backed receivables are a real,
"industry benchmark" mechanism in this sector, which lends some credibility to INA management's
explanation as describing a real financing instrument rather than an invented one. But the same
peer that uses this mechanism converted 84% of PAT to operating cash in the same period INA
converted none (negative). This means the LC mechanism ITSELF does not explain a cash flow
collapse of INA's magnitude; peer evidence points toward the SEVERITY of INA's receivables
problem (not its existence) being company-specific — most plausibly tied to collection discipline
or customer concentration (recall Solarworld Energy Solutions Ltd at 12.4% of FY26 revenue,
never discussed on any INA call per B05) rather than a sector-wide financing-terms shift. No
peer specifies a 45-day tenor matching INA's claim, so that specific detail remains unverifiable.

### Q8. Polysilicon/cell price volatility and short (6-9 month) fixed-price order book duration

**Verdict: VERIFIED on volatility; CONTRADICTED on order-book duration (peers report LONGER
duration than INA's 6-9 months).**

Peer evidence:
- Silver/commodity volatility is discussed on nearly every call across all three peers: Waaree
  cites silver at "less than 9%" of module cost impact with hedging management (22-Jan-2026
  call, p.7); Premier discloses silver cost per watt rising from ~1 cent to ~2.5-2.7 cents over a
  year, offset by "almost six months of silver which is hedged" (23-Jan-2026 call, p.9, Nitin
  Arora Q&A); Websol reports a 20-25% reduction in silver CONSUMPTION via process
  optimization plus advance procurement to manage the same volatility (30-Jan-2026 call, p.5;
  11-Aug-2026 call, p.4). All three confirm the volatility is real and sector-wide, consistent with
  INA's claim.
- On order-book duration, peers report LONGER visibility than INA's 6-9 months: Premier's
  "existing order book... spans between 12 to 18 months" (29-Oct-2025 call, p.13, Chiranjeev
  Saluja) with "70% to 75% of our total order book... due for execution over the next 12 months"
  (23-Jan-2026 call, p.13, Vinay Rustagi) and cell orders extending into FY28-FY29 (Aug-2026
  call, p.4-5); Waaree's order book converts over "three to four years" for the overseas portion
  (25-Feb-2026 call, p.11-12, Abhishek Pareek). Websol's order book (smaller company, closer in
  scale to INA) is the exception and partially supports INA: "Rs.1,161 crores... something which
  we feel confident that we can complete in one financial year" (28-Apr-2026 call, p.6, Sanjana
  Khaitan) — closer to a 12-month horizon, still longer than INA's stated 6-9 months but the
  closest match among the three.

Net read: input-cost volatility is a genuine sector-wide condition, not an INA-specific hedging
failure — this part of INA's framing holds up well. But INA's claimed 6-9 month fixed-price
order book duration is shorter than what any of the three peers discloses, including the
most comparably-scaled peer (Websol, ~12 months). This suggests INA's order book, even before
its reported decline, may have carried unusually short visibility relative to sector peers — a
detail worth probing further rather than accepting as a sector-standard constraint.

## PART 2: UNPROMPTED CROSS-READ

**2A. Demand environment.** All three peers describe a demand environment that is strong but
SEGMENTED, not uniformly hot: DCR/government-scheme demand (KUSUM, PM Surya Ghar,
ALMM-compliant C&I) is described as tight-to-undersupplied by all three; non-DCR/open-market
module demand is explicitly called oversupplied and "not profitable" by Premier (Aug-2026 call,
p.6-7). This complicates INA's undifferentiated "no overcapacity" framing (see Q1) — the
sector consensus is bifurcated demand, not blanket strength.

**2B. Pricing and input costs.** Consistent story across all three: silver/commodity cost inflation
is real and sector-wide (Waaree, Premier, Websol all discuss it independently), largely absorbed
through hedging, silver-consumption reduction programs, and pass-through clauses rather than
margin erosion in the near term. This matches INA's own margin narrative directionally (cost
volatility is real) but all three peers describe ACTIVE hedging and procurement programs INA's
own corpus (per B05) never mentions — a disclosed capability gap worth noting, not a
contradiction of INA's numbers.

**2C. Capex cycle.** This is an industry-wide capacity race, not a lone-expander situation. All
three peers are simultaneously expanding cell capacity, moving toward ingot/wafer backward
integration ahead of ALMM List 3, and (Waaree, Premier) diversifying into BESS, inverters,
transformers and glass. INA's own capex program (cell line, aluminium frame, wafer/ingot
evaluation) follows the same industry template rather than standing out as either unusually
aggressive or unusually conservative. The distinguishing feature in INA's case is execution
consistency (per B05's credibility grade D), not capex direction.

**2D. Competitive mentions.** No peer transcript names Insolation Energy, "INA Solar," or an
unmistakable description of the company across all 11 calls. This silence is neutral: at INA's
scale, absence from Tier-1 peer commentary is expected and not itself informative either way.

**2E. Risks peers discuss that INA's own corpus does not (per B05).** (i) China anti-dumping/
anti-involution price dynamics and their pass-through effect on Indian cell costs (all three
peers); (ii) active silver hedging and consumption-reduction programs as a named risk-management
discipline (all three; INA's own calls per B05 never discuss hedging); (iii) explicit non-DCR
module overcapacity and unprofitability (Premier, Aug-2026); (iv) Mono PERC-to-TOPCon/G12R
technology transition risk and the "stranding" of undifferentiated smaller cell lines (Premier,
23-Jan-2026 call, p.19-20); (v) the ALMM-2 deferral itself as a live, evolving regulatory risk
(Websol, Premier, Aug-2026 calls) rather than a fixed date; (vi) customer-side liquidity
constraints causing deferred offtake as a RECOGNIZED, discussed industry phenomenon
(Websol, Premier — both explicitly name and explain it to analysts), versus INA's own corpus
(per B05) where a direct analyst question on debtor days was answered "only with a rationale, no
number given."

  wrong; June 2026 date itself was later deferred to December 2026), Q7 (LC mechanism is real
  but does not explain INA's specific cash-flow outcome).

**The single most consequential contradiction:** Q7. Websol confirms letter-of-credit-backed
receivables are a genuine, "industry benchmark" financing mechanism in this sector — so INA
management's explanation is not fabricated. But Websol converted 84% of its FY26 PAT to
operating cash using that same mechanism, while INA converted none (negative Rs73.13cr against
Rs201cr PAT). The mechanism is sector-standard; the outcome is not. This shifts the burden of
explanation back onto INA-specific factors (collection discipline, the undiscussed Solarworld
Energy Solutions customer concentration at 12.4% of revenue) rather than a sector financing-
terms shift, and should be treated as a priority item for synthesis and for the operator's
verification queue.

**The single strongest independent confirmation:** Q2. Two independent, well-capitalized peers
(Waaree, Premier) report Rs14-16/watt non-DCR and Rs21-23/watt DCR realizations in the SAME
quarter (Q4 FY26 / Q3 FY26) INA claims Rs13-14 and Rs20-22 respectively. This is about as clean
a same-quarter, cross-peer match as this kind of verification produces, and it removes pricing
as a plausible source of INA's margin or cash flow underperformance.

**Overall assessment.** The peer set complicates more than it simply supports or undercuts
INA's own narrative. It supports the core sector mechanics INA relies on: real DCR pricing
premium, real cell-integration margin uplift, real cell-capacity scarcity relative to nameplate
claims, and a genuine (if imprecisely dated) regulatory tailwind. It complicates INA's
undifferentiated framing of "no overcapacity" (peers explicitly separate DCR scarcity from
non-DCR oversupply) and its regulatory-date certainty (the June 2026 date every peer confirmed
was itself deferred to December 2026, after INA's corpus closed). It undercuts, specifically, the
sector-wide-financing-shift explanation for INA's cash flow problem: the peer that most clearly
uses the mechanism INA cites did not suffer the cash flow consequence INA suffered, in the same
period, under the same input cost pressures.

## PART 5: CROSS-PEER HYPOTHESIS

Comparing the three peers' financing-terms and receivables disclosures side by side reveals a
pattern none of them states explicitly: financing-term exposure in this sector appears to track
how close a manufacturer sits to a government-subsidy-dependent END CUSTOMER, not
company size or balance sheet strength. Waaree and Premier, whose order books are dominated
by well-capitalized IPPs, C&I corporates, and their own retail/EPC channels, report a stable,
advance-payment-based commercial model across all eight of their combined calls with no
mention of letter-of-credit terms at all. Websol, whose customers are described as module
manufacturers and pump/rooftop-scheme intermediaries who themselves resell into
government-reimbursed programs (PM-KUSUM, PM Surya Ghar), is the one peer that has adopted
LC-backed receivables specifically to manage the credit risk created by ITS CUSTOMERS' OWN
exposure to government disbursement timing — and still converts cash well, because the LC
itself is bank-guaranteed rather than a promise from the subsidy-dependent end user. This
suggests LC/receivables exposure in Indian solar manufacturing is a function of which rung of
the distribution chain a manufacturer's immediate customer occupies (well-capitalized IPP/EPC
vs. subsidy-scheme intermediary), not of company scale or of a uniform "sector-wide shift" in
payment terms. Testable implication: INA's receivables problem is more likely explained by
where INA's own customer base sits on this chain (and by customer concentration, e.g.
Solarworld Energy Solutions at 12.4% of FY26 revenue) than by a generic move to 45-day LC
terms across the industry — the next verification step should be to identify what kind of
counterparty sits behind INA's largest receivable balances.
