## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description
Macpower designs, manufactures and sells CNC (computer-controlled) metal-cutting machines — turning centres, VMCs, HMCs, turn-mill centres, double-column and 5-axis machines, including its premium NEXA range — to Indian manufacturers across 27 industry segments, increasingly including defence and aerospace factories (AR p.1, p.9, p.11-13; Inv. Pres. slide 13).

### 1B. Money flow chain, by revenue stream

**Stream 1 — Sale of machines (99.35% of FY25 revenue from operations, AR p.159 note 22):**
[Steel castings, bought-out ball screws/LM guides/spindles/CNC controllers, some imported components] → [Macpower's Metoda GIDC (Rajkot) machine shop, assembly, spindle-assembly, sheet-metal and powder-coating units design, machine, assemble and test a CNC machine (AR p.1, p.10)] → [delivers a complete, ready-to-run CNC machine, dispatched to the customer or accepted per contract terms (AR p.136, note 2.3 revenue recognition)] → [manufacturing customers across automobile, general engineering, agriculture, die & mould, railways, defence/aerospace, medical etc. (AR p.9)] → [customer pays contracted price, commonly financed through the end-customer's own bank/equipment loan (AR p.82, SWOT threat: "majority of machines roll out based on end user's loan facilities")].

**Stream 2 — Sale of services (0.54% of FY25 revenue, AR p.159 note 22):**
[Trained service engineers, spare parts, 9 tech centres] → [installation, AMC and after-sales service delivered through a 39-city branch/service network (AR p.1)] → [uptime and support for the installed base] → [existing machine owners] → [pay service/AMC fees].

**Stream 3 — Other operating revenue (0.11% of FY25 revenue, AR p.159 note 22):**
This line is a net foreign-currency gain on USD-denominated import trade payables (AR p.171, note 36), not a genuine third revenue stream — flagged here so it is not mistaken for a service or export line.

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version
Think of Macpower like a tailor shop for metal-parts factories — except instead of stitching clothes, its "sewing machines" are giant, computer-controlled metal cutters. A car-parts maker, or even an army ordnance factory, tells Macpower exactly what shapes and tolerances it needs cut, and Macpower builds a machine to do that job, using steel and precision parts (some imported), tests it, and ships it out. Because these machines are expensive and last many years, most customers pay through a bank loan, a bit like buying an auto-rickshaw on EMI. Macpower also keeps a large stock of imported components on hand — like a tailor stocking rare imported fabric so an order is never held up — but that ties up a lot of cash on the balance sheet. Increasingly, Macpower is also winning orders from the army and space programme's own factories to make tank and rocket parts, work that pays better and is much harder for a new competitor to break into.

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage and company position
The AR frames the Indian machine-tool industry as still import-dependent (domestic production meets "less than 50.0 percent of domestic demand," AR p.79) and targeted for 20-25% annual growth under Make in India/Atmanirbhar Bharat (AR p.79) — this is a **growth-stage domestic industry riding import substitution and defence indigenisation**, inside a mature global machine-tool technology curve. Macpower's own MD&A states it has expanded capacity to "nearly 2,500 machines per annum," positioning for a 15-25% CAGR over the next 3-5 years (AR p.76) — i.e., the company is mid-transition from a basic/mid-market machine maker toward a higher-end, defence-qualified supplier (product evolution from "Turner" basic lathes pre-2018 to NEXA/5-axis/HMC/DCM by FY26, Inv. Pres. slide 10).

**Working-capital physics (derived, illustrative — not company-disclosed as a single cash-conversion-cycle figure):**
Inventory days ~223 (365 ÷ 1.64x, AR p.85) + receivable days ~39 (365 ÷ 9.32x, AR p.85) − payable days ~137 (Rs 5,544.38 lakh trade payables, AR p.155 note 18, ÷ Rs 14,743.67 lakh cost of materials consumed, AR p.159, × 365) ≈ **~125-day cash conversion cycle**. This is the single most important physics fact about the business: cash is tied up far more in raw-material/component inventory than in customer credit.

## SECTION 4: RISKS, VALUATION APPROACH & MONITORING

### 4A. Business-model-specific risks

| Category | Risk | First financial line item to watch for early deterioration |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue model | Order-book/dispatch-timing lumpiness — a quarter can look weak/strong purely on shipment timing, not demand | Sequential decline in the unexecuted order-book value disclosed in AR MD&A / quarterly presentations |

| Structural | Customer dependence on bank/equipment financing for machine purchases, tying demand to the credit cycle | AR's own SWOT names this explicitly (AR p.82); watch system-wide capital-goods credit growth and machine-tool industry order intake data |

| Method | Applicable? | Rationale |

**Cycle stage that matters for valuation:** mid-cycle capacity-expansion phase — the company is transitioning from a small/mid-market domestic CNC maker toward a higher-end, defence/aerospace-qualified supplier, so entry multiples should reflect the length and quality of order-book visibility (institutional/defence orders carry longer, more predictable cycles than general-engineering orders) rather than a single trailing year's earnings.

### 4C. Quarterly monitoring checklist (14 items)

1. Unexecuted order book (Rs cr) — good: sequential growth; trouble: two straight quarters of decline (AR p.76 methodology).
2. NEXA / high-end mix of order book — good: rising share; trouble: stagnant or declining (Inv. Pres.-sourced KPI, not in FY25 AR).
3. EBITDA margin — good: ≥15% and stable/rising; trouble: sustained sub-13% (AR p.4).
4. Inventory as % of revenue / inventory turnover — good: stable or improving as capacity ramps; trouble: rising further alongside slowing sales (AR p.85, p.148).
5. Capex progress vs. guided plan (13-acre ~Rs 50 cr/12-month plan; 60-acre signing) — good: on schedule; trouble: repeated delay/cost overrun (AR p.157 CWIP; operator context for guided timeline).
6. Net debt / net cash position — good: remains net cash; trouble: borrowings rising materially beyond capex-linked need (AR p.177).
7. Defence/aerospace bid pipeline and conversion — good: bids converting to firm orders; trouble: falling win-rate versus bids submitted (Inv. Pres. slide 5, 13).
8. Realisation per machine / ASP trend — good: rising with mix upgrade; trouble: flat or declining ASP (derived from AR + Inv. Pres.).
9. Headcount / R&D team growth — good: growing in step with capacity and new-model launches; trouble: flat/declining amid stated growth ambitions (AR p.85).
10. Customer concentration — good: no customer ≥10% of revenue (as in FY25/FY24, AR p.183); trouble: any single customer/tender crossing that threshold.
11. Trade receivable ageing — good: majority within 6 months (AR p.149 shows Rs 2,912.99 lakh of Rs 3,444.66 lakh total <6 months); trouble: rising share in the >1-year buckets.
12. Contingent liabilities / bank guarantees — good: growing roughly in proportion to order book; trouble: disproportionate BG growth (AR p.161 note 32).
