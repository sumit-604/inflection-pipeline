## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description
TOTEM makes and brands metal cutting tools, taps, dies, drills, thread mills, end mills,
burrs and spring lock washers, that wear out inside a customer's machine and must be
rebought, and sells them mainly through a distributor network to India's auto, defence,
aerospace, oil & gas and general engineering factories.

### 1B. Money flow chain, one chain per revenue stream

**Stream 1: Sale of manufactured finished goods (taps, dies, drills, thread mills, end
mills, burrs, spring washers) — 98.96% of FY26 revenue from operations**
[Input: HSS steel, tungsten carbide and cobalt raw material, about 31% of the FY26 raw

[Distributors and OEM accounts pay, channel rebates/discounts netted against receivables
under the "Engineering segment" rebate arrangement (Note 36, AR p.124)] → [Credit sale,

**Stream 2: Sale of traded goods (hand tools, carbide rod, wear parts bought-in, not

[Input: finished tools bought from third-party makers] → [Company adds no manufacturing

**Stream 3: Service income — 0.20% of FY26 revenue**
[Input: application/technical support hours] → [Company's sales & application engineers

**Stream 4: Export incentives (RoDTEP/duty-drawback type government scheme income) —

[Input: qualifying FOB export shipments, Rs 31.89 cr in FY26 (Board's Report Annexure,

**Stream 5: Other operating revenue, mainly scrap sales — 0.39% of FY26 revenue**
[Input: metal turnings and off-cuts from the grinding/turning process] → [Sold as scrap] →

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version
Think of a cutting tool like the blade in a barber's razor. It does not last forever, it
wears down every time it is used, and the barber has to buy a new one again and again.
TOTEM makes those "blades" for factories instead of barbershops, in the shape of drills,
taps and cutters. The company does not sell its blades at its own shop; it sells through
200-odd hardware-style distributors spread across India, the way a razor-blade company
sells through kirana stores rather than door to door. Because the buyer keeps coming back
every time a tool wears out, the base business should be steady. But the steel and carbide
that go into the blade are partly imported and their prices swing a lot, so in a year when
metal costs jump, TOTEM's profit per blade gets squeezed until it can raise its own prices
to catch up.

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage and TOTEM's position

The Indian precision-tooling and machine-tools industry sits in a mature-but-expanding
phase, riding a rising industrial capex base (Make in India, PLI, defence indigenisation,
auto sector growth from an estimated $222 bn to $300 bn by 2026, IM p.51) rather than an
early-growth or declining phase. TOTEM itself is an old brand (65 years) in a young
corporate shell: the business was demerged from Forbes & Company Limited effective April 1,
2023, and this is only the company's 4th standalone annual report (AR p.1; IM p.9). The
company is therefore in a post-demerger consolidation and scale-building phase, investing
in capacity (furnace, CNC lines) and governance (SAP S/4HANA, first interim dividend paid
in FY26) rather than in a first-growth or mature-harvest phase.

## SECTION 3: FINANCIAL METRICS THAT MATTER FOR THIS BUSINESS MODEL

### 3A. Ignore-these, track-these

| Commonly tracked ratio | Why it misleads or is irrelevant here | Track instead |
|---|---|---|
| Debt-to-equity as a standalone risk gauge | TOTEM is net debt free (Debt-Equity ratio 0.10x FY26, Note 33.1, AR p.117) and looks low-risk on this measure, yet the real FY26 stress shows up in working capital (quick ratio fell below 1.0, MSME payables overdue nearly doubled to Rs 14.32 cr, Note 19, AR p.99), not in leverage | Quick ratio and cash conversion cycle together with Debt-Equity |
| Same-store-sales-style growth | Not a retail or store-network business; irrelevant | Revenue growth alongside the raw-material-cost-to-sales ratio, to separate volume growth from cost pass-through |
| Order book / book-to-bill | Not disclosed and not a natural KPI for a catalog/replenishment consumable model, unlike EPC or capital-goods peers | Distributor count and capacity-addition announcements as the closest available demand-pipeline proxies (both themselves thinly disclosed) |
| Multi-year average gross margin as a "sticky" reference point | Misleading here because carbide/cobalt/HSS steel are internationally priced and the FY26 evidence shows a real lag between input-cost spikes and price pass-through (materials cost 32.1% of sales FY25 to 37.2% FY26, screening Data_Sheet.csv) | Raw-material-cost-to-sales ratio tracked quarter on quarter, not as a multi-year average |
| P/B (price-to-book) as a valuation cross-check | The equity base carries demerger-related accounting history rather than a clean asset build-up, and brand/distribution value sits off balance sheet; P/B risks understating or misreading value here | Comparable-company P/E and EV/EBITDA instead (see 4B) |
| Dividend yield or payout-ratio trend | Only one data point exists: the first interim dividend was paid in April 2025 (Board's Report, AR p.29, printed p.27); too short a history to read a "policy" | Watch for a second and third consecutive year of dividend payment before treating payout as a trend |

### 3D. Unit economics: the physics of the business

A clean per-unit figure (price and cost per tool, or per kg) is NOT FOUND anywhere in the
FY26 AR or the 2024 Information Memorandum; production and sales volumes in physical units
are not disclosed, only installed-capacity estimates by product line for FY24 (IM p.57),
which the company itself says should not be relied on. The physics can still be described
directionally:

| Element | Reading |
|---|---|
| One unit | One cutting tool (a tap, drill, die, thread mill, end mill, burr or spring washer), bought as a wear-and-replace item |
| Revenue per unit | NOT FOUND (no per-piece price disclosed) |
| Cost per unit | NOT FOUND (no per-piece cost disclosed); raw material alone was 37.2% of revenue in FY26 (Note 23A) |
| Volume drivers | Customer machine hours/production run rate (replacement frequency), new distributor/customer additions, capacity additions (furnace +90 MT, CNC +~350,000 HSS drill units in FY26, established upstream) |
| Price drivers | Extent and speed of raw-material cost pass-through (partial and lagged in FY26), product mix shift toward higher-value carbide/HPT lines versus standard HSS, export versus domestic mix |
| Cost drivers | Imported carbide/cobalt/HSS steel (~31% of FY26 material cost imported), power for heat-treatment and grinding, skilled and unionised labour (labour settlement valid to 2028, Board's Report, AR p.27, printed p.25), freight/distribution through the channel |
| Incremental margin / operating leverage | FY26 shows the opposite of operating leverage: revenue grew 7.9% but operating margin fell from 18% to 16.39% because raw-material cost grew 25.3% (screening Data_Sheet.csv); Q1 FY27 (established upstream) shows margin recovering to a 16.1-22.9% range, consistent with a lagged pass-through finally catching up rather than a structural leverage gain |

### 4D. Highest-value questions for management

1. What share of FY26 revenue came from automotive/auto-components versus other end
   markets, and how has that mix moved over the last three years?
   Reassures: a falling or already-modest auto share. Worries: a rising auto share into an
   EV transition the company itself has flagged as a risk (AR p.26).
2. What is current capacity utilisation by product line after the FY26 furnace and CNC
   additions?
   Reassures: utilisation above roughly 75% and rising. Worries: utilisation still well
   below 50%, meaning capex has run ahead of demand.
3. What share of revenue is replacement/consumable reorder from existing qualified
   accounts versus new-customer or new-machine-linked demand?
   Reassures: a high, sticky replacement share. Worries: revenue leaning mostly on new
   accounts, which is less durable.
4. Why did operating margin fall from 18% to 16.39% in FY26 despite stated price
   pass-through actions, and how many quarters is the pass-through lag?
   Reassures: a one-to-two-quarter lag that is now closing, consistent with the Q1 FY27
