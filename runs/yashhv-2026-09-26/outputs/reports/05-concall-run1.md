### 2B. Excuse pattern analysis

There are few outright "misses" analysts pressed on, because headline financial guidance was beaten each period. The pattern that does recur is not blame-shifting but **reframing a slipped date or an escalated cost as if it were the plan all along**, with no acknowledgement of the earlier number:
- Greenfield timeline moved from "March-2026" (Call-A) → "trial Feb-March 2026" (Call-2) → "trial October 2026" (Call-3), stated each time in confident, unhedged language ("we don't see any delay," "everything is happening on time," Call-2; "on schedule," Call-3).
- Capex moved from Rs90cr → Rs100cr+ → Rs153cr with no "we underestimated" language.
- The one instance of a genuine external-blame answer is measured and specific: the Middle-East war is named as a cause of "indirect cost escalation" via oil/gas-linked input prices (Call-3), immediately followed by "we are also able to successfully communicate to our customers" (i.e., passed through, not absorbed) — external factor named, but management also credits itself for managing it. This is the healthiest pattern in the three calls.
- No instance across the three calls of management volunteering "we made a mistake" or "we got this wrong." The closest is an admission of a genuine operational trade-off: JIT inventory management was explicitly abandoned ("once upon a time there was a concept of just in time... now in our situation today it is better to have the available components in hand," Call-2) in response to a direct question about rising inventory — this is transparent, not defensive.
- Analyst pressure, not proactive disclosure, is what surfaces the two most sensitive topics: the cyber-fraud incident (raised only when Akshay asked directly in Call-3) and the DRHP-vs-press-release volume discrepancy (raised only when Naman Parmar asked in Call-3). Neither was mentioned in opening remarks.

## SECTION 3: COMPETITIVE INTELLIGENCE FROM CONCALLS

### 3A. Competitor commentary and credibility check

Management names a consistent competitor set across all calls: globally Hitachi, Trench (Italy), and GE ("~80% of the global market" combined, Call-2); the balance held by Yash, Crompton Greaves (CG Power), Moser-Glaser (Yash's former Swiss technology/equity partner, now purely a core supplier), Chinese makers, and Mehru Isolator / Massa Izolyator (Russian JV, MIM Bhiwadi). Domestically: Crompton Greaves and Hitachi Energy India (the latter "also having an assembly model as what we have," Call-2). Management consistently declines to rank itself against named competitors when asked directly (Call-3, Sk Nathani: "everybody has got its own core competency to sell") — a diplomatic, non-defensive answer, credible as far as it goes, but it also means no verifiable market-share comparison against Hitachi Energy or Crompton Greaves is ever volunteered beyond Yash's own claimed 30-35% domestic and ~70% RIP-up-to-245kV share (Call-A), which is self-reported and unverified in these transcripts.

### 3B. Industry and market intelligence

- Global transformer bushing market: ~Rs25,000-26,000cr, growing at 5.6% CAGR through 2034 (Call-2); Yash's addressable range (33kV-245kV, later extended to 550kV) is Rs10,000-12,000cr today, expanding to Rs15,000-16,000cr with the 550kV extension.
- Only 12-13 independent bushing makers globally vs 250+ transformer manufacturers investing in capacity (Call-2) — the core "supply-constrained for 6-10 years" thesis management repeats in every call.
- Bushing cost is consistently cited as 2-6% of transformer value (Call-A: 2-6%; Call-3: ~5%); every transformer needs 6-7 bushings (3 LV, 3 HV, 1 neutral, Call-3).
- RIP:OIP price differential: ~4-4.5x in India, ~2x globally at similar kV rating (Call-1).
- Domestic-vs-export price realisation delta cited as 30-40% (generic, not RIP-specific, Call-2).
- A notable **narrative reversal** in Call-3: OIP (not RIP) is now the supply-constrained product domestically — "OIPs are in shortage... we are also limited on capacity" — inverting the RIP-shortage story told in every earlier call; management attributes this to having stabilised RIP supply while OIP capacity lagged, and cites it as the reason for the brownfield OIP investment.
- Chinese bushings have "never been restricted in India... last 6, 7 years" but Indian buyers have not switched due to pricing, availability, and after-sales service (Call-3) — relevant to the import-substitution thesis (LBF4): the domestic-substitution argument for RIP/RIS cores rests on cost and lead-time advantage, not on any policy barrier to imports.
- Management states repeatedly it does not expect a bushing-side price war for 7-8 years even if transformer OEMs enter one, because bushing capacity (unlike transformer capacity) cannot be stood up quickly given multi-year customer-qualification cycles (Call-2, responding to Keshav's overcapacity question).
