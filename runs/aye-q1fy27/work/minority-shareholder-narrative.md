# The story for a minority shareholder (plain language, 2026-07-23)

Written for the operator, who holds 4% at Rs 132. Saved to the AYE Notion page.

You own a slice of a micro-enterprise lender that hands small working-capital loans, around Rs 1.5 lakh each, to tiny businesses that banks ignore. The market is huge and barely tapped, and Aye is one of the few doing it at scale with 571 branches and 6.7 lakh borrowers. That is the real attraction. The recovery is genuine and you can see it in the numbers: bad loans have fallen four quarters running to 4.49%, the loan book grew 28% this year, profit tripled to Rs 75 crore in the quarter, and after the February IPO the company is sitting on so much capital (about 41 to 42% adequacy) that it can grow for two more years without asking you for more money. A second rating agency just upgraded it, which will lower its borrowing cost. On the surface, a small owner has plenty to like.

Now the parts a minority holder must not gloss over. First, nobody at the top has founder-style wealth riding on the share price. The company has no promoter, it is backed by private equity and venture funds who came in to exit, so your interests are watched by professionals, not by an owner whose net worth moves with yours. Second, the good credit-cost number is partly managed: the reported 4.01% includes a Rs 6 crore cushion the company chose to build, so the true figure is nearer 3.68%, and management openly says it will keep smoothing profits this way in good years. Third, and this is the one that should bother you most, when analysts asked for the slippage number, the coverage ratio, and the status of loan covenants that were breached, management simply did not answer. As a small shareholder you are a passenger, and this quarter the driver decided what you got to see.

The price is the other hard truth. At Rs 132 you bought near the bottom of the range our own work called fair, but the most conservative and most appropriate lens for a lender, price to book, says fair value is only Rs 81 to Rs 90, and you are paying almost twice book. So you have not bought cheap; you have bought a forward recovery that now has to actually show up. The whole case rests on one number staying down, the bad-loan ratio, because if it turns back up the asset quality and the returns crack together. And the growth itself carries a quiet risk: most of it comes from lending more to borrowers Aye already has, in a segment that only recently blew up from too many lenders piling on the same people.

The honest bottom line for you as a minority owner: this is a real, improving business run by a clean and capable team, bought at a full price, where you must trust disclosures you cannot fully verify. What decides whether your Rs 132 was smart or early is simple to watch. Next quarter, does the company finally show the slippage number and the coverage ratio, and does the bad-loan ratio keep falling. If yes, your entry looks good and you can consider adding. If the silences continue or the bad loans tick up, you hold what you have and add nothing, because the price already assumes the good ending.
