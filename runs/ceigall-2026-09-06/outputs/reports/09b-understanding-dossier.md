### PART A — THE FROM STATE

**A1. Archetype.** The business is a HYBRID of two archetypes in one set of
accounts, with opposite cash and working-capital profiles (B04
FLAG-HYBRID-VALUATION):
- EPC line (57.86% of FY26 revenue, AR sheet 32): **Order-book business
  (EPC/defence/capital goods)** — order inflow, book-to-bill, execution
  pace, working capital, margin on backlog.
- HAM line (41.68% of FY26 revenue): closest fit is **Licence/scarcity
  business** — the concession itself is a per-asset regulatory exclusivity
  (a 15-20 year NHAI annuity award), with a regulated fixed payment and a
  capacity cap set by the concession terms (B04, B07: "per-asset
  regulatory exclusivity once a HAM concession is won: durability high but
  narrow, not portfolio-wide"). It does not fit "lender" or "franchise"
  because there is no repeat-customer or cross-sell economics across the
  portfolio (B07 C1: "no cross-sell... beyond O&M's 0.46%").
- O&M (0.46%) is a minor fixed periodic service-fee sliver riding on the
  HAM line; not separately archetyped.

**A2. The simple analogy.** Ceigall is a road-building contractor. Twenty
four years old, it bids for government road, highway, metro-viaduct, tunnel
and bridge jobs, wins some of them on lowest qualified price, and builds
them under milestone-billed contracts (B04, B03). For a growing minority of
jobs it does something different: instead of just building the road and
handing it over, it puts its own equity into a project company, keeps the
finished road for 15 to 20 years, and collects a fixed government payment
twice a year instead of tolls (B04). It has sold exactly one of those roads
so far (B02, B05). The customer in every case is a government body — NHAI,
MoRTH, a state public works department, or a metro corporation — and the
customer, not Ceigall, sets the price by picking the lowest qualified
bidder (B04).

## SECTION 3: BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING NARRATIVE

Ceigall builds roads, highways, metro viaducts, tunnels and bridges for
Indian government agencies. That contracting work, EPC, was 57.86 percent of
FY26 revenue, and operations and maintenance was another 0.46 percent (AR
sheet 32, B04). A second engine, HAM, was 41.68 percent: Ceigall builds a
road, puts its own equity into a project company, and holds a 15 to 20 year
concession under which NHAI pays fixed twice yearly instalments instead of
tolls (B04). The customer cannot easily do without a contractor, because
government agencies tender road work rather than build it, and the tunnel,
elevated corridor and hilly terrain jobs narrow the pool of bidders who
qualify (B04, B07). The buyers are named: NHAI, MoRTH, state public works
departments, the Uttar Pradesh and Jaipur metro corporations, and GMADA
(B03, B04). They buy at the lowest qualified price, they prequalify on
technical record and bid capacity, and they pay against milestone bills
certified by their own engineer, so switching costs fall to near zero once a
contract closes and every new road is bid again (B04).

Demand today comes from the national and state road building programme. The
signals that drive it are the NHAI monthly award and construction km
bulletin, the Union Budget MoRTH and NHAI allocation, Bharatmala Pariyojana
Phase II and NIP 2.0 progress reports, state PWD and road development
corporation tenders in Punjab, Bihar and Madhya Pradesh, metro rail
corporation awards from UPMRC and JMRC, the NHAI InvIT and TOT monetisation
announcements that recycle funding back into fresh tendering, and the crude
oil and bitumen price index that sets input cost (B09). Whether that demand
grows is less settled. NHAI awarded about 3,100 km in FY26, down 22 percent
year on year and short of a 7,500 km target, which all three peers attribute
to land acquisition and cabinet approval delay (B06, B09). The order book of
Rs 18,554 crore at 4.8 times book to bill gives Ceigall its own visibility
regardless, and the market sizing work leaves 10.1 times revenue headroom on
a 9.9 percent current share of the served market (B09). The same sizing puts
the reachable path at roughly 10 percent implied revenue growth a year,
below management's own minimum 15 percent guide for FY27 (B09). Renewables
and transmission carry signed letters of award and power purchase
agreements worth Rs 4,343 crore, and showed 0.0 percent completion on every
order book line at 30 June 2026 (B04).

Competitive advantage sits in one place only. Standard road EPC has none,
because the lowest bidder sets the price (B04). The specialised structure
record in tunnels and elevated corridors is documented and carries early
completion bonuses, but the emerging moat scan reads it as an execution lead
rather than a structural barrier, since a well capitalised peer can
mobilise the same capability given time (B07). HAM gives exclusivity on one
road for one concession term and nothing across the portfolio; renewables
and transmission have no moat and no revenue yet (B04, B07). The scan
scored 4.3 out of 92 and classified the forward moat as NONE, with research
and development and technology absorption disclosed as nil in both FY25
and FY26, and equipment rented rather than owned (B07). The run did not
establish how much of the 41.68 percent HAM revenue is construction phase
work versus post commissioning annuity income, because that split is not
disclosed anywhere in the filing (B04).

## SECTION 5: PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY

1. Ceigall builds roads, highways, metro viaducts, tunnels and bridges for
   Indian government agencies (B04).
2. It also builds and owns roads under HAM. It puts in equity, holds a 15
   to 20 year NHAI concession, and gets paid fixed instalments twice a
   year instead of tolls (B04).
3. FY26 revenue splits 57.86 percent EPC contracting, 41.68 percent HAM,
   and 0.46 percent operations and maintenance (B04, AR sheet 32).
4. The buyers are named: NHAI, MoRTH, state public works departments, and
   metro corporations in Uttar Pradesh and Jaipur (B03, B04).
5. Buyers pick the lowest qualified bidder and pay against milestone
   bills, so a contractor keeps almost no pricing power once a job starts
   (B04).
6. Demand rides the national road-building programme, but NHAI awarded
   about 3,100 km in FY26, down 22 percent year on year and short of a
   7,500 km target (B06, B09).
7. The order book of Rs 18,554 crore, 4.8 times last year's revenue, gives
   near-term visibility even while the wider award market slows (B03).
8. Market sizing shows 10.1 times revenue headroom on a 9.9 percent served-
   market share, but the reachable growth path implied is only about 10
   percent a year, below management's own 15 percent floor for FY27 (B09).
9. The one durable edge is a specialised-structure execution record in
   tunnels and elevated corridors, read as an execution lead a
   well-funded peer can eventually match, not a structural barrier (B07).
10. HAM gives exclusivity on one road for one concession term only, not
    across the whole portfolio, and renewables and transmission carry
    signed awards worth Rs 4,343 crore but zero revenue as of 30 June 2026
    (B04, B07).
11. The claimed transition is from pure contracting to owning and
    recycling road assets. Management has completed exactly one sale, and
    the price was never disclosed on any of four calls or in the annual
    report (B05, B02).
12. The mental model here is a hybrid: an order-book EPC business trying to
    climb from a commodity price-taker toward a value-added, spec'd-
    supplier standing, next to a HAM line whose real quality tier cannot
    be set because the split between construction revenue and annuity
    income inside it is undisclosed (B04, Section 2 of this dossier).
13. The fragility read is FRAGILE: several forward variables, most of them
    dependent on company disclosure, and the disclosure of the ones that
    matter, consolidated debt, EBITDA, and margin sustainability, has been
    getting worse, not better, across four quarters (B05).
14. The corpus could not establish how much of HAM revenue is construction
    phase versus annuity income, could not establish a rating agency's own
    view on cash conversion, and could not establish the promoter group's
    full history because the IPO prospectus is absent (B04, B08, B00).
15. The two biggest open questions at Halt 1: which step-down subsidiary
    actually carries the auditor-resignation and cash-loss flags, and
    whether the four-quarter cash-flow and disclosure pattern reflects a
    HAM build-out or a genuine collection problem (B02, B03,
    gate-recommendation.md).
