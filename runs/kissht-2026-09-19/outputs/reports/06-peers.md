### Claim 4 — Peer cost-of-funds trajectory for FY27 and whether peers
cite the same FCNR(B)-driven system liquidity easing Kissht cites

**Verdict: CONTRADICTED (direction, for the quarter in question)**

Peer evidence:
- POONAWALLA: cost of borrowing DID fall through most of FY26 — 8.04%
  (Q1 FY26) to 7.65% (Q3 FY26, Jan-2026 call, p.16), driven explicitly by
  NCD-mix shift (7% to 27% of borrowing) and "cumulative repo cuts of 125
  basis points since February" (POONAWALLA-Concall_Jan_2026_Transcript.txt
  p.14). But by Q1 FY27 (Jul-2026 call — the SAME quarter Kissht made its
  FCNR(B)-tailwind claim), Poonawalla's cost of borrowing ROSE to 7.72%
  from 7.63% in Q4 FY26 (+9 bps QoQ), which management (Arvind Kapil,
  Sanjay Miranka) attributes to bond-yield and "geopolitical environment"
  pressure, not further easing (POONAWALLA-Concall_Jul_2026_Transcript.txt
  p.16, p.19).
- SBICARD: cost of funds stable at 6.6% in Q1 FY27, but management states
  explicitly "we expect cost of funds to trend HIGHER in line with market
  rates" going forward (SBICARD-Concall_Jul_2026_Transcript.txt p.5) — the
  opposite direction to a continuing system-wide easing tailwind. The
  earlier-cycle easing had already been largely spent: SBICARD's own
  Feb-2026 call states "the benefit from repo rate cuts on our floating
  book has been mostly absorbed. The cost of funds is expected to remain
  stable" (SBICARD-Concall_Feb_2026_Transcript.txt p.5).
- Neither SBICARD nor POONAWALLA mentions FCNR(B) deposits or any RBI
  system-liquidity measure by name in any transcript. UGROCAP contains no
  cost-of-funds trajectory commentary of comparable specificity for FY27.

Net read: two of three peers, reporting for the SAME quarter (Q1 FY27,
Jul-2026 calls) in which Kissht cited a system-wide FCNR(B)-driven
liquidity tailwind, independently describe cost of borrowing as
FLAT-TO-RISING, not easing, and neither cites FCNR(B) or a comparable
liquidity mechanism at all. This is a genuine, quantified CONTRADICTION
of the DIRECTION of Kissht's claimed tailwind at the time it was made.
This is consequential: Stage 5 graded Kissht's cost-of-borrowing guidance
as "delivered so far" partly on the strength of this claimed tailwind: peer
evidence says the tailwind two comparable lenders actually experienced in
the same quarter ran the other way.

### Claim 6 — Precedent for large preferential/QIP capital raises shortly
after listing or at a CRAR comfortably above regulatory minimums, with
numeric rationale

**Verdict: CONTRADICTED (as a precedent for Kissht's specific pattern)**

Peer evidence:
- POONAWALLA raised Rs 2,500 Cr via QIP in April 2026. Pre-raise CRAR was
  16.83% (Tier-1 15.90%) — only ~1.8 points above the RBI's 15% NBFC
  regulatory minimum, not "comfortably above" in the sense Kissht's 40.2%
  represents (2.5x the minimum). Post-raise pro forma CRAR reached 20.74%
  (POONAWALLA-Concall_May_2026_Transcript.txt p.14). The rationale given
  was explicit and numeric: an analyst (Jay Betai) asked directly how a
  negative long-tenor ALM gap would be bridged, and management answered
  "it gets bridged. With the capital raise, it has been bridged" (p.19) —
  i.e. a stated, quantifiable balance-sheet need, not a general growth
  platitude.
- UGROCAP: management (Shachindra Nath) volunteers an explicit, repeated
  historical pattern: "our model necessitated that almost at every point
  of time when we reach roughly around 18% of capital adequacy, we need
  to raise more capital... every 1 year, 1.5 years... we were raising
  capital" (UGROCAP-Concall_Nov_2025_Transcript.txt p.9). The Rs 550 Cr
  preferential-plus-Rs 380 Cr rights-issue raise referenced on that same
  call (Jun-2025) was tied explicitly to funding the Profectus
  acquisition and continued growth (p.4, p.9), at a CRAR of 25.4% at the
  time of the call (already post-raise, not several multiples of the
  regulatory floor).

Net read: CONTRADICTED as a precedent for Kissht's specific fact pattern.
Both peers that discuss capital raises give a clear, NUMERIC rationale
(an ALM gap to bridge for Poonawalla; a modelled ~18%-CRAR growth/
acquisition-funding trigger for Ugro), and Poonawalla in particular
raises when its CRAR sits close to its own working range, not several
multiples above the regulatory floor. Neither peer's precedent resembles
a raise at 40.2% CRAR (2.5x the 15% minimum) four months post-IPO with no
numeric rationale given anywhere in the filed record. This strengthens,
rather than weakens, B05's LBF-4 flag: sector practice among comparable
lenders does not normalise Kissht's pattern; if anything it sharpens the
contrast.

## PART 5: CROSS-PEER HYPOTHESIS

Kissht's management met a rising-delinquency question with a
"technicality... not anything to do with the credit quality improving or
worsening" framing (B05 LBF-1). Independently, on the SAME kind of
question, Poonawalla's management denied that a credit-cost/write-off
move had anything to do with "accelerated write-offs," calling it
"business-as-usual credit costs" (POONAWALLA-Concall_Jan_2026 p.19), and
Ugro's management attributed a GNPA rise from 2.2% to 2.5% to "a
denominator effect as the non-focus book runs down," explicitly stating
"it is essentially not a deterioration in the incremental portfolio"
(UGROCAP-Concall_Apr_2026_Transcript.txt p.6).

**Hypothesis:** three separately-run management teams, facing
structurally identical analyst pressure (a rising headline asset-quality
ratio), independently reach for the SAME rhetorical move — attribute the
rise to a measurement mechanic (write-off timing, denominator shrinkage)
rather than engage a credit-quality read — even where, as with Kissht,
independently-derived evidence (B02/B03's write-off-rate acceleration
finding) leaves room for a more mixed interpretation. No single peer
transcript states this pattern; it is visible only by placing all three
management teams' answers to the same style of question side by side.
This is testable: if a fourth NBFC/digital-lender concall in this
coverage universe shows the same "technicality"/"denominator
effect"/"not accelerated write-offs" reflex on a rising delinquency or
credit-cost print, that would be evidence of a SECTOR-WIDE investor-
relations convention, not a Kissht-specific credibility problem — and
would argue for weighting management's own framing of asset-quality
moves somewhat less heavily across the ENTIRE coverage universe, not
singling out Kissht. Absent that fourth data point, this hypothesis
remains a pattern across three observations, offered as a hypothesis to
test in future runs, not a proven sector law.
