## BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING NARRATIVE

Ceigall builds roads, highways, metro viaducts, tunnels and bridges for
Indian government agencies. That contracting work, EPC, was 57.86 percent of
FY26 revenue, and operations and maintenance was another 0.46 percent. A
second engine, HAM, was 41.68 percent: Ceigall builds a road, puts its own
equity into a project company, and holds a 15 to 20 year concession under
which NHAI pays fixed twice yearly instalments instead of tolls. The customer
cannot easily do without a contractor, because government agencies tender
road work rather than build it, and the tunnel, elevated corridor and hilly
terrain jobs narrow the pool of bidders who qualify. The buyers are named:
NHAI, MoRTH, state public works departments, the Uttar Pradesh and Jaipur
metro corporations, and GMADA. They buy at the lowest qualified price, they
prequalify on technical record and bid capacity, and they pay against
milestone bills certified by their own engineer, so switching costs fall to
near zero once a contract closes and every new road is bid again.

Demand today comes from the national and state road building programme. The
signals that drive it are the NHAI monthly award and construction km
bulletin, the Union Budget MoRTH and NHAI allocation, Bharatmala Pariyojana
Phase II and NIP 2.0 progress reports, state PWD and road development
corporation tenders in Punjab, Bihar and Madhya Pradesh, metro rail
corporation awards from UPMRC and JMRC, the NHAI InvIT and TOT monetisation
announcements that recycle funding back into fresh tendering, and the crude
oil and bitumen price index that sets input cost. Whether that demand grows
is less settled. NHAI awarded about 3,100 km in FY26, down 22 percent year on
year and short of a 7,500 km target, which all three peers attribute to land
acquisition and cabinet approval delay. The order book of Rs 18,554 crore at
4.8 times book to bill gives Ceigall its own visibility regardless, and the
market sizing work leaves 10.1 times revenue headroom on a 9.9 percent
current share of the served market. The same sizing puts the reachable path
at roughly 10 percent implied revenue growth a year, below management's own
minimum 15 percent guide for FY27. Renewables and transmission carry signed
letters of award and power purchase agreements worth Rs 4,343 crore, and
showed 0.0 percent completion on every order book line at 30 June 2026.

Competitive advantage sits in one place only. Standard road EPC has none,
because the lowest bidder sets the price. The specialised structure record in
tunnels and elevated corridors is documented and carries early completion
bonuses, but the emerging moat scan reads it as an execution lead rather than
a structural barrier, since a well capitalised peer can mobilise the same
capability given time. HAM gives exclusivity on one road for one concession
term and nothing across the portfolio; renewables and transmission have no
moat and no revenue yet. The scan scored 4.3 out of 92 and classified the
forward moat as NONE, with research and development and technology absorption
disclosed as nil in both FY25 and FY26, and equipment rented rather than
owned. The run did not establish how much of the 41.68 percent HAM revenue is
construction phase work versus post commissioning annuity income, because
that split is not disclosed anywhere in the filing.

Order book grows, group cash does not

Ceigall is a 24 year old road contractor from Punjab that listed in August
2024. FY26 consolidated revenue was Rs 4,022 crore and profit Rs 312 crore.
The order book stands at Rs 18,554 crore, up 71.7 percent in a year, which is
4.8 times last year's revenue. Two thirds of that book is roads and
highways; the rest is metro, tunnels, and now solar and transmission lines
that have not started. The customers are NHAI, MoRTH, state road agencies and
two metro corporations. There is one promoter, and the company has been
public for two years.

The claimed change is a move from pure contracting to owning the roads it
builds. Ceigall puts equity into project companies, holds 15 to 20 year NHAI
annuity concessions, and then sells the finished asset to recycle the money.
It did this once, selling Malout-Abohar-Sadhawali to Neo Infra in June 2026.
Management calls that the start of a repeatable cycle. The sale price was
never disclosed on any of the four calls, and the annual report does not carry
it either. The second and third planned divestments were promised before
September 2026, pushed to Q2 or Q3 FY27, and then dropped from the August
2026 call entirely. A second claimed change, into solar and transmission,
carries Rs 4,343 crore of letters of award and zero revenue.

The screening numbers put the company at 37 out of 100 on the core quality
score and 45 out of 160 overall, which classifies as AVOID. Two hard rules
fired. Operating cash flow was negative in five of the last six years, and
cumulative operating cash flow against cumulative profit is minus 0.70 times,
while profit rose from Rs 112 crore in FY21 to Rs 312 crore in FY26. That
scored zero out of 20 on the cash block. A second zero, on the ownership
block, is a missing document rather than a finding: no shareholding filing was
in the corpus, so it should not be read as a governance mark. Growth and
returns scored 14 and 15 out of 20 on their own blocks. Five of twelve moat
tests scored zero for want of peer data, so the moderate moat read is
unconfirmed rather than negative.

Cash is the run's central question and the evidence points both ways.
Consolidated operating cash flow was minus Rs 516 crore in FY25 and minus Rs
91 crore in FY26, an 82 percent smaller burn. In the same year the parent
company alone generated Rs 457 crore from operations while lending Rs 314
crore onward into its project companies. That is the mechanism: the
contractor makes cash and the concessions consume it. Against that reading,
Contract Assets, which are EPC revenue booked ahead of billing, rose 61.8
percent from Rs 873 crore to Rs 1,413 crore with no impairment at all, faster
than revenue grew. Add the concession receivable of Rs 1,458 crore and the two
together equal about 71 percent of a full year of revenue. Trade receivables
older than six months went from 9.4 percent of the gross book to 28 percent,
even as the total balance fell 18.8 percent. Payable days went from 79 to 138.
The rating rationale, the one document that normally separates a growth build
from a collection problem, is not in the corpus.

Management delivers on the numbers it guides and stays silent on the numbers
it does not. Revenue growth, order inflow, margin band and standalone
deleveraging were all met or beaten across four calls; FY26 order inflow came
in at Rs 11,332 crore against Rs 5,000 crore guided. Five promises were kept,
two were partly kept, three were missed. Nobody mentioned the Rs 8.97 crore
procurement fraud, the qualified internal financial controls opinion at both
company levels, NHAI's termination of a project company, a subsidiary
auditor's resignation, or contingent liabilities at 83.7 percent of net
worth, on any of the four calls. Consolidated debt disclosure went from a full
breakdown to nothing across those four calls, and consolidated EBITDA and
profit disclosure went dark in the same August 2026 call. In that call
management mentioned, as an aside, that all IPO money is spent and royalty
income has stopped, one quarter before an Rs 859 crore equity commitment on
which Rs 23 crore has been infused. One analyst asked twice whether the margin
beat was a one off and got a list of new projects both times. The credibility
grade is C, at the bottom of the band.

The three road peers confirm the setting and contradict five specific
Ceigall claims. All of them describe FY26 awarding as slow for the same
reasons, all name the West Asia conflict as the driver of bitumen and crude
cost, and all are entering solar, transmission or mining at the same time, so
the diversification push is industry wide rather than a lone bet. HG Infra
missed its own FY26 order inflow guide by 87 percent and said so unprompted,
in the year Ceigall beat its guide 2.3 times. On the contradictions: two peers
disclose enterprise value on completed HAM sales where Ceigall disclosed
nothing; no peer uses a blanket "cash flow is not a problem" framing, all
name the specific driver of each debt move; all three itemise bonus and
royalty income separately rather than folding it into headline margin; two
disclose their own governance trouble unprompted, including a CBI search and
an NHAI show cause with a bidding suspension; and all three describe the
escalation cost relief that Ceigall called full compensation as partial and
time boxed. HG Infra's own Q4 FY26 margin fell to 9.37 percent on unrecovered
escalation in the exact quarter Ceigall printed its best margin of the year.

The market is large enough and the near term arithmetic is tight. The
conservative market estimate is Rs 1.22 lakh crore of national highway spend a
year, growing about 5 percent, with a served market of Rs 40,565 crore of
which Ceigall holds 9.9 percent. That is 10.1 times revenue headroom and a
GOOD runway class. The catch is that the reachable share path implies about 10
percent revenue growth a year at both the three and five year marks, below
management's own 15 percent floor for FY27 and well below what the strategy
requires. Three explanations sit open: the market estimate excludes state
highway, metro and tunnel spend that Ceigall bids into and is understated;
FY26's inflow beat already exceeded normal share gain; or growth increasingly
depends on renewables and transmission, which management credits with 35
percent of FY26 inflow and which had zero revenue one quarter into the year
it was guided to reach 20 to 25 percent of the top line.

Governance carries real evidence on both sides. Nine changes at senior
management or board level happened in about 17 months, including three CEO
level transitions, at a company under two years post listing. An independent
director appointed in February 2025 sits on the board of C&C Constructions,
the outside company holding a Rs 50 crore Ceigall loan that has not moved in
two years. There were two separate compliance events in FY26: the procurement
fraud with an FIR, and a DGGI search over wrongful input tax credit on IPO
expenses, reversed as inadvertent. Set against that, the two newest executive
directors are outside hires with sector records, the internal auditor is Grant
Thornton rather than an in house team, the secretarial auditor disclosed both
compliance events in a dedicated exception annexure instead of filing a clean
report, and the audit committee chairman was reappointed for a second five
year term through the churn. IPO proceeds were fully and verifiably used per
the independent monitoring agency.

One factual question stayed open at the end of phase 1. Two reading passes
over the same dense audit table disagree on which project company carries the
auditor resignation and the two years of cash losses. One pass read Ceigall
Ludhiana Bathinda Greenfield Highway; a full clarity re render read Ceigall
Ludhiana Rupnagar Greenfield Highway, which is also the company whose NHAI
concession was terminated. The answer decides whether this is one troubled
project company or two.
