# PLAIN-LANGUAGE BRIEF (mandatory final narrative)

## 1. Summary narrative
Exicom grew fast and lost money at the group level again. Standalone revenue rose 57 percent to Rs237 Cr and standalone EBITDA doubled to Rs21 Cr at an 8.8 percent margin. On the consolidated basis that includes the US Tritium fast-charging business, revenue rose 61 percent to Rs331 Cr but EBITDA was a loss of Rs21.9 Cr. That number matters more than any other this quarter. In May the operator pre-committed to a single test: consolidated quarterly EBITDA at or above zero was the bull case, below minus Rs20 Cr was the bear case. The print was about minus Rs22 Cr, so this is the bear outcome, and it reversed the small positive Rs0.3 Cr the company had reached in the March quarter. This is a flag for the human, not an automatic decision, and the Decision Status stays WATCHLIST.

The consolidated headline looks better than it is. The PAT loss narrowed to Rs73.6 Cr from Rs83.1 Cr a year ago, but the entire improvement is a Rs12 Cr restructuring charge from last year not repeating. Strip that out and pre-tax profit before exceptional items actually got slightly worse year on year. The standalone return to profit is also thin: the Rs4.9 Cr profit leans on Rs7.3 Cr of other income, and the core business before other income is marginally loss-making at the profit-before-tax line because depreciation and interest eat almost all of the EBITDA. Depreciation jumped because the new Hyderabad plant just switched on, and there is a temporary Rs8.7 Cr cost of running two plants in parallel.

Management framed the margin drop as seasonality plus the running-out of cheap inventory acquired with Tritium. The seasonal part is real; the Tritium part sounds structural, and they did not quantify either. They re-guided consolidated breakeven to the September or December quarter and could not say which. The bright spots are genuine: Tritium order bookings doubled to $20.8 million, the first GRID-FLEX unit is live at a large data-centre customer, and the Hyderabad plant is producing. The worries are also real: India EV revenue growth slowed to 15 percent from the 60 percent seen earlier, the reported net debt of Rs370 Cr does not obviously reconcile to last quarter's figures, and five governance and working-capital questions have now gone unanswered for two straight quarters. The next clean test is the September-quarter consolidated EBITDA print.

## 2. Sector intelligence
Exicom sits in two industries. The first is EV charging in India, both the AC home and destination chargers it makes at scale and the DC fast chargers, plus the US DC-fast-charging business it owns through Tritium. The second is critical and telecom power, which supplies power systems, batteries and enclosures to telecom towers and to the government BharatNet fibre programme. The demand backdrop for both is favourable on paper. India crossed 80,000 electric four-wheeler sales in a quarter for the first time (this quarter, press release), and policy is supportive: Delhi has a four-year Rs15,000 Cr EV policy targeting 30,000-plus charging points (concall, this quarter), several cities now mandate EV-ready buildings, and the central PM E-Drive scheme funds buses and charging. On the telecom side, the shift to 5G, hybrid solar-plus-battery site power, and the BharatNet rollout to smaller towns are the tailwinds (concall, this quarter). The headwind that showed up in the numbers is input cost and currency: consolidated gross margin fell to 31.7 percent from 39.4 percent a year ago, which management attributed to exchange-rate volatility and component-price pressure (press release, this quarter). The sector's structural promise remains intact from the prior Notion thesis (EV Charging / CPaaS), but the quarter is a reminder that policy tailwinds do not automatically become margins.

## 3. Business-model intelligence
Exicom makes money by manufacturing and selling hardware: telecom and data-centre power systems and batteries, AC and DC EV chargers in India, and DC fast chargers plus power-conversion products in the US through Tritium. The standalone Indian business is profitable at the EBITDA line (8.8 percent this quarter, down from 10.6 percent last quarter, prior Notion memory) but only marginally profitable before tax once depreciation and interest are counted, because it is in a heavy capacity-investment phase (the new Hyderabad plant, this quarter's filing and concall). The consolidated business is loss-making because Tritium in the US is running its plant at roughly one-quarter of capacity (concall, this quarter) and carrying large capitalised research costs that will depreciate over the next five to seven years (concall, this quarter). The model works only if two things happen: the Indian plant fills up so its depreciation is absorbed, and Tritium's order bookings (which doubled to $20.8 million, deck and press release, this quarter) convert into revenue that fills the US plant and reaches breakeven, guided to the March 2027 quarter. The metric the filings did not disclose, and that the model turns on, is Tritium's normalised gross margin now that the cheap acquired inventory is gone; that number is the single biggest gap in this quarter's disclosure. Cash conversion is also undisclosed this quarter because Q1 filings carry no cash flow statement, so the cash quality of this growth is unproven until the half-year figures arrive.

## 4. Competition intelligence
Exicom's competitive position is clearest in Indian AC EV charging, where it supplies India's top vehicle makers at scale, this quarter became sole supplier of 7.4 kW units to a leading carmaker, secured 100 percent share of a leading international brand's wallbox business, and holds a historical 60 percent wallbox share from its listing document (prior Notion memory; the current "north of 50 percent" figure was flagged by management as a personal estimate, not researched, and should not be treated as fact). In telecom power it claims about 60 percent wallet share of the BharatNet work with the system integrators it supplies (concall, this quarter). Where Exicom is structurally weaker is US DC fast charging through Tritium: it is a turnaround asset running at low utilisation, its upside is concentrated in one largest customer, one hyperscaler and one large US charging network (deck, this quarter), so a single customer slipping would hurt, and it competes there against established global fast-charging and power-electronics players. The competitive risk to watch is twofold: in India, that EV charging growth decelerating to 15 percent (press release, this quarter) signals larger or cheaper competitors taking share as the market scales; and in the US, that Tritium's trials do not convert to the $40-50 million of CY27 contracts the whole scale story depends on. No listed peer reported a concall within the comparison window, so the peer cross-check this quarter is unavailable and stated as such.
