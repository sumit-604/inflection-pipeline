## 3. THE CATALYST STORY IN PLAIN WORDS

**What could keep revenue growing.** The Samlaya plant in Gujarat is the
concrete one. The company said in the FY26 deck it would start in Q1 FY27, and
it told the exchange on 3 June 2026 that commercial production of polymer
adhesive had partially begun, with the rest expected over six to eight months.
That is a promise made and kept, confirmed by a third party filing rather than
by management describing itself. A second phase adds styrene butadiene rubber
latex aimed at construction chemicals, targeted for completion by the end of
Q3 FY27, and it opens a market the company is not in today. Together the two
phases are Rs 50 crore of brownfield spend for 30,000 tonnes a year of new
capacity. Confirming evidence would be an exchange filing or a deck slide
saying full capacity is running. What kills it is a slip past Q3 FY27 on phase
two, or the plan being quietly cut back.

The softer part is demand. Adhesives grew strongly through FY26 and again in
Q1 FY27, helped by lower goods and services tax rates and wider distribution
across roughly 1,450 distributors and 31,000 retailers. But the claim that
FY26 growth came from market share gains across all businesses could not be
verified, and one claim was contradicted outright: management called global
latex demand subdued in FY26, while Apcotex reported its highest ever export
volumes and profit up 48% to 61% across the same window. Treat the share gain
story with more suspicion than the recent quarterly prints.

**What could lift margins, and what is pushing back.** In favour: the new
plant should cut freight and improve market coverage, the company has launched
several new products, and it says it is working procurement and cost
programmes. Against: no cost programme carries a number or a date anywhere in
the filings, and all four peer companies independently describe the same input
cost spike, with sulphur running from about Rs 27,500 a tonne in April 2025 to
about Rs 1,00,000 a tonne by mid 2026. When every competitor names the same
shock, it is a sector condition, not something this company is beating.
Confirming evidence would be a group margin above 11.5% for two quarters
running. What kills it is another quarter like Q1 FY27.

**What could fix cash conversion.** Essentially one thing, and it is
structural rather than operational: the demerger. The board approved moving the
whole agri division into Jubilant Agri Solutions on 4 November 2025, the
exchanges cleared it on 17 April 2026, the National Company Law Tribunal
allowed the first motion on 8 July 2026, and shareholders and creditors meet on
5 September 2026. When it completes, the fertilizer subsidy receivable that
caused the problem leaves with the business that generated it, and the residual
company is renamed Jubilant Industries.

What is absent matters as much. There is no receivables factoring facility, no
disclosed debtor day target, and no working capital programme with a number
attached. The one working capital promise management did make, that agri
inventory would be sold down by Q1 FY27, was missed, and the replacement
guidance is that working capital will simply stay high. That is a promise
broken and then withdrawn rather than rescheduled, and it earns the extra
discount the protocol applies to management that has missed before.

**What could keep capital efficiency high.** The demerger again, from the other
side: it removes a segment earning 19.8% from a group earning 36%, which lifts
what remains. Samlaya moving from money spent to money earning does the same.
Pushing back, the company put 92.8% of FY26 capital spending into the retained
polymer segment and the polymer capital base jumped about 24% in the first
quarter of FY27, so there is a real lag before that spending shows up in the
ratio.

**Where management said things and did things.** Running the intent and action
ledger across the four transitions, the pattern is consistent. On revenue and
capital efficiency there is documented action, Samlaya commissioned on the date
promised and capital spending concentrated in the surviving business, and the
verdicts already reflect it. On margin there is talk of cost programmes with no
documented programme behind it. On cash there is a documented action, the
demerger, but it reallocates the problem to a separate listed entity rather
than solving it, and alongside it sits a missed promise and explicit guidance
that working capital stays high. So the ledger confirms the two conservative
verdicts rather than loosening either. Nothing here was too harsh.

## 4. THE VERDICT

**Composite 3 out of 8, which is DEEP WATCH. Wait for confirmation.**

Capital efficiency scores +2 and revenue +1, while margin and cash both score
zero, and that is the whole story: a genuinely excellent small business earning
36% on capital with almost no debt, attached to a fertilizer operation that
generated all of last year's profit growth and all of last year's cash problem,
and that is in the process of leaving. Neither the Kernex cap nor the trim rule
engages, so the score stands on its own arithmetic.

The call turns on one thing: the first set of accounts for the retained polymer
business on its own, after the demerger takes effect. Everything attractive
here is a segment level number that has never been reported as a standalone
company, and everything worrying is about to be moved into a different one.
Until that print exists you are underwriting a 67.5% return on capital that no
audited entity has ever published.

Confidence is reduced twice over: there are no earnings calls at all, so
management has never been questioned on any of this, and the credit rating in
the folder predates the entire cash conversion problem it would otherwise help
explain.
