## NEW-1 🔴 Standalone FY26 operating cash flow was NEGATIVE ₹3,229.65 lakh

Standalone Statement of Cash Flows (p.70–71; Consolidated CFS p.112–113, identical): Operating profit before working capital changes was ₹5,816.48 lakh (FY26) vs ₹4,284.33 lakh (FY25) — the accrual story looks fine. But working-capital movements flip it: (Increase) in trade receivables ₹(5,004.39) lakh, (Increase) in Inventories ₹(5,220.59) lakh, against only a partial offset from Increase in trade payables ₹1,826.22 lakh. Cash generated from operations was ₹(2,097.50) lakh (FY26) vs +₹1,377.10 lakh (FY25). After direct taxes paid ₹(1,132.14) lakh, Net cash from operating activities (A) = ₹(3,229.65) lakh (FY26) vs +₹821.03 lakh (FY25).

This is the first hard, quantified confirmation of the cash-conversion crisis Pass 1 inferred qualitatively (from receivables ageing and turnover ratios). The company's accrual profit grew (PBT +66%) while actual operating cash flow swung from positive to a ₹32.3 Cr outflow in the same year.

## NEW-2 🔴 The operating and investing cash burn was funded almost entirely by the warrant issue and fresh short-term debt, not by organic cash generation

Standalone/Consolidated CFS (p.70–71/112–113): Net cash used in investing activities (B) = ₹(4,477.75) lakh, mostly capex (Purchase of PP&E ₹(3,089.06) lakh) and bank deposits ₹(1,079.79) lakh. Combined with the ₹(3,229.65) lakh operating outflow (NEW-1), total cash consumed by the business before financing was roughly ₹77 Cr. This was covered by Net cash from financing activities (C) = +₹7,292.08 lakh, made up almost entirely of Proceeds from issue of share warrants ₹6,125.35 lakh and net Proceeds from short-term borrowings ₹2,879.50 lakh. Net cash actually fell ₹(415.33) lakh for the year despite the ₹61.25 Cr equity raise. Without the preferential warrant issue, FY26 would have needed a much larger draw on bank lines to stay solvent through the year — the capital raise timing is directly load-bearing for FY26 liquidity, not a discretionary growth-funding choice.

## NEW-3 🟡 A second wholly-owned subsidiary (US) exists at balance sheet date but is absent from every audited financial note

Board's Report Section 19 (p.50) and AOC-1 (Annexure II, p.60): Vinyas Innovative Technologies Inc., incorporated in the USA on 3 February 2026, is a second 100%-owned subsidiary alongside Vinyas Technologies Pvt Ltd. As at 31 March 2026 it "had neither commenced business operations nor completed the Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) formalities... Accordingly, its financial statements have not been consolidated." This entity does not appear anywhere in the audited Notes: not in standalone Note 5.1 Investments (only the Indian subsidiary's ₹1.00 lakh investment is shown), not in Note 33 Related Party relationships (only Vinyas Technologies Pvt Ltd is named as "Subsidiary Company"), and not in the consolidated Note 39 Schedule III enterprise table (Parent + Vinyas Technologies Pvt Ltd only). Its existence surfaces only in the Board's Report and AOC-1, both non-audited-note sections. Given the MD&A's "Expansion into the United States" is presented as a FY26 strategic highlight (p.39), the total absence of the entity from the financial notes (even as a disclosed-but-unconsolidated investment) is a disclosure gap worth resolving before crediting the international-expansion narrative.

## NEW-4 🟡 The Financial Risk Management note promises foreign-currency risk disclosure and then omits it

Note 30 (standalone p.98–99 / consolidated p.138–139): the note explicitly states "Market risk comprises three types of risk: interest rate risk, foreign currency risk and other price risk," then provides a full qualitative discussion only of Interest Rate Risk ("the Group is not significantly exposed to interest rate risk" since debt is floating-rate and investments are floating too) before moving straight to Note 31 Capital Management. No "Foreign Currency Risk" subsection follows. This sharpens Pass 1's FX finding (Top 10, #4): it is not merely that FX exposure goes unquantified — the company's own risk-management framework names FX risk as a category it will address and then structurally skips it, on a name where 70–75% of raw material is imported per company memory.

## NEW-5 🟡 Payables financing mechanics: Buyer's Credit and bill discounting sit behind the "faster payables turnover" red flag

Note 14.3 Other Financial Liabilities (consolidated p.132; standalone balance sheet total ₹2,907.91 lakh, FY25 ₹2,767.73 lakh) includes a discrete "Buyer Credit & Other Payables ₹2,598.54 lakh" line — buyer's credit is a bank-intermediated, typically USD-denominated trade-finance instrument for imports. Cross-referenced against Note 23 Finance Costs (consolidated p.134), which shows "Interest on purchase bill discounting ₹62.46 lakh" as a distinct finance-cost line, this indicates the company is using bank-financed payables instruments (buyer's credit / bill discounting), not organic cash, to pay suppliers on the accelerated schedule that drove Trade Payables Turnover from 17.50x to 28.66x (Pass 1 finding #2). This reframes that finding: the "faster payment to suppliers" is partly financed, not evidence of stronger self-funded liquidity, and both instruments (buyer's credit especially) carry embedded FX exposure that compounds the disclosure gap in NEW-4.

## NEW-6 🟡 GST input credit more than doubled, a second independent balance-sheet trace of the inventory/purchase build

Note 9 Other Current Assets (standalone p.85; consolidated p.126): GST Input rose to ₹1,329.52 lakh (FY26) from ₹525.87 lakh (FY25), +152.8% — an incremental ₹8.0 Cr of working capital tied up in unclaimed/unset-off input tax credit. This tracks Note 20 Cost of Materials Consumed (consolidated p.133), which shows Raw Material purchases of ₹45,211.88 lakh during the year (~88% of revenue), and independently corroborates Pass 1's raw-material-build red flag (Top 10 #3) via a different note.

## NEW-7 🟡 A separate "Amortization of Other Assets" P&L line (₹70.40 lakh) reflects a policy of capitalising tooling costs rather than expensing them

Note 24 Depreciation and Amortisation Expense (consolidated p.134) shows: PPE depreciation ₹615.71 lakh, ROU amortisation ₹17.50 lakh, and "Amortization of Other Assets ₹70.40 lakh" — a category not discussed in Pass 1. This ties to accounting policy Note 2.4/3.4 (standalone p.74/76; consolidated p.117): "Stencils and tools have been classified as current assets and amortization is provided as per the amortization policy... at 20% considering useful life of 5 years and scrap value as Nil." Tooling costs for a build-to-spec EMS manufacturer are being spread over five years rather than expensed as incurred or through COGS — a policy choice that smooths costs over multiple periods. Not improper, but it is an earnings-smoothing mechanism worth watching for consistency as tooling spend scales with the order book.

## NEW-8 🟡 A discrete "Liquidated Damages ₹74.32 lakh" line sits inside Other Expenses

Note 25 Other Expenses (consolidated p.135): among the FY26 expense breakdown, "Liquidated Damages ₹74.32 lakh" (₹0.74 Cr) appears as its own line, not folded into miscellaneous expenses. Liquidated damages are contractual penalties typically levied for late or non-conforming delivery. No prior-year comparator or explanatory narrative is given in the note, so a trend cannot be established, but the amount is large enough to be broken out separately, and it sits at a company whose investment case depends on reliable execution for defence/aerospace/medical customers with "long qualification cycles" and "stringent customer approvals" (MD&A p.40–41). This is an execution-risk indicator Pass 1 did not surface.

## NEW-9 🟡 Deposits securing bank guarantees are not disclosed as earmarked, despite bank guarantees growing 84%

Note 8.2 Cash and Bank Balance (standalone p.85; consolidated p.126): Deposit accounts with maturity 3–12 months grew from ₹626.85 lakh to ₹1,706.64 lakh, +172%, in the same year Outstanding Bank Guarantees (Note 36) grew 84% to ₹3,568.30 lakh. Banks typically require margin-money deposits against guarantees issued. Yet the note's "In earmarked accounts (with original maturity period of less than 12 months)" line is shown as nil/blank in both years. The AR does not disclose whether any portion of the ₹17.07 Cr deposit balance is lien-marked against the ₹35.68 Cr of bank guarantees. If a material share is pledged, the company's apparent liquid/free cash position is smaller than the balance sheet total suggests — this is a disclosure absence, not a confirmed pledge, and should be verified directly with the company.

## NEW-10 🟡 The pattern of ECL-reducing entries on trade receivables is not new to FY26 — it recurs from FY25

Standalone CFS FY25 comparative column (p.70) shows a "Bad debts written off ₹17.47 lakh" non-cash add-back — a figure that exactly equals the FY25 closing ECL allowance balance from Note 8.1(e). Note 8.1(e)'s own FY25 movement shows a separate ₹26.89 lakh reversal that year (opening ₹44.36 lakh to closing ₹17.47 lakh). Net effect on FY25 P&L from receivables provisioning was a ₹9.42 lakh net credit (26.89 reversal less 17.47 write-off), before FY26 added a further ₹14.59 lakh reversal (Pass 1 finding #1). This shows generous/provision-reducing treatment of receivables is a two-year pattern, not a single-year anomaly, even as the gross book has grown from ₹17,722.78 lakh to ₹22,712.57 lakh over the same period.

## NEW-11 🟢 Minor: a second internal headcount inconsistency in the same AR

Corporate Overview "Workforce Proportion" donut chart (p.29) shows Male Employees 252 / Female Employees 161 (61%/39% of 413 total) as at the reporting date. Board's Report Section 41 "Gender-wise Composition of Employees" (p.54) states Male Employees 251, Female Employees 162, for the same 31 March 2026 date. A one-person swing is immaterial financially, but it is a second instance (after the MSME note's "six months period ended 31 March 2025" drafting error already flagged in Pass 1, Section 8) of numbers not being cross-checked across sections of the same document — a mild but repeated proofing-quality signal.
