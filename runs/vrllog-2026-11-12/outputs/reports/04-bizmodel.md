## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A One line
VRL picks up small parcels from about 10 lakh customers, packs them together on its own 5,932 trucks through a hub and spoke network of 1,337 branches, and gets paid mostly in cash at booking or delivery (AR p.77, p.82; Inv. Pres. p.22, p.25).

### 1E Chai-stall-uncle version
Think of VRL as a big bus service for parcels instead of people. Your small box goes on a truck with many other boxes, so no one pays for a whole truck. VRL owns the trucks and the bus stations, so it does not rent them from others. Most people pay when they hand over the box or when the receiver takes it, so VRL does not wait for money. The money is made by filling every truck and by buying diesel and spare parts cheaply. The weak point is that the trucks, stations and drivers cost money even when boxes are fewer, and diesel costs keep moving.

### 2D Industry lifecycle
Road logistics is in a growth and formalisation stage, not mature: GST and e-invoicing push shippers to organised carriers, and the AR says organised players "stand to benefit at the expense of smaller players" (AR p.80-82). The industry is valued at over USD 240 bn, road carries 60-65% of freight (AR p.76). VRL itself is mature in network (50 years, 1,337 branches) and is in a margin-reset and re-expansion phase: pruned low-margin contracts in FY25-FY26, re-adding branches (97 in FY26, 67 in H1 FY27) (AR p.82; Inv. Pres. p.16).

- Operating leverage runs both ways. In FY26 tonnage fell 7% and EBITDA still rose because price rose 9.6%. A price cut with flat cost would hurt in the same ratio. A 1% freight rate change equals about Rs 3.2 Cr of quarterly EBITDA at Q2 revenue (961.9 x 1%, before cost response, DERIVED).
