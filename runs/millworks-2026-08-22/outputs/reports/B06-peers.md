## PART 2: UNPROMPTED CROSS-READ

**2A Demand environment.** All three peers describe a broadly constructive demand backdrop by mid-2026, following a rough patch. Unimech's H1 FY26 was depressed by US tariff shocks (50%→18% reduction referenced repeatedly, Feb 2026 p.2-3) with clear sequential recovery by Q4 FY26/Q1 FY27 (order book Rs210cr → Rs314cr → Rs280cr, revenue growth 71% YoY in Q1 FY27, Aug 2026 p.2). Airfloa and Apsis show consistent order-book growth throughout without a comparable dip (Airfloa order book Rs405cr→Rs486.9cr; Apsis Rs24cr→Rs40.5cr). No peer contradicts a sector tailwind narrative, but Unimech's experience is a reminder that export-exposed peers (95% export at Unimech) carry tariff-cycle risk that a domestically concentrated name like Millworks may not share directly.

**2B Pricing and input costs.** Airfloa and Apsis both cite geopolitically-linked raw-material inflation compressing margins: Airfloa's EBITDA margin fell from 25% to 20.1% in FY26, explicitly attributed to aluminium (+80%) and stainless steel (+60-65%) price increases "because of the war that has been happening" (Jun 2026, p.12-13); Apsis cites "geopolitical uncertainties, supply-chain disruptions and foreign-exchange fluctuations" as a factor in its EBITDA margin moderating from 49% to 37% (Jun 2026, p.4). Unimech's margin pressure in FY26 instead traces to US tariffs and fixed-cost under-absorption during the low-revenue quarters, not input-cost inflation. This is a real, peer-corroborated geopolitical channel (input costs), but it is a different channel from the one Millworks' claim package focuses on (receivable days), and it does not appear in the injected question set for Millworks at all — a gap worth flagging to synthesis.

**2C Capex cycle.** This is an industry-wide capacity race, not a lone-expander situation. Unimech (Rs450cr Hobel acquisition, $30mn Saudi JV, board resolution to raise up to Rs750cr), Airfloa (new 14-acre facility, debt-funded Rs120cr at 8.25%), and Apsis (Rs60cr Unit 2, Unit 3 land already identified) are all expanding simultaneously and aggressively through FY26-FY27. Millworks' capex commitment sits inside a genuine sector-wide investment wave across aerospace, defence and rail small/micro-caps, which somewhat de-risks the "isolated capex bet" framing but also means Millworks is competing for the same skilled labour, machinery lead times, and customer qualification slots as three well-funded peers simultaneously.

**2D Competitive mentions.** No peer mentions Millworks by name or unmistakable description in any of the 7 transcripts. Unimech names MTAR as a nuclear-space competitor and explicitly states it has no domestic defence presence "as of now" (Feb 2026, p.11); Airfloa names Kineco, Hindustan Fiberglass Works, DTL Ancillaries, Vibgyor, Universal Engineers, Tata and Fainsa as competitors (Jun 2026, p.9-10), none of them Millworks. This silence is not informative either way given none of the peers appear to operate in Millworks' specific niche.

**2E Risks peers discuss that Millworks' claim package does not raise.**
- US tariff whiplash risk (50%→18%, and a fresh 100% tariff threat raised by an analyst in Aug 2026, p.16) — material for Unimech's 90%+ export book, largely inapplicable to a domestically defence-concentrated name but a reminder that trade policy volatility is live in this sector.
- Geopolitically-linked raw material cost inflation (aluminium/steel, Airfloa; broad commodity/FX, Apsis) — not addressed in any of the six injected Millworks questions.
- China-sourced capital equipment/export-control risk: Airfloa's capex was delayed because the Chinese government began requiring Indian end-user certificates for high-precision machinery (Jun 2026, p.20) — a supply-chain risk class not present in the Millworks question set.
- Proactive customer-concentration de-risking as a disclosed KPI: Apsis reduced its top-customer share from 52% to 35% and top-5 from 94% to 87% during FY26, and discloses this openly as a strategic priority (Jun 2026, p.4). This is a useful benchmark for how a comparable small-cap frames concentration risk transparently, in contrast to the opacity around the Quick Pay relationship at Millworks.
- Funding-model risk during rapid growth: Airfloa is running negative operating/free cash flow and funding growth via Rs120cr bank debt at 8.25% (Jun 2026, p.7-8, p.11) — a leverage risk class for fast-growing small-caps in this space not covered by the Millworks question set.

## PART 5: CROSS-PEER HYPOTHESIS

Across the three peers, capex timing relative to capacity utilization splits cleanly into two patterns. Unimech — the most diversified platform-builder across aero tooling, nuclear, semiconductor, energy and now bellows/tubing via Hobel — committed to its largest capex/M&A decisions (Rs450cr Hobel acquisition, $30mn Saudi JV) while utilization sat at just 50-60%, betting on qualification-pipeline conversion (FAI counts, SKU counts) rather than realized demand. Airfloa and Apsis — both narrower, more concentrated specialists with disclosed near-monopoly positions on specific components ("hardly one or two competitors in India," Apsis, Jun 2026 p.13; Unit 1 "completely utilised," same call) — waited until utilization hit 85-100% before committing new capex, and funded it via debt (Airfloa) or IPO proceeds already in hand (Apsis) rather than speculative expansion.

This suggests a testable, sector-specific hypothesis: in this Indian aerospace/defence/rail small-cap cohort, capex-ahead-of-utilization behavior correlates with breadth of platform ambition rather than with sector tailwind strength alone — diversifying "platform" builders invest ahead of proven utilization because their moat argument rests on qualification breadth and customer stickiness across multiple end markets, while niche/near-monopoly component specialists invest only at or near full utilization because their moat argument rests on scarcity and qualification barriers that a premature capacity build would not by itself validate or protect. If this holds, Millworks' own capex-to-utilization posture (72-77% utilization ahead of the Rs61.03cr machinery spend, per the peer question) places it closer to the platform-builder pattern than the niche-specialist pattern — a signal stage 11 valuation should weigh when assessing whether Millworks' moat argument is closer to Unimech's (breadth) or Apsis's (scarcity), since the two patterns carry different capital-efficiency and execution-risk profiles.
