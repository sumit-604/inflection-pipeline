## PART 1: CLAIM-BY-CLAIM VERIFICATION

### Claim 1 — Organised e-commerce mattress market size (~Rs2,000cr, 15-20% growth) and SFL ~30% share
**Verdict: UNVERIFIABLE**

Peer evidence: Wakefit never cites a market-size figure in rupees for the organised e-commerce
mattress segment. On its Feb-2026 (Q3 FY26) call, when directly asked about market share,
management stated: "in the absence of any industry reports, which give us quarter-on-quarter
updates on how growth has happened or share has changed, unlike in FMCG or mobile phones, we
only have an estimate. Our estimate is that we must be in the early double digits in terms of
market share in the overall organized mattress market" (C. Ramalingegowda, WAKEFIT Q3 FY26).
On the online-only cut specifically: "it will be more than a quarter of the market belonging to
Wakefit, although we don't have an exact number given the absence of industry reports" (same
call). No rupee-crore market-size figure, no 15-20% growth rate, and no comment on SFL's implied
~30% share is offered anywhere in either Wakefit call.

Peers silent: Centuryply and Greenlam do not operate in mattresses and were not expected to
speak to this (check_peers list was Wakefit-only; confirmed correctly scoped).

Net read: This is not simply an evidence gap — Wakefit's own admission that "we only have an
estimate" in the absence of industry reports is itself informative. It suggests any precise
market-size or share figure (SFL's or Wakefit's) in this segment is an internal estimate dressed
as fact, not independently verifiable. Confidence in SFL's ~Rs2,000cr/~30%-share framing should
be treated as management's internal estimate, not a market-sourced number.

### Claim 2 — TDI/polyol raw-material shock and 25-35% pass-through amid the Middle East war
**Verdict: PARTIALLY VERIFIED** (direction and timing strongly corroborated; magnitude diverges)

Peer evidence:
- WAKEFIT (Q4 FY26, May-2026 call): "The March quarter saw sharp inflation across several input
  categories with select raw materials such as the chemicals which get used like the polyol,
  TDI, etcetera, registering price increases ranging from 30% to 150%" (Parul Gupta, CFO). On
  pass-through: "Between February and May, the prices have seen a lot of volatility... we are
  operating at a 25% to 30% increase in some raw materials and about 15% to 20% increase in
  some raw materials... to pass this on, we have done two measured calibrated price increases of
  about 7% to 8% in March and 7% to 8% in April" — roughly 15% cumulative pass-through
  (C. Ramalingegowda). Management explicitly flags TDI/polyol by name, matching SFL's claim
  almost exactly on chemistry and timing (Q4 FY26/Q1 FY27).
- CENTURYPLY (Q4 FY26, May-2026 call): "During the quarter, the industry witnessed inflationary
  pressure in certain chemicals and resin-related input costs due to ongoing geopolitical
  conflicts and supply chain disruptions in global markets" (Sanjay Agarwal, opening remarks).
  Sumant Wattas (MDF/PB CEO): "we and the industry have taken a 15% price increase, which barely
  actually covers the cost that has gone up on account of chemicals disruption." Nikita Bansal
  (plywood): "we have taken a 7% price rise already in the month of April due to these reasons
  because we are so import heavy as well as our chemicals... You must be reading about phenol
  and melamine in the papers anyway." Vishu Goel (laminates): a further 10-12% price increase to
  cover chemical costs. Sanjay Agarwal's closing remark — "With Iran War supposedly ending, I
  hope progress there will be good" — confirms this is the same Middle East/Iran-linked
  geopolitical shock SFL's calls reference.
- GREENLAM: the most recent transcript available (Jan-2026, Q3/9M FY26 call) predates the acute
  phase of the shock that Wakefit and Centuryply describe in their May-2026 calls. Greenlam's
  Jan-2026 commentary on "geopolitical issues" affecting resin actually concludes the opposite at
  that point in time: "chemical costs have more or less been -- chemical and the output of resin
  more or less been stable despite the rupee depreciation" (Saurabh Mittal). This is not a
  contradiction of the later shock — it is a earlier data point taken before the spike — but it
  means Greenlam contributes no corroboration for the acute Q1 FY27 phase because no Greenlam
  call covering March-May 2026 was provided.

Peers silent: Greenlam on the acute (March-May 2026) phase specifically, for the reason above.

Net read: Two independent peers in different sub-segments (mattress D2C, wood panels/MDF/
laminates) corroborate the same war-driven chemical/resin cost shock in the same quarter as SFL
— strong triangulation on direction, timing and root cause. But the pass-through magnitude SFL
reports (25-35%) is notably higher than both Wakefit's (~15% cumulative, March+April) and
Centuryply's (~15% MDF, 7-12% plywood/laminates). This gap is worth flagging to synthesis: either
SFL's mattress-foam input mix is genuinely more chemical-intensive than peers' wood/resin mix
(plausible, since TDI/polyol is a much larger share of foam COGS than of plywood/laminate COGS),
or SFL's stated pass-through figure is optimistic relative to what comparable branded players
have actually achieved in the same window.

### Claim 3 — Market-share shift from unorganised to organised, and whether peers cite third-party data SFL does not
**Verdict: VERIFIED** (direction, via three independent peers) — but the "cited third-party data" sub-claim is effectively CONTRADICTED (no peer cites one either)

Peer evidence:
- WAKEFIT (Q3 FY26, Feb-2026): "We believe that a lot of the transition from offline
  unorganized buying will move to online organized buying because the consumer behavior itself
  has changed in these last 10 years" (C. Ramalingegowda). Repeated in the Q4 FY26 (May-2026)
  call in response to a direct question on whether the price-hike gap between organised and
  unorganised widens the shift: "that is the overall benefit, which is structural movement from
  unorganized to organized. We cannot take any credit for that" (same speaker).
- CENTURYPLY: repeatedly across calls. Aug-2025 (Q1 FY26): "the ongoing formalization of the
  sector aided by BIS implementation and stricter environmental regulations is anticipated to
  benefit organized players, leading to consolidation in the market" (Sanjay Agarwal). Nov-2025
  (Q2 FY26): "we need to stop looking at [real estate] cycles... we need to start concentrating
  more towards capturing the unorganized to organized [shift]. And if that is going to be the
  focus, the growth is going to be there irrespective of the real estate cycle" (Nikita Bansal).
  Feb-2026 (Q3 FY26): Keshav Bhajanka estimates organised plywood share moved from "4%, 4.5%"
  pre-COVID to "around 8%, 8.5%" now — an internal estimate, explicitly caveated as such.
- GREENLAM (Aug-2025 analyst meet): independent director Jalaj Dani, in closing remarks: "the
  shift from unorganized to organized. I think buying from a branded player definitely has
  happened in the last few years. And Greenlam stands out as far as quality is concerned."
  Management separately cites QCO/BIS implementation as a driver of the same shift.

Peers silent: none on the general direction — all three engage with it unprompted.

Net read: The unorganised-to-organised narrative is the best-corroborated claim in this set —
three independent peers across two adjacent categories (mattress D2C, wood panels/laminates)
describe the same structural shift, driven by similar mechanisms (QCO/BIS-style quality
standards, changing consumer research behaviour, e-commerce/online discovery). However, not one
of the three peers cites third-party market-tracking data either — Centuryply explicitly says
"there is no authenticated data alignment, it is all on the basis of estimates" (Keshav Bhajanka,
Q1 FY26 call) and Wakefit says the same. This means SFL's uncited U2O claim is standard industry
practice, not a distinguishing red flag versus peers — worth softening the severity of that
observation in synthesis, even though the underlying shift itself is well corroborated.

### Claim 4 — Competitive discipline on pricing/promotions: improved, or still heavy discounting?
**Verdict: CONTRADICTED**

Peer evidence: WAKEFIT (Q3 FY26, Feb-2026): "In the recent past, we have seen some competitive
intensity in the category. And hence, we expect that in the medium term, we anticipate some
ramp-up in our brand building efforts with the advertisement and promotion" (Parul Gupta).
WAKEFIT (Q4 FY26, May-2026), directly on point: "the competition intensity has definitely gone
up over the last six to nine months, where certain companies in the INR100 crores to INR300
crores annual revenue with a much lower cost structure, although they do not have a recognized
brand per se, they might be very well-respected labels on marketplaces or they might be strong
in specific two or three regions. So there, we definitely have seen increased advertising
spending as well as increased matching of prices similar to Wakefit. So both of these have been
accelerated over the last six to nine months" (C. Ramalingegowda). An unprompted industry
teardown from an analyst on the same call (Pranay Roop Chatterjee) reinforces this: a 10-year
study of Sleepwell, Kurlon, Duroflex, Wakefit, Sleep Company, Springwell and Peps found "the
overall gross profit pool and the EBITDA pool in margin terms has actually deteriorated sharply
over the last 10 years... in line with the heightened competitive intensity," with newer D2C
entrants ("Sleepwell company" as named by the analyst — likely The Sleep Company, a newer D2C
brand, distinct from SFL's Sleepwell line which is listed separately in the same sentence)
already "half your size" of Wakefit within a few years of existence.

Peers silent: Centuryply and Greenlam were not asked to check this (check_peers list was
Wakefit-only; correctly scoped, as promotional discounting dynamics are mattress/D2C-specific).

Net read: This is a direct contradiction of SFL's implicit thesis that competitive intensity
moderates for better-funded, listed peers post-IPO. Wakefit's own management states the opposite
has happened over the exact period SFL's calls cover — intensity and discounting/price-matching
have both increased, not decreased, over the last two to three quarters. This is the single most
consequential finding in this stage (see Part 4) because it bears directly on SFL's ASP
premiumisation and margin-durability narrative.

### Claim 5 — Capex intensity / capacity utilisation and SFL's "2-2.5x spare capacity, minimal capex through FY27" claim
**Verdict: PARTIALLY VERIFIED / MIXED** — weight-adjusted for segment mismatch

Peer evidence:
- WAKEFIT (mattress/D2C, closest comp): capex is retail-store led, not manufacturing-led:
  "most of this capex is going to be in retail, not for any manufacturing capacity increase...
  this will include nearly 60%, 70% of the jumbo's initial capex" (C. Ramalingegowda, Q4 FY26).
  FY27 capex guided at Rs120-140cr, almost entirely stores/Jumbo format, consistent in spirit
  with "minimal new production capex" — though the underlying reason differs (Wakefit is
  asset-light by store-led design, not because of spare manufacturing capacity from an
  acquisition, as SFL's Kurlon-driven framing implies).
- CENTURYPLY (adjacent, not a direct comp — wood panels): the opposite pattern. Plywood running
  at 91-99% utilisation across multiple quarters, MDF at 80-85%, both with large, continuing
  capex programmes (Hoshiarpur, Chennai, Uttar Pradesh MDF/plywood, ~Rs2,000cr multi-year). "We
  are utilizing our capacity at practically 100%... that's why we are expanding our capacities
  into our existing plants also" (Sanjay Agarwal, Q4 FY26, May-2026).
- GREENLAM (adjacent, not a direct comp): same pattern — laminates at 83-96% utilisation across
  the three calls, with a further 2-line brownfield expansion at Naidupeta (~Rs375-400cr revenue
  potential) already announced and under construction; Rs1,450cr already invested over the prior
  three years in new capacity.

Peers silent: none, but the two most capex-intensive peers (Centuryply, Greenlam) sit in a
different end-product category (wood panels vs mattress/foam), so their capacity race is not a
direct segment match for SFL's spare-capacity claim.

Net read: The closest business comp (Wakefit) is directionally consistent with low near-term
manufacturing capex, but for a different structural reason than SFL's claim. The two peers that
are actually capital-intensive manufacturers (Centuryply, Greenlam) are in the opposite
situation — running near-full utilisation and actively racing to add capacity — which weakens
confidence that "abundant spare capacity requiring minimal capex" is a broad building-materials/
home-furnishings industry pattern rather than an SFL-specific (post-Kurlon-merger) situation.
This belongs in the capex-cycle cross-read (2C) as much as in claim verification.

### Claim 6 — Online-vs-offline price-hike asymmetry (offline hikes taken broadly, online lagging)
**Verdict: UNVERIFIABLE**

Peer evidence: Wakefit's May-2026 (Q4 FY26) discussion of its 15% cumulative price hike frames
it as an industry-wide, largely channel-agnostic move: "if we were the only company to have
taken a price increase, probably the impact would have been much higher just for us. However,
given that it was an industry-wide impact, pretty much all of the industry has also taken price
increases commensurate to the increase in raw material prices. So net-net, for the consumer, it
has gone up almost uniformly within a 1% or 2%, 3% difference between all of the major brands"
(C. Ramalingegowda). This addresses brand-to-brand uniformity, not channel (online vs offline)
sequencing or lag. No point in either Wakefit call specifically separates online-channel pricing
timing from offline-channel pricing timing during the RM shock.

Peers silent: Centuryply and Greenlam were not asked to check this (correctly scoped as
mattress/D2C-specific).

Net read: No peer evidence either confirms or refutes the specific online-lag claim. Should not
be upgraded to verified on plausibility grounds — it remains a checkable, SFL-specific assertion
that this peer set could not settle.

## PART 2: UNPROMPTED CROSS-READ

### 2A Demand environment
No peer describes an unambiguous demand boom in the same window SFL's calls cover (Q2-Q4 FY26 /
Q1 FY27). Centuryply is the strongest performer of the three — broad-based growth across
plywood/MDF/laminates/particle board through FY26, record quarterly revenues each quarter, and
management describes demand as "quite stable" even after a 15% price hike in Q1 FY27 (May-2026
call). Wakefit reports softer H2 FY26 conditions explicitly: "the second half of the FY '26
witnessed certain headwinds that impacted overall growth momentum. Following softer
discretionary spending trends in Q3 FY26... demand moderation continued further in the latter
half of the Q4 FY26 too amid evolving geopolitical and macroeconomic uncertainty" (Ankit Garg,
May-2026 call). Greenlam's Jan-2026 (Q3 FY26) call is the softest: "demand is just generally a
bit slow and there are cash flow challenges" reported anecdotally from the field (Saurabh
Mittal), alongside a revenue guidance shortfall for the quarter. Net: the peer set shows a mixed-
to-softening demand backdrop into Q4 FY26/Q1 FY27, not an industry-wide demand boom — this is
worth weighing against any unqualified demand-strength framing in SFL's own calls.

### 2B Pricing and input costs
Consistent picture across all three peers: timber/wood-based inputs were comparatively stable to
softening through FY26 (Centuryply notes timber "more or less at similar levels" through most of
the year, with a temporary Punjab flood-driven spike in Q2 FY26 that reversed; Greenlam similarly
describes wood costs as "stable or slightly softening"), while chemical/resin-linked inputs
(TDI, polyol, phenol, melamine) spiked sharply from around February-March 2026 onward, tied to
the same geopolitical (Iran/Middle East) conflict SFL's calls reference. This matches SFL's own
margin narrative closely on the "which input, when" question, even though (per Claim 2) the
pass-through percentage SFL reports is higher than what Wakefit and Centuryply report achieving.

### 2C Capex cycle
This is a genuine lone-situation-vs-industry-pattern question, and the answer is mixed by
segment. Wakefit (mattress/D2C, the closest comp) is in a low-capex, asset-light retail-expansion
mode. Centuryply and Greenlam (adjacent wood-panel/laminate makers, not direct comps) are both
running nearly all major product lines at 80-99%+ utilisation and are in the middle of, or
about to begin, large multi-year capacity-race capex programmes (Centuryply: Hoshiarpur,
Chennai, and a new ~Rs1,100cr Uttar Pradesh MDF+plywood complex; Greenlam: a ~Rs1,450cr
three-year investment cycle just completed, with further brownfield lines already announced).
This is a capacity race in the adjacent building-materials category, not a lone-expander
situation — worth naming explicitly per the prompt's framing, while flagging that it is not a
same-segment comparison to SFL's spare-capacity claim.

### 2D Competitive mentions of the main company
No peer names Sheela Foam, Furlenco, or the SFL entity directly. However, one Wakefit-call
analyst (Pranay Roop Chatterjee, Burman Capital Management, Q4 FY26/May-2026 call) explicitly
lists "Sleepwell, Kurlon, Duroflex, Wakefit, Sleep Company, Springwell, Peps" in a comparative
10-year industry profitability study, concluding the sector's combined EBITDA pool has
deteriorated sharply over a decade of intensifying competition and new-entrant D2C brands. This
is a high-value, unprompted third-party characterisation of the industry SFL's Sleepwell and
Kurlon brands compete in (note: the "Sleepwell company" the analyst separately names alongside
Kurlon and Wakefit appears most likely to be a distinct newer D2C brand — The Sleep Company — not
SFL's own Sleepwell line, since both are listed as separate names in the same sentence; this
ambiguity should be resolved by a downstream stage if the distinction matters). Wakefit
management declined to comment on named competitors or the industry profit-pool trend
specifically ("I will not be able to comment on industry or specific companies" — C.
Ramalingegowda).

### 2E Risks peers discuss that the main company does not
- **Structural industry profit-pool erosion**: the Wakefit-call analyst's finding that the
  combined mattress-industry EBITDA pool has deteriorated over 10 years amid new-entrant
  competition is a structural risk category not raised with the same framing in SFL's own calls
  per B05.
- **FX/mark-to-market risk on import-linked project debt**: both Greenlam (a Rs18.8cr notional
  euro-loan forex loss tied to its chipboard project, Aug-2025 call) and Centuryply (forex swings
  tied to imported machinery credit) flag currency mark-to-market volatility on capex-linked
  foreign debt as a live earnings-quality issue. This is a risk category (not the same instrument)
  worth cross-checking against SFL's Australia/Spain international exposure and any foreign-
  currency borrowings, which B05 does not appear to interrogate with the same specificity.
  Coding notes: SFL's own depreciation-policy flag (per B05) is an accounting-earnings-quality
  issue; the peers' FX mark-to-market flag is a distinct, cash-adjacent earnings-quality issue.
- **Regulatory/import tailwind (QCO/BIS) not analogous but instructive**: Centuryply and Greenlam
  both credit Quality Control Order (QCO)/BIS implementation with materially reducing imports and
  benefiting organised players in wood panels. Mattress/foam does not have an identical
  regulatory lever in these calls, but the absence of any comparable regulatory-tailwind
  discussion in SFL's calls (per B05) is a difference in kind worth noting, not necessarily a
  missing risk.

## PART 4: TRIANGULATION SUMMARY

- **Claims verified**: 1 of 6 (Claim 3, unorganised-to-organised shift — three independent peers)
- **Claims partially verified**: 2 of 6 (Claim 2, RM shock/pass-through — direction yes, magnitude
  no; Claim 5, capex/spare-capacity — mixed by segment)
- **Claims contradicted**: 1 of 6 (Claim 4, competitive-discipline improvement — priority item for synthesis)
- **Claims unverifiable**: 2 of 6 (Claim 1, e-commerce market size/share; Claim 6, online-vs-offline price-hike asymmetry)

**Most consequential contradiction**: Claim 4. SFL's ASP-premiumisation and margin-durability
narrative implicitly assumes competitive intensity in mattresses is moderating for better-funded,
listed players post-IPO. Wakefit's own management states the opposite occurred over the same
period: "competition intensity has definitely gone up over the last six to nine months... we
definitely have seen increased advertising spending as well as increased matching of prices."
This directly complicates SFL's margin/pricing-power thesis heading into FY27.

**Strongest independent confirmation**: Claim 3. Three independent peers across two adjacent
categories (mattress D2C; wood panels/laminates) all describe the same unorganised-to-organised
structural shift, driven by similar underlying mechanisms (quality-standard regulation, changing
consumer research/discovery behaviour, e-commerce penetration), without any coordination or
incentive to support SFL's narrative. This is the single best-triangulated finding in this stage,
though it is undercut in one respect — none of the three peers cite third-party market data
either, so SFL's own uncited assertion is industry-standard practice, not a distinguishing flag.

**Overall**: The peer set complicates SFL's narrative more than it supports or undercuts it
wholesale. It strongly corroborates the two largest structural claims SFL makes (unorganised-
to-organised shift; a genuine, geopolitically-driven chemical/RM cost shock in the same quarter),
which is meaningful independent support for SFL's broad strategic framing. But it also directly
contradicts SFL's assumption of moderating competitive intensity (the single most decision-
relevant finding here, bearing on margin durability), shows SFL's stated RM pass-through running
well above what comparable branded peers achieved in the same window, and — via the wood-panel
peer pair, an imperfect but instructive adjacent comp — shows an industry capacity race rather
than the “spare capacity, minimal capex” pattern SFL's own segment claims. Two of SFL's six
specific checkable claims (market size/share; online-offline price asymmetry) simply could not
be settled either way by this peer set and should remain open items rather than default to
management's framing.
