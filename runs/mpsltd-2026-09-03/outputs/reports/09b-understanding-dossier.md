assets, a large delivery workforce in lower-cost centres, and a growing

## SECTION 3: BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING NARRATIVE

*(Drafted per the shared five-question spec in prompts/13-synthesis-pipeline.md.
Sourced from B03/B04/B06/B07/B09 only, per that spec. Stage 13's copy is the
version of record and may update this.)*

**1. What the products are and why they matter.** Research Solutions
(about 60% of revenue, B04) is the production line behind scientific,
technical, and medical journals: manuscript copy-editing, typesetting,
peer-review workflow management, research-integrity screening, and the
hosting platforms (HighWire, DigiCore Pro) publishers use to move an
article from author to reader (B04 revenue_streams). A publisher cannot
easily drop this work because it is embedded in the publisher's own daily
production system (B04 moats_present, "Switching costs: High"). Education
Solutions (about 27%, B04) supplies content, assessment, and, since the
Unbound Medicine acquisition, a point-of-care medical-knowledge
subscription used by hospitals and nursing institutions at the bedside
(B04, B07 catalysts_12m). Corporate Learning (about 13%, B04) builds and
delivers managed training programmes for enterprises on a project basis
(B04 revenue_streams).

**2. Who the customers are.** Customers are STM publishers, education
publishers, hospitals and nursing institutions, and corporate training
buyers; the AR names no individual customer (B04 flags). Consolidated
concentration is real but easing: the top customer is 17.3% of FY26
revenue and the top 15 are 58.1% (B04 flags). Standalone concentration
runs higher. The buying relationship in Research is sticky because the
work sits inside the publisher's production workflow, a switching cost
rather than a preference (B04 moats_present).

**3. Why there is demand.** Present demand traces to the volume of
research being published and money spent digitising education. The
downstream candidates named in B09 tie this directly: global STM
journal/article output (about 3.5 million articles a year) drives
Research production demand (B09 downstream_candidates); K-12 and
higher-education digital-learning procurement and enrolment budgets drive
Education demand; hospital and nursing point-of-care tool adoption drives
the Unbound subscription; enterprise learning-and-development budgets
drive Corporate Learning (B09 downstream_candidates).

**4. Why demand should grow.** Forward drivers, each tied to an
externally checkable signal per B09: more articles published each year
(STM Association Annual Report); open-access and research-integrity
mandates such as Plan S (COPE / Retraction Watch); wider point-of-care
tool adoption (HIMSS Analytics surveys); and industry consolidation
pushing fragmented editorial and training work toward a few
technology-led specialists, a pattern partially corroborated (not
confirmed) against two of the four named peers (ECLERX, INDGN; B06
partially_verified). The same peer set also discloses a real, quantified
countervailing force never netted in management's own margin narrative:
three of four peers independently report 2-3.5% a year of wage-inflation
drag on margin (B06 net_narrative_effect: "complicates").

**5. Where the competitive advantage sits.** The moat sits in Research,
not across the whole company. Research carries the two best-anchored
categories the emerging-moat scan found: embedded customer relationships
(C1, "Strong") and industry-consolidation benefit (H1, "Strong") (B07
active_categories). Education is moderate: a recurring subscription
(Unbound) that is real but still small and economically unproven (B07,
overall em_classification "MODEST"). Corporate Learning has no moat: it
declined in FY26, its goodwill was impaired, and it runs project to
project with low predictability (B04 revenue_streams predictability "L";
B07). The BridgeAI/AR-VR leg inside MPS Europa AG is small, shrinking,
and loss-making, so it carries no advantage either (B07 flags).

3. Its two smaller businesses supply education content and assessments,
   plus enterprise training programmes built project by project.
4. Publishers buy Research services because the work sits inside their
   own daily production process. Leaving is hard.
5. Schools, hospitals, and companies buy the other two lines for
   content, a bedside medical-reference subscription, and training.
6. The top customer is a bit over 17 percent of group revenue. The top
   15 customers are 58 percent. Concentration is real.
7. Demand grows with more research articles published each year, more
   digital spending in education, and wider point-of-care tool use in
   hospitals.
8. Three of four named peer companies report rising wage costs eating
   into margin. MPS has not yet weighed this against its own AI story.
9. The moat sits in Research. It comes from switching costs and from
   smaller rivals losing work to bigger, tech-led players like MPS.
10. Education has a small, new, unproven recurring subscription. It is
    not yet a strong moat.
11. Corporate Learning has no moat right now. It shrank in the last full
    year and took a write-down on goodwill.
12. The mental model is a transition: MPS is trying to move from a
    services outsourcer into an AI-enabled knowledge platform, one
    business line at a time, funded partly by acquisitions.
13. The fragility read is MODERATE. Most of the variables that decide
    this story can be checked from filed reports. One, Research margin
    facing AI commoditisation, could break the whole case on its own.
14. The corpus could not establish Unbound Medicine's exact economics
    beyond the acquisition-date numbers, and it could not establish
    whether Corporate Learning's recent recovery is a real structural
    turn or a temporary bounce.
15. The two biggest open questions: did the company's AI products
    actually move the needle in cash terms, and does the Corporate
    Learning goodwill write-down mark a permanent problem or a one-time
    reset the newest quarters are already reversing.
