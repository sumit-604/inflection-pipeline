### PART A — THE FROM STATE

**A1. Archetype.** Build-to-spec component maker (RIP/RIS, OIP,
high-current and wall-through bushings; customer capex cycle, design-in
qualification, content-per-unit driven by voltage/current class,
input-cost pass-through) with licence/scarcity-business features layered
on top (8-10 year utility/OEM qualification cycle functions like a
scarcity gate even though no government licence is involved) (B04
business_type, analyst_note; B03 Section 2A/2B). One business line; the
retrofit/after-sales stream (6.8% of FY26 revenue) shares the same
archetype and customer base and is not treated separately (B04
revenue_streams).

**A2. The simple analogy.** A power line is a highway for electricity.
Where the highway must pass through a wall, it needs a carefully built
gate that lets current through without a fire. That gate is the bushing.
Yash Highvoltage makes those gates, custom-built for each transformer, in
a few designs (oil-filled OIP, resin-filled RIP, extra-heavy-duty for
huge currents). Big buyers such as PGCIL and NTPC only let a supplier
make these gates for them after years of testing, so once approved, a
supplier is hard to replace. Today Yash still imports the most important
ingredient of its main product, the RIP/RIS core, from Europe, like a
sweet-shop that imports a key ingredient it cannot yet make at home
(B04 business model canvas; B03 Section 1E "chai-stall-uncle version").

## SECTION 3: BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING NARRATIVE

Yash Highvoltage makes transformer bushings. A bushing is the insulated
sleeve that carries high voltage current through the wall of a
transformer tank without a flashover or a fire. Every transformer
carries six or seven of them, and no substitute product exists. Resin
impregnated paper bushings, called RIP or RIS, are 83% of sales and are
the dry, newer design the industry is moving to. Oil impregnated paper
bushings, called OIP, are 10% of sales and are the older design, still
in demand for replacement and for United States and European exports.
High current bushings, wall through bushings and a retrofit and testing
service make up the rest, with retrofit at about Rs 16 Cr (B04
revenue_streams; B03 Section 1B/1C).

The buyers are transformer makers of the GE and Hitachi Energy class,
and utilities such as PGCIL, NTPC, NPCIL and DRDO, which have each
approved Yash as a supplier. A utility takes 8 to 10 years to qualify a
bushing supplier, because a failed bushing can burn a whole transformer.
Once approved, a supplier is hard to displace. The customer list is
getting narrower, though. One customer took 18.8% of FY26 sales, about
Rs 44 Cr, against two customers near 10% each a year earlier (B02 rank 8;
B03 Section 2A/4B).

Demand today comes from grid and generation spending. The run tracks it
through the CEA National Electricity Plan transmission capex targets,
the PGCIL capex and repair budget, and the order books of transformer
makers such as BHEL, CG Power, Hitachi Energy India, GE Vernova and
Siemens. All three peers confirm the demand at the transformer level for
the same period. Hitachi Energy India reports a record order backlog,
and Vilas Transcore says there is no slowdown in transformer makers (B06
industry_cross_read; B09 downstream_candidates).

Growth should come from three places. The first is more transformers,
visible in MNRE renewable capacity addition data and the CEA targets.
The second is import substitution, visible in the India bushing import
value under HS code 85049010, which the run could only confirm up to
2023. The third is exports, where Weidmann, Electrolink and the new Yash
HV USA office are the signals to watch, alongside Quality Power's
disclosures on the Sukrut Electric joint venture (B09 downstream_
candidates; B04 revenue_streams; B03 Section 1D).

The competitive edge sits in customer approvals, not in cost or brand.
The RIP and OIP lines both carry a real switching cost from the long
qualification cycle, and the run confirmed that mechanism from the
annual report. The RIP line has no cost edge yet, because about 95% of
its cores are still imported from Europe. The new Vadodara plant is
meant to change that, but it has not started trial production. The moat
scan rates backward integration and the China plus one export channel as
its two leading forward categories, but neither has produced revenue.
Network effects do not exist here, the talent asymmetry test found no
evidence, and cost advantage and brand could not be scored because peer
financial data came out empty. The retrofit line has no separate moat
the run could establish (B07 active_categories, em_score; B04
moats_present; B01 M11 override).

## SECTION 5: PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY

1. Yash Highvoltage makes bushings, the insulated sleeves that carry
   high-voltage current through the wall of a transformer.
2. Every transformer needs six or seven bushings, and there is no
   substitute product for them.
3. Most of Yash's sales, 83%, are the newer resin-type bushing called
   RIP, with the older oil-type OIP making up another 10%.
4. Buyers are large transformer makers and power utilities, including
   PGCIL, NTPC, NPCIL and DRDO.
5. A utility takes 8 to 10 years to approve a bushing supplier, because a
   failed bushing can burn a whole transformer, so once approved, a
   supplier is hard to remove.
6. One customer bought 18.8% of FY26 sales, up from about 10% each for
   the top two customers a year before, so the buyer list is narrowing.
7. Demand today rides India's grid and generation building programme,
   confirmed by all three peer companies in the same period.
8. Growth over time should come from three places: more transformers
   being built, Indian-made cores replacing imported ones, and more
   sales going abroad.
9. Yash's edge is the years-long approval it has already earned with big
   customers, not a cost or brand edge.
10. About 95% of the core material for Yash's main product still comes
    from Europe; a new plant is meant to make that material in India, but
    it has not yet started test production.
11. The mental model here is a climb: from a bushing maker that imports
    its key input to one that makes its own input and reaches higher
    voltages, and that climb is proven only once the new plant passes its
    tests.
12. The fragility read is MODERATE: four variables decide this story,
    about half can be checked from public filings and half rest on the
    company's own word, and no single one of them alone can break the
    story.
13. The corpus could not establish who really controls the technology
    behind Yash's main core, since three concalls gave three different
    answers.
14. The corpus could not establish why cash collected from customers ran
    far below profit in FY26, or why a bank stock report differed from
    the company's own books for 12 quarters running.
15. The biggest open question is whether Yash's own founder, who now runs
    a competing bushing venture backed by another listed company, still
    holds any influence over Yash's technology or customers.
