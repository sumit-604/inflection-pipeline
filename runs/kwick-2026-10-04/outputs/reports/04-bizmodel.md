### 1A. One line
Kwick buys forensic kits, cyber tools and DNA machines from global and Indian suppliers, assembles and delivers them to police and forensic labs mostly through tenders, and rents out handheld scanners to one state (Pros. p.130-132, p.143).

### 1E. Chai-stall-uncle version
Kwick is like a big shop that supplies uniforms and gear to police stations. It does not make most of the gear. It buys from makers, packs it the way the department's list says, and delivers it. Winning orders needs the right paperwork and good local contacts. The police pay late, so Kwick needs a lot of cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills. Profit looks good on paper, but cash comes in slower. One state also rents scanners from Kwick every month.

Primary: P/E on forward earnings built on the Section 1B basis hierarchy, with the margin bridge keyed to gross margin, not to historical EBITDA margin. Secondary: EV/EBITDA. At most one tertiary: cash-flow yield or reverse DCF. Cycle stage that matters: the tender and scheme-release cycle and its effect on year-end receivables.

Sector cap row read. Row "Cybersecurity / VAD" (25x) is supported as the closer fit. Reasons: the economics are value-added resale and integration of OEM tools (gross margin 27.06%, supplier OEM tie-ups non-exclusive, goods 91.08%; Pros. p.24, p.149, p.162). Cyber and digital is 33.32% of revenue, so the row matches the mechanics more than the product label. Alternative "Defence / strategic" (38x) is not supported by the business model evidence: buyers are police and FSLs (Pros. p.131), no defence order, no proprietary platform in revenue, no export, no long-term programme contract. Flag stays for phase-3 confirmation, but this stage's read is against 38x. The row choice is a Role 1 and operator decision. Stage 4 names the evidence only.
