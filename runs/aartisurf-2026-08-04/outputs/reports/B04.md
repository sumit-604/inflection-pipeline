## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description
Aarti Surfactants makes the ionic and non-ionic surfactant chemicals and
specialty ingredients that go INSIDE other companies' shampoo, detergent and
soap bottles — it does not sell a single product with its own name on a
shelf; its customers are Hindustan Unilever, P&G, Colgate-Palmolive and Dabur
(CARE Report p.2), not consumers.

### 1B. Money flow chain, by revenue stream

**Domestic (Local) sales — the core stream**
[Imported/domestic petrochemical & palm-oil-linked feedstock: lauryl
alcohol, fatty acids (HCFA), alpha olefin (CARE Report p.2)] → [Aarti
Surfactants reacts/formulates these into surfactants, blends, mild
surfactants and specialty HPC ingredients at its Pithampur (MP) and Silvassa
(D&NH) plants, 113,000 MT combined installed capacity (CARE Report p.2)] →
[bulk/drum shipments of customised, formulation-specific ingredient batches]
→ [large FMCG manufacturers — HUL, P&G, Colgate-Palmolive, Dabur among named
accounts (CARE Report p.2)] → [invoiced on 15-90 day domestic credit terms,
geography/customer dependent (AR Note 24(b), p.136)].

**Export sales**
[Same feedstock/production process] → [product shipped to customers in the
US, Europe and Southeast Asia, 30+ countries total reach (AR p.2 "About the
Company")] → [payment against sight documents, documents-against-acceptance,
or letters of credit, 15-135 days (AR Note 24(b), p.136)].

**Deemed export sales**
A small residual stream (goods supplied domestically but classified as
"deemed export" under trade regulation, e.g. to export-oriented units) — Rs
480.87 lakh in FY25, 0.7% of Sales of Products (AR Note 24, p.136).

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version
Imagine a company that doesn't sell soap to you — it sells the "soapy
stuff" inside the soap to the companies that make Lux, Dove, Colgate
toothpaste and Dabur products. Aarti Surfactants buys palm-oil-linked
chemical building blocks, cooks them into surfactants at two factories in
Madhya Pradesh and Gujarat's neighbour Silvassa, and ships them to a
handful of giant FMCG customers who then bottle the final shampoo or
detergent. The catch: when the price of its raw ingredients spikes — which
happens often, because they track volatile palm oil markets — this company
often can't simply raise its own prices, because its giant customers have
the negotiating power, not the other way round. So it's stuck in the
middle: input costs that swing a lot, and buyers big enough to resist price
hikes. That's exactly what happened in FY25 and FY26: sales grew nicely,
but the slice of every rupee of sales that turns into profit shrank.

**Overall moat read: weak-to-moderate.** The only credible moat evidence

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage
The global surfactants market is cited in the AR's own third-party research
citations as ~USD 39.5bn (CY24) growing at a 5.3% CAGR to 2030, with the
Indian market cited to reach USD 10.92bn by 2035 (AR p.26-27, sourced to
custommarketinsights.com / grandviewresearch.com / marketresearchfuture.com
— management's framing of third-party data, not independently verified in
this document). This describes a **mature, steadily-growing industry**, not
an early-stage or declining one. AARTISURF's own position within it is that
of a small, sub-scale, margin-challenged domestic player riding the
industry's growth (17.55% revenue CAGR FY20-26, B01 Block C) without yet
capturing the margin profile of its larger domestic peers.
