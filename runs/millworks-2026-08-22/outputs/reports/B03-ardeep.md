## SOURCE AND SCOPE NOTE (read first)

Millworks has never filed a standalone Annual Report. It was incorporated November 1, 2021 and is
converting from private to public via SME IPO. The source document is the Red Herring Prospectus
dated July 7, 2026 (365 pages), which substitutes for the AR per the run brief. The RHP carries the
audited RESTATED Financial Information for FY24–FY26 only (three years, per SME IPO Guidance Note
— there is no fourth or fifth year to triangulate against). This is a RECENTLY-LISTED degradation:
every trend claim in this report rests on a three-point line, not five-to-ten years of history, and
every "consistency with prior years" judgment is correspondingly thinner than a standard AR deep
dive would produce.

A second structural feature governs Phase 1: the RHP contains an **Independent Auditor's Examination
Report on Restated Financial Statements** (Vishnu Daya & Co LLP, dated June 27, 2026, RHP p.192–196,
file pp.13872–14087) — this is a restatement-examination opinion, not a statutory audit report. It
states there were no qualifications in the underlying FY24/FY25/FY26 audit reports (RHP p.193, para 6)
but it does **not** reproduce Key Audit Matters, a CARO 2020 annexure, or an Internal Financial
Controls opinion. No such sections exist anywhere in the 365-page document (confirmed by full-text
search for "CARO", "Key Audit Matter", "material weakness", "internal financial controls...over
financial reporting" — no hits beyond the Audit Committee's forward-looking terms of reference,
RHP p.170). Phase 1 below documents what the Examination Report does say, and separately flags what
a normal AR deep dive would expect and does not find here. This gap is itself informational, not a
mechanical failure — the pipeline does not halt.

Pagination note: this report cites the RHP's own printed page numbers (the "N | P a g e" footer,
matching the RHP's Table of Contents, RHP p.5) for narrative chapters. The Annexures to the Restated
Financial Statements (Annexure I–L) carry no printed page number of their own — they use "F1–F44" /
"S1–S3" folio labels instead — so for Annexures this report cites the extracted file's own page
marker plus folio, e.g. "(RHP, extracted p.214, Annexure XIV/F23)", consistent with B02's convention.

### 2A. Accounting policy aggressiveness
Revenue recognition: goods revenue recognised Ex-Factory at dispatch readiness (Accounting Policy
(n), RHP p.203) — a conventional, not aggressive, policy on its face, undermined in practice by the
restated sales cut-off errors in FY24 and FY25 (per B02's restatement list, Note 3g). Services
revenue (new ₹2,300.00L domestic line in FY26, 15.5% of revenue — RHP extracted p.216/Annexure XXIV)
uses proportionate-completion for fixed-price contracts (Accounting Policy (n)) — a judgment-heavy
method introduced in the same year receivables exploded, with **no customer or nature disclosure**
for this line (confirmed — searched for further detail, none found beyond the single Annexure XXIV
row). Depreciation: Schedule II useful lives, SLM, goodwill amortised over 5 years per AS-14
(Accounting Policy (g), RHP p.202) — not itself aggressive, but the restatement corrected an "incorrect
useful life, scrap value ignored" error (B02 Note 3k), meaning even a conventional policy was
initially mis-applied. Borrowing costs: capitalised for qualifying assets per AS-16 (Accounting Policy
(l), RHP p.203) — standard; but rent and salary expense were wrongly capitalised to CWIP in FY24/FY25
and had to be restated out (B02 Notes 3e, 3f) — a classic expense-deferral pattern that inflated
reported profit and assets in the years it occurred, corrected only via restatement, not caught by
original-year controls.

### 2B. RPT map — extended
Beyond B02's figures, the RPT summary table (RHP p.56–57) shows the Company runs a **revolving loan
account with its own executive directors**, not merely one-off transactions: Sridhar Acharya,
H K Madhu and Rashmi Sridhar Acharya each show "Loan Taken" and "Loan Repaid" line items in the RPT
table for multiple years (e.g., Sridhar Acharya: Loan Taken ₹78.61L / Loan Repaid ₹104.67L in FY26;
₹39.62L / ₹1.95L in FY24). This is a distinct pattern from the Section 185 violation (which concerns
loans **from** the Company to related individuals) — this is money moving **both directions**,
routinely, between the Company and its own promoter-directors, outside the specific Section 185 facts
already confirmed. No interest rate or tenor is disclosed for these director loan balances. This
extends, rather than duplicates, B02 Finding 6.

RPT total as % of revenue: V3 Technologies alone (purchase of materials ₹47.20L + rent ₹155.76L +
advances/security deposit flows, RHP p.56–57) plus director remuneration/loans is a small fraction of
FY26's ₹14,876.70L revenue in pure transaction-value terms, but the **investment and equity-dilution**
side (Quick Pay ₹575.06L equity stake, V3's ₹707.74L PPE-for-equity settlement) is balance-sheet
material relative to the Company's ₹8,266.99L FY26 net worth (6.96% and 8.56% respectively).

**CFO/PAT has been below the 0.7x flag threshold in every one of the three restated years**, and
negative in two of three. This is the single clearest earnings-quality problem in the filing: a
company reporting 24.9% PAT margin (FY26) generated no operating cash at all — cash was consumed,
not produced, by growth. The CFO quality checks specified by the protocol:
- **One-time inflators:** the ₹441.00L FX translation gain (95.3% of Other Income, 8.8% of PBT) is
  a non-cash, reversible item embedded in PBT before the working-capital adjustments in the cash flow
  statement — it is added back correctly as a non-cash adjustment (Annexure III: "Unrealised Foreign
  Exchange Gain/(Loss) (363.79)"), so it does not itself distort the CFO calculation, but it does
  inflate the PBT numerator that CFO/PAT is measured against, mechanically worsening the ratio's
  optics is not the issue — the real issue is that PBT quality (see 3C) is itself inflated by a
  non-operating item.
- **Interest classification:** Interest Cost of ₹312.23L is treated as a financing-activity item, not
  netted into CFO before the subtotal — standard treatment, no aggressive reclassification found.
- **Unsustainable payable stretching:** confirmed and material. Trade payables rose ₹6,787.14L in
  FY26 (Annexure III) — this is the primary financing mechanism for the receivables build, not bank
  debt. Combined with the MSME Section 43B(h) disallowance trend (B02 Finding 7) and the recurring
  bank drawing-power discrepancies (B02 Finding 4), the payables side of working capital shows its own
  quality problems, not just the receivables side.
- **Inventory rundown:** not present — inventory grew, did not run down, so this specific inflator
  does not apply.

### 4A. Disclosed risks — real vs boilerplate

Four risks are evident from Phases 1–3 but are not named as standalone risk factors:

### 6E. Quiet Abandonment Check (mandatory)

**Finding: implicit retraction, material.**

The opening "Our Business" chapter frames diversification across four sectors as a **core competitive
strength**: "Our experience across multiple regulated sectors enables us to address differing
technical and quality requirements...allowing us to service diverse customer programs across our
business Sectors" (RHP p.124, Competitive Strengths). The same chapter's own forward-looking Business
Strategies section restates this as an active priority: "we intend to diversify our product offerings
within our existing manufacturing capabilities" and "diversify revenue streams within approved
customer frameworks" (RHP p.130–131).

The operational section that should show this diversification working — the Revenue from Operations
by Business Sectors table, presented in both Risk Factors (RHP p.29) and "Our Business" itself
(RHP p.130) — shows the opposite happened in the same fiscal year the diversification claim is made.
Aerospace fell from 8.91% (FY24) to 4.79% (FY25) to 0.99% (FY26) of revenue. Semiconductor fell from
22.72% to 20.20% to 5.94%. Defence rose from 11.15% to 6.81% to **69.43%**. Customer concentration
(top customer) rose from 54.09% (FY24) to 33.51% (FY25) back up to **47.02%** (FY26) (RHP p.28).

This is classified as **(a) implicit retraction**: the opening chapter states diversification is a
strength and an active strategy (X), and the operational sector/customer tables in the same document
show concentration increasing sharply (not-X), with **no acknowledgment anywhere in the Competitive
Strengths or Business Strategies sections that FY26's actual sector mix moved in the opposite
direction from the stated strategy.** MD&A does discuss the Defence/drone-kit growth driver factually
(RHP p.208), but never frames it as tension with, or a departure from, the diversification narrative
stated four sections earlier in the same document.

**Materiality: this changes the thesis, not merely framework noise.** A reader who took the
Competitive Strengths section at face value would believe Millworks is a balanced, four-sector
precision manufacturer with diversification as a structural moat. The financial reality is a company
whose FY26 growth is a single-customer, single-sector, defence/drone story layered directly on top of
the Quick Pay concentration and AS-18 scoping question already flagged in Phases 1–3. The strategic
narrative and the financial reality describe two different companies.

No second Quiet Abandonment candidate of comparable materiality was found in the sections reviewed
this pass.

**Phase 6 summary:** No Chairman's letter exists to assess in the traditional sense; the adapted
6A–6D checks show a filing that is candid about specific transactional facts (compliance history,
hedging policy, working capital pressure) but frames its strategic self-description in terms
(diversification, balanced multi-sector capability) that FY26's own operational data contradicts.

**Phase 6 verdict: 🟡 Watch**, driven primarily by the 6E finding.
