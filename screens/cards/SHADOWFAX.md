## Business Understanding Narrative

**What does it do, and how does it make money?** Shadowfax is a third-party
logistics network. It carries parcels for e-commerce sellers, runs the returns
and exchange leg, does same-day intracity and next-day intercity delivery, and
powers last-mile drops for quick-commerce platforms. It adds critical
logistics for time-sensitive and luxury goods through CriticaLog, which it owns
outright, and it has begun running dark stores (Investor Presentation FY26,
page 7). It works through 2.3 lakh active delivery partners (Investor
Presentation FY26, page 12).

The money is orders multiplied by price per order, against a network cost base
that is largely fixed. That is why the margin moves so violently with volume.

**What is changing?** Operating leverage, and it is arriving fast. Adjusted
EBITDA went from Rs 5 crore at a 0.7% margin in Q4 FY25 to Rs 58 crore at 4.7%
in Q4 FY26 to Rs 67 crore at 4.9% in Q1 FY27. Profit after tax went from
negative Rs 10 crore in Q4 FY25 to positive Rs 65 crore in Q1 FY27 (Investor
Presentation FY2026 Q4, page 16; Investor Presentation Q1 FY27, page 13).

**Why now?** Because the volume is still compounding while the margin
expands. Q1 FY27 delivered 24.7 crore customer orders, 9% above Q4 FY26, on
revenue of Rs 1,358 crore, 65% above Q1 FY26. That was the fifth consecutive
quarter of 60%-plus revenue growth (Investor Presentation Q1 FY27, page 8).

**What must be true for the thesis to work?** Price must hold while the
network fills. Management argues it does, because quick commerce is not a
duopoly: "there are not just one or two players in this segment. It's a six,
seven player sort of a market. We are probably working with each one of them...
we believe there is no leverage that any one partner has on 3PLs" (Earnings
Call FY26, page 14).

**What breaks it?** A large platform insourcing its own last mile. Every
quick-commerce operator can build delivery in-house, and several have. The
company's answer is that it is the default for all of them, which is a strong
position and a fragile one at the same time.

## 2. Business model and archetype

One network, several services. The FY26 deck sets them out (page 7): forward
express parcel, reverse pickup and hand-in-hand exchange, Prime same-day and
next-day delivery, hyperlocal quick commerce and food delivery, plus
CriticaLog and dark store operations.

Revenue by line, FY26 (Investor Presentation FY26, page 12): Express Rs 3,041
crore, up 77.2%, and Hyperlocal Rs 792 crore, up 54.3%, within total revenue
from operations of Rs 4,202 crore, up 69.1%. Total orders 72.6 crore, up 66.4%.

The company frames its future as four growth engines: Quick-Comm, Prime, Prime
Large and CriticaLog. Prime Large is live in 6,000 pin codes and expanding to
10,000 in FY27, on a Rs 60 crore annual run rate based on Q4 FY26. CriticaLog
runs at a Rs 130 crore annual run rate on the same basis (Investor
Presentation FY26, page 21).

**Archetype: platform and network.** Active users, take rate, unit economics
per transaction, network density and retention are the variables.

## 3. Competitive advantages

Density, and it is measurable. The company covers 15,600 of India's 19,300 pin
codes, targets about 17,000 by the end of FY27, has passed 99% coverage in
Uttar Pradesh and Haryana, and intends to cover the whole country by FY28
(Earnings Call FY26, page 7). Management is explicit that coverage itself wins
share: "our mere presence in some of these pin codes unlocks volumes and market
share gains".

That is a real network effect. A seller who needs national reach cannot use a
carrier that reaches four-fifths of the country.

The claimed share is large and partly self-measured. Forward express parcel
share is given as 8% in FY22 rising to 27% to 29% in Q4 FY26, and the deck's
own footnote says share to Q2 FY26 is from a Redseer report while **the Q4 FY26
figure is a company estimate** (Investor Presentation FY26, page 7). Use the
trend, not the number.

Moat: present and strengthening in coverage; unproven in price.

## 6. The transition and the quality ladder

FROM R2, a cost-advantaged converter of trucks and riders into deliveries, with
no pricing power and cyclical returns. TO R4, a franchise and share-of-wallet
leader, where density and default status create repeat economics.

Two mechanisms carry it. The first is coverage, which management calls a direct
market-share gain per incremental pin code. The second is forward integration
into the customer's operation through dark stores, and this one is new.

Management's claim, quoted once: "This strategy is not going to be about market
share win. It is about category creation... what we are seeing and feeling now
is verticalized quick commerce becoming the newest game in town" (Earnings Call
FY26, page 7).

The dark store economics are stated with unusual candour: "our top stores would
be running at about 20% plus gross margins with a further space to expand as
the dark store volumes go up" (Earnings Call FY26, page 18). Management also
says it ran 15 stores for about two years, 90% of them in a single city, before
scaling. That is the behaviour of an operator testing before spending.

One rung, inside the base rate.

## 8. Proof check

The triggers claim operating leverage. The last two quarters show it without
ambiguity. Q4 FY26 adjusted EBITDA Rs 58 crore at 4.7%. Q1 FY27 Rs 67 crore at
4.9%, with profit after tax up 716% year on year. Order volume rose 9%
sequentially into a quarter that is not seasonally the strongest.

Dark stores moved from 15 to 47 in one quarter, against a stated target above
100. That trigger is being delivered in front of the reader.

**Name the weakness:** none of this comes from a statutory filing in this
corpus. Adjusted EBITDA is the company's own measure and its adjustments are
not disclosed in the pages held. A `/step1` must replace every figure on this
card with the filed statement.

## 9. Flags from the documents

- **Every number here is from a presentation, not a filing.** Flag first.
- "Adjusted" EBITDA of Rs 67 crore sits close to reported profit after tax of
  Rs 65 crore in Q1 FY27. Either the adjustments are small, or something below
  the line is helping. The corpus does not say which. Flag.
- The Q4 FY26 market share of 27% to 29% is a company estimate, not the Redseer
  number used for earlier periods, and the deck footnotes this itself. Flag.
- Dark stores take the company into its customers' business. A 3PL that
  operates stores competes with some of the platforms it delivers for. Flag the
  channel conflict.
- Management's answer on pricing power rests on there being six or seven
  quick-commerce buyers. If that list consolidates, the argument inverts. Flag.
- Crude and geopolitics were raised on the call as a cost risk to the next six
  months (Earnings Call FY26, page 14). Flag.
- Promoter and pre-IPO holdings, lock-in schedule, customer concentration and
  credit rating NOT FOUND.

- **Ugliness: ARTIFACT-OF-CLIMB.** The Q4 FY25 loss was a network being built

## 12. Verdict card

**PROCEED to `/step1`.**

The proof gate has fired on delivered numbers, not on a plan. A fixed-cost
network filling up is the cleanest form of operating leverage there is, and
this one is filling while still growing revenue at 65%. The dark store
extension is being tested rather than announced: fifteen stores in one city for
two years before scaling to forty-seven is disciplined behaviour, and the 20%
gross margin figure is specific enough to be checked.

Proceed with one condition stated plainly. Every figure in this card comes from
a slide deck. The first job of a `/step1` is to replace them with filed
statements and to learn what "adjusted" adjusts.

**Load-bearing facts a `/step1` must verify first**
1. The statutory results for FY26 and Q1 FY27. Reconcile adjusted EBITDA to
   reported EBITDA and name every adjustment.
2. Customer concentration. How much of Hyperlocal revenue comes from the
   largest platform, and what happens if it insources.
3. Promoter and pre-IPO investor holdings, and the lock-in expiry calendar.
4. Price per order by service line, over the last eight quarters. The whole
   pricing-power argument lives there.
5. Dark store unit economics: capital per store, payback, and whether the 20%
   gross margin survives beyond the top stores.
6. Working capital and receivable days against e-commerce platform customers.

**What would change the view**
- A sixth quarter of margin expansion with volume growth intact.
- A named platform taking its last mile in-house.
- Price per order falling while volume grows, which would mean the share is
  being bought.

No Role 1 valuation was run and no target price is set. This verdict decides only whether the
name earns a full `/step1`, and it is not a position.
