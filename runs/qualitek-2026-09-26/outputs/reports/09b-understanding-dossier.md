### PART A — THE FROM STATE

**A1. Archetype.** Two lines, read as a hybrid (B04 analyst_note explicitly
flags this as imperfect-fit):
- Core testing/inspection/certification line (85%+ of revenue):
  **Outsourcing partner (CDMO/EMS/IT services variant)** — client
  concentration (no long-term contracts, purchase-order basis), capacity
  fill (utilisation lag named as the top FY27 MD&A priority, B04), price
  per assignment. Layered with elements of **Licence/scarcity business**:
  the NABL/NABCB/USFDA accreditation gates whether Qualitek may sell a
  test at all (B04 moats_present; B07 category B2).
- New QTIPL (software/digital) and LabOps Global (lab-operations
  outsourcing) lines: too small and undisclosed to assign an archetype;
  QTIPL logged FY26 revenue of Rs 37.63 lakh and a loss, LabOps zero
  revenue in its first partial year (B12b finding location "B05 1A
  QTIPL row"; B03 missing_risks).

**A2. The simple analogy.** A manufacturer that wants to sell a car part,
a packet of food or a batch of medicine usually cannot sell it until an
outside, accredited lab signs a report saying it meets the required
standard. Qualitek runs that lab. It owns 10 of its 16 labs and runs 6
more inside government facilities under public-private-partnership
contracts (business-narrative.md). Customers place one purchase order at
a time, with no long-run contract locking them in (B04 revenue_streams).
The company earns a fee per test, not a share of what its customer sells.

## SECTION 3: BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING NARRATIVE

*(Drafted per prompts/13-synthesis-pipeline.md BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING
NARRATIVE spec, from B01-B09. Stage 13's copy is the final version.)*

Qualitek runs accredited laboratories that test other companies' products
against required standards and hand back a signed report. A factory needs
that report before it can sell, export or certify what it makes. Transport,
defence and mechanical testing is 40 to 45% of revenue: emission,
vibration, durability, EV battery and EMI/EMC tests against BIS, ASTM and
international standards. Food and feed testing is 25 to 30% and pharma
testing is 15 to 20%, both run mostly through Interstellar Testing Centre,
the subsidiary that supplied about 45% of FY26 consolidated revenue. The
balance is new inspection, audit and certification work under a NABCB
Type A accreditation, plus two small bought businesses, QTIPL in software
and LabOps Global in lab operations, and neither reports material
revenue. The customer cannot easily do without the report because a
regulator or a buyer requires an accredited lab to sign it, and a
validated product moved to a new lab must be validated again, although
the run did not quantify that switching cost (B04).

The customers are manufacturers and processors in automotive, defence,
food, pharma, minerals and electricals, plus government bodies. Six of
the 16 labs run in PPP mode, and the named public sector clients are
Indian Oil at Panipat and at its 2G ethanol plant, and Western Coalfields
through the Quality Council of India (B03 ar_new_downstream_entities).
Customers buy on purchase orders with no long-term contracts. The last
concentration figure is from FY23, before the acquisitions: the top 5
customers were 25% and the top 10 were 38% of standalone revenue. The run
did not establish current customer concentration (B04 input_gaps).

Present demand comes from mandates. New BIS Quality Control Orders,
FSSAI food-safety circulars and MoRTH vehicle safety and emission rules
each create tests a manufacturer must buy. The NABL and NABCB
accreditation status decides whether Qualitek may sell those tests at
all. Indian Oil and other PSU work-order renewals, the USFDA status of
the Bengaluru pharma lab, and the IIP manufacturing index are the other
named demand signals (B09 downstream_candidates).

Demand should grow as regulation widens. The run puts third-party
testing market growth near 6.5% a year on its conservative basis, and
Vimta puts its own sector growth at 7.5 to 9%. Vimta also puts the
electronics industry at about 4.5%, the slowest figure any peer gave,
and that is the segment Qualitek plans to enter in South India (B06).
Each new Quality Control Order or FSSAI mandate is gazetted, so this
driver can be checked outside the company. Qualitek names no market size
and no industry growth rate anywhere in its own filings (B09 flags
FLAG-NO-MGMT-CLAIM).

The moat sits in accreditations and government relationships, not in
price. Accreditation lock-in scored Strong in the emerging moat scan, and
the government customer book scored Moderate, but those contracts run 24
to 36 months and must be won again (B07). Under verifier review the
USFDA-lab and first-mover claims fell to inference, the international
partner list fell to claim-tier, and the corrected score of 12.5 sits
0.5 above the line between MODEST and NONE (B12c). The testing lines hold
a partial moat through accreditation, but pricing power is weak because
every job is priced per purchase order. The inspection line holds an
accreditation with no disclosed revenue, and QTIPL and LabOps show no
moat on any evidence. Talent asymmetry and the cannibalization barrier
did not score and sit in the optionality register as unevidenced (B07
optionality_register).

## SECTION 5: PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY

1. Qualitek runs accredited labs that test products for other companies
   and hand back a signed report the customer needs to sell or export.
2. Transport, defence and mechanical testing is the largest line, 40 to
   45% of revenue. Food and feed is next, then pharma.
3. It runs 16 labs today, 10 owned and 6 inside government partnerships,
   with more than 1,200 staff and 450 scientists.
4. Customers are manufacturers who buy one purchase order at a time. No
   customer signs a long-term contract.
5. Government bodies are named customers too. Indian Oil and Western
   Coalfields both appear in the filings as new work.
6. Demand grows when regulators write new rules. New BIS orders, FSSAI
   food rules and vehicle safety rules each create tests a company must
   buy.
7. This demand driver can be checked from outside the company, because
   every new rule is published by the regulator, not claimed by Qualitek.
8. Qualitek itself states no market size and no industry growth rate in
   any filing it has made.
9. The moat is the accreditation, not the price. A lab without NABL,
   NABCB or USFDA status cannot sell a valid test report at all.
10. That moat scored Strong for accreditation and Moderate for the
    government relationship, but after a check the wider score fell to
    just above the line between a modest moat and no moat.
11. The mental model: a fee-per-test testing business is trying to climb
    into a spec'd, accredited supplier with real switching costs, and the
    proof needed is new lab capacity turning from a cost into a profit,
    which has shown for one half-year so far.
12. The fragility verdict is FRAGILE. Two of the four variables that
    decide this thesis are reported by the company alone, with no outside
    check, and the promoter side of the business carries an unexplained,
    unvalued related-party stock swap worth close to half of one year's
    revenue.
13. The corpus could not establish current customer concentration; the
    only figure is from before the company's biggest acquisitions.
14. The corpus could not establish the government or public-sector share
    of revenue or of unpaid customer bills, which is the single fact that
    would settle whether slow cash collection is normal for this kind of
    government work or a warning sign.
15. The two biggest open questions: who valued the related-party stock
    swap and on what basis, and whether the margin gain seen in the second
    half of last year holds up or reverses in the next half-year results.
