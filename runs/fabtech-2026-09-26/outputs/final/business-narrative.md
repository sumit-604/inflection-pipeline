# Fabtech Technologies (FABTECH): Business Narrative

_Phase 1 (evidence gate). No valuation content. Market cap Rs 648 Cr. Consolidated basis. Listed October 2025._

## Business Understanding Narrative

Fabtech builds factories for drug makers as fixed price turnkey projects. The plants make pharmaceuticals, biotech products, vaccines and animal health products. Fabtech designs each plant, buys the equipment, installs it, and validates it to cGMP, WHO, EU-GMP and USFDA standards. The physical deliverables are clean rooms with controlled air, purified water systems and process lines. Fabtech does not manufacture this equipment; in FY25 about a quarter of its purchases came from promoter group companies and the rest from outside suppliers. Equipment supplied inside these contracts was 91% of FY26 revenue, and installation and commissioning services were 9%. A drug maker cannot sell regulated medicine without a plant that passes inspection, but it can hire a different contractor for its next plant, and the company's own prospectus says barriers to entry are few. The buyers are pharmaceutical, biotech, vaccine and animal health manufacturers, mostly in the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Africa, with India at 11% of FY26 sales. The five largest customers were 56% of FY25 revenue, down from 75% in FY23, and the list rotates: three of the four largest named FY25 customers fell below 10% in FY26, while Al-Afiya rose from under 2% to 12.6%. Each project is a separate tender that runs 12 to 36 months and bills on shipment and milestones, so each win is a new sale, not a recurring contract. Present demand comes from Middle East and African governments and drug makers that want to make medicines at home. Named programmes fund that push: the AfDB Continental Pharmaceutical and Vaccine Manufacturing Vision, with $11bn earmarked for the pharma industry to 2030, Gavi's African Vaccine Manufacturing Accelerator at about $1.5bn, and in Saudi Arabia the Vision 2030 localisation rules under LCGPA and NUPCO and the KSA National Biotechnology Strategy. Recent named awards include a Botswana veterinary vaccine plant, a West Africa oral solid dosage facility and a North Africa veterinary formulations plant. Forward growth can be checked from outside in three places: named end customer project awards filed with the exchange, a second exchange filed Saudi civil or MEP order through the SACE entity, and a refresh of Middle East and Africa pharma capex tracking. The run sizes the reachable pool at about Rs 14,700 Cr of plant spend a year, growing about 4.5% a year, and Fabtech holds about 2.8% of it; peers confirm that pharma and API demand holds up, but they report order deferrals, mostly in domestic segments, not a broad capex race. No business line shows a proven moat: the turnkey equipment line passed one of twelve moat tests at Gate 0, a verifier cut that to zero under the strict rule, and the services line rides the same projects with no moat of its own. The emerging moat scan found its best case in Saudi localisation and early Africa entries, but a funded rival can copy a majority local Saudi entity, so talent asymmetry (Category 21) and the cannibalisation barrier (Category 22) both scored zero, and the verifier corrected score of about 10 reads NO MEANINGFUL EMERGING MOAT.

## Stalled order book, unproven cash, thin moat

Fabtech Technologies builds drug, vaccine and biotech plants for customers, mostly in the Gulf and Africa. FY26 consolidated revenue was Rs 411 Cr, up 26%. PAT was Rs 38 Cr, down from Rs 46 Cr. Equipment that Fabtech buys and installs is 91% of revenue; it makes none of it. The UAE was about 31% of FY26 sales, Saudi Arabia about 20%, and India 11%. Kenya, Morocco, Egypt and Algeria together were about 19%; the moat scan left Morocco out, and the numbers verifier restored it. Subsidiaries carry about 35% of revenue, so only the consolidated accounts tell the story.

The claimed transition is from a UAE heavy contractor to a local player in Saudi Arabia and a vaccine plant builder in Africa. Saudi revenue rose 130% to Rs 17 Cr in Q1 FY27. Management describes the UAE book as under severe regional headwinds. Contribution margin rose from 37.6% to 46.7% in the same quarter, which management credits to geography mix and project selection. That is one quarter, not a trend. The Saudi step is not settled either. On the Q1 FY27 call the CEO called SACE an incorporation with the acquisition still proposed, while the growth officer called it acquired. An Italian acquisition has slipped twice and now targets March 2027. The IPO added about Rs 207 Cr of equity. Only 22% of proceeds were deployed by March 2026, and none of the Rs 30 Cr set aside for acquisitions.

Gate 0 scored 62 of 160, AVERAGE. Growth and the balance sheet score well: debt to equity is 0.10, and cash and deposits are about Rs 209 Cr. Cash generation scores near the floor. Operating cash flow was Rs 60 Cr in FY24, minus Rs 36 Cr in FY25, and Rs 0.5 Cr in FY26 against PAT of Rs 38 Cr. Seven year cumulative operating cash flow is 0.35 times PAT. FY25 predates the listing, so the drag is not an IPO artefact. Receivables rose to Rs 204 Cr from Rs 151 Cr. The share older than six months rose from 35% to 44%. The auditor made Rs 59 Cr of dues older than a year a key audit matter. A verifier found the Gate 0 working capital trend used the wrong formula; on the fixed formula the cash conversion cycle moved only 4 days since FY20. The AVERAGE class holds either way.

Reported profit leans on items outside operations. Other income was Rs 21 Cr, 43% of Rs 48 Cr PBT, and 58% of it was a foreign exchange gain. The hedge book is one USD 2 million forward against about Rs 221 Cr of foreign currency assets. A 10% currency move now swings PBT by Rs 23.5 Cr, against Rs 8.1 Cr a year earlier. EBITDA margin fell from about 20% to about 14%. The FY26 annual report carries six internal number inconsistencies. One is a balance sheet face that overstates receivables by Rs 37 Cr against its own note and the filed results. Accounting quality scores 5 of 10. In its favour, the MD&A names the receivables problem plainly, and both audit opinions are clean.

Management cut FY27 growth guidance on three calls in a row: 30 to 40% in February, about 25% in April, 20 to 25% in August. It never called it a cut. Q1 FY27 revenue grew 10.3% to Rs 75 Cr. The order book sat at Rs 900 to 926 Cr from July 2025 to June 2026 while management reported new wins each quarter. Management has refused a numeric breakdown of that book on every call. Retention terms, the hot lead pipeline and the revenue recognition method each changed between speakers or calls. FY26 revenue beat the Rs 380 to 400 Cr guide, and PAT of Rs 38 Cr missed the Rs 39 Cr floor. In April management said receivables would fall at once in Q1 and Q2; the Q1 call put them at Rs 211 to 215 Cr. On the verified record one promise was delivered, three partly, and six missed. The concall stage graded credibility C; the red flag verifier lowered it to D.

The promoter review returns CAUTION, with no deal breaker. Pledge is zero and the promoter group holds 68.94%. The largest single promoter, Aasif Ahsan Khan, holds no board seat at Fabtech. He chairs Fabtech Technologies Cleanrooms, a listed group company from which Fabtech bought Rs 23.6 Cr of goods in FY26. A Sharjah company where he is a director earned Rs 3.6 Cr of sales commission from Fabtech. A promoter group company bought the 51% FABL stake that Fabtech sold. A Rs 23.5 Cr current loan labelled related party has no named counterparty. The Company Secretary resigned on 24 September 2026, and an Executive Chairman change was announced without a name. On the other side, two independent directors rotated out on schedule and were replaced by a chartered accountant and a pharma regulatory specialist. The CEO and CFO stayed through a hard cash year, and media report a first dividend of Rs 0.60 a share.

Three listed peers, SETL, HLE Glascoat and Praj, run process equipment and process plant businesses; none is a pure turnkey pharma plant contractor. They confirm sector cost pressure and report order deferrals rather than cancellations. Most of those deferrals are domestic, and only one SETL call ties them to the war. They weaken Fabtech's claim that it faces only European rivals, since SETL and HLE Glascoat describe mixed Indian, European and Chinese fields. They make equipment, not whole plants, so that test is partial. The pipeline's own reading that Fabtech's 33% and 43% cost figures were inflation rates was wrong; they are rupee cost growth against 28% revenue growth. No peer discloses a win rate, so Fabtech's shifting win rate claims cannot be benchmarked.

The runway is real on paper. Two methods put the Middle East and Africa plant pool at about Rs 14,700 Cr a year. Fabtech holds about 2.8% of it, which leaves about 36 times revenue headroom. The pool grows about 4.5% a year. A realistic share path gives Rs 632 Cr of revenue in three years and Rs 779 Cr in five, a 15% and 14% CAGR, below the 20 to 25% guide. Management's $30bn ten year regional claim sits 1.7 times the conservative pool and cites no source. Demand is not the binding limit. The limit is turning Rs 9,300 Cr of enquiries into a growing order book and funding the Rs 100 to 165 Cr of extra receivables that growth needs at the current collection pace.
