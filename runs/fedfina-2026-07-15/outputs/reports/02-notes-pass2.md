## PASS 2 NEW FINDINGS (by note number)

### 1. Auditor transition mid-year, not disclosed in quantum or reason (Note 34.1, p.209)
The auditors' remuneration footnote states: **"Includes remuneration paid to previous Auditors."**
This confirms a change of statutory auditor occurred during or immediately before FY25 (current
auditor: KKC & Associates LLP, engagement partner Hasmukh B. Dedhia, per the signature block at
Note 59/p.258). Statutory audit fee **fell 42.4% YoY** (₹59 Lakh → ₹34 Lakh) while Limited Review
fee rose 20.0% (₹35 Lakh → ₹42 Lakh); the note gives no breakdown of what was paid to the
outgoing vs incoming auditor, nor the reason for the change. An auditor transition at a recently
listed (Nov-2023) NBFC is a governance item an investor should independently verify (via the
Corporate Governance report/AGM minutes, outside Notes scope) rather than infer from this footnote
alone. 🟡 Watch

### 2. Real estate sector concentration — ~47% of the loan book, previously unquantified (Note 48.08, p.238)
Note 48.08 (missed entirely in Pass 1, which stated no segment-wise revenue split exists in the
Notes) discloses **direct exposure to real estate**: Residential Mortgages ₹4,85,115 Lakh
(₹4,851.15 Cr) + Commercial Real Estate ₹76,490 Lakh (₹764.90 Cr) = **Total Real Estate Exposure
₹5,61,605 Lakh (₹5,616.05 Cr)**, up from ₹5,02,173 Lakh (+11.85% YoY). Against total gross on-book
loans (amortised cost + FVOCI, ₹11,83,842 Lakh), this is **47.4% of the entire loan book** (FY24:
50.6% of a smaller book) — i.e., despite FEDFINA's positioning as a diversified Gold/LAP/Home
Loan/SME/Wholesale lender, real-estate-collateralised lending (LAP + Home Loans) is structurally
just under half the balance sheet. The *share* is declining as the gold loan book grows faster
(gold loans rose from 30.95% to 35.85% of total assets per Note 8.2, already known from Pass 1),
but the absolute real estate exposure is still growing in Rupee terms and remains the single
largest asset-class concentration in the book, a data point Pass 1 did not surface. 🟡 Watch

### 3. NPA vintage is aging, not just growing — Doubtful (1-3 year) bucket up 292.6% (Note 48.30(A), p.248-249)
Within the RBI IRACP-vs-Ind AS bridge table (Pass 1 covered the aggregate Stage 1&2 provisioning
swing from this note but not the sub-bucket aging detail), the NPA composition by age shows:
Sub-Standard (Stage 3, <1yr) ₹15,124 Lakh (FY24: ₹9,092 Lakh, +66.4%); **Doubtful (1-3 year)
₹3,467 Lakh vs ₹883 Lakh, +292.6%**; **Doubtful (more than 3 year), newly appearing at ₹45 Lakh**
(FY24: nil); Loss category ₹406 Lakh vs ₹220 Lakh (+84.5%, ECL coverage 100.7%). The sharp rise in
the 1-3 year doubtful bucket and the first-ever appearance of a >3-year doubtful bucket indicates
a portion of the NPA stock is aging and not resolving quickly, which is a different signal from
simply "more Stage 3 this year" — it points to slower workout/recovery on the oldest tranche of
bad loans even as fresh Stage 3 additions (Sub-Standard) also rose. 🔴 Red Flag

### 4. Restructured loan book resolved almost entirely via write-off, not cure (Note 49, p.252)
The restructured-advances roll-forward (not covered in Pass 1) shows: opening balance (1-Apr-24)
4 borrowers / ₹286 Lakh outstanding / ₹65 Lakh provision (all under the "Others" restructuring
category — no CDR or SME Debt Restructuring Mechanism accounts exist). During the year: **zero
fresh restructuring, zero upgradations to standard, but write-offs of 3 of the 4 borrowers /
₹257 Lakh (89.9% of the opening outstanding balance) / ₹56 Lakh of the provision.** Closing
balance: 1 borrower / ₹29 Lakh. The legacy restructured book did not cure into standard
classification during the year — it was almost entirely written off, a negative signal on
restructuring recovery quality even though the absolute Rupee amount is immaterial. 🟡 Watch

### 5. FVOCI-book Stage 3 loans grew far faster than the amortised-cost book Stage 3 already flagged in Pass 1 (Note 8(d)/(f), 44.1.2(b), p.192-193, 227)
Pass 1's finding #7 quantified Stage 3 growth on the **amortised-cost** book only (+20.3% YoY,
₹161.9 Cr → ₹194.75 Cr). Missed: on the **FVOCI** book, gross Stage 3 loans rose from ₹335 Lakh to
**₹7,127 Lakh, a 2,027.5% YoY increase** (Note 8(d)), with the associated ECL allowance rising from
₹70 Lakh to ₹2,715 Lakh (+3,778.6%, Note 8(f)) and the fair value of collateral against this Stage 3
FVOCI exposure rising from ₹509 Lakh to ₹7,683 Lakh (Note 44.1.2(b)). While the FVOCI book is
smaller and the base effect is extreme (off a near-zero prior-year balance), the pace of Stage 3
migration in the assigned/FVOCI-classified portfolio is materially faster than in the core
amortised-cost book and was not captured in Pass 1's Stage 3 discussion. 🔴 Red Flag

### 6. Asset-liability maturity gap swung negative in the >1-year bucket (Note 42, p.222)
The contractual maturity analysis of assets and liabilities (not read in Pass 1) shows the net
position (Total Assets minus Total Liabilities) by bucket: **Within 1 year: +₹3,07,663 Lakh
(₹3,076.63 Cr) surplus, up from +₹2,20,079 Lakh in FY24. After 1 year: -₹52,927 Lakh (-₹529.27
Cr) deficit, a swing from +₹6,004 Lakh (surplus) in FY24.** This is the first precise Rupee
quantification of the funding-tenor mismatch that Pass 1's finding #6 flagged only qualitatively
(the shift toward short-tenor WCDL/cash-credit funding, +331.6% YoY). The company now runs a
structural liability-heavy position beyond the 1-year horizon, funded by a liquidity surplus in
the near-term bucket — consistent with, and a sharper quantification of, the funding-mix risk
already on watch. 🟡 Watch (reinforces and quantifies Pass 1 finding #6)

### 7. Interest rate sensitivity shows rising liability-side rate exposure (Note 44.3.2, p.229)
Not covered in Pass 1: the EaR (Earnings-at-Risk) sensitivity table shows that for a 25 bps
parallel rate increase, PAT impact from loans is **+₹571 Lakh** (FY24: +₹446 Lakh) but from
borrowings is **-₹1,690 Lakh** (FY24: -₹1,345 Lakh). Netting these, a +25 bps rate shock would now
reduce PAT by **₹1,119 Lakh**, up from a net ₹899 Lakh impact in FY24 — a 24.5% YoY increase in
net negative rate sensitivity, consistent with the growing share of floating/short-tenor
borrowings (WCDL/CC) relative to fixed-spread loan assets. 🟡 Watch

### 8. Rent and third-party recovery costs surging well ahead of revenue growth (Note 34, p.208)
Two line items inside "Other Expenses" not discussed in Pass 1: **Rent expense rose 293.4% YoY
(₹243 Lakh → ₹956 Lakh)**, even though ROU (capitalised branch leases) grew a more moderate 37.0%
(already known from Pass 1) — implying a large increase in short-term/low-value leases expensed
directly rather than capitalised (confirmed by Note 47(f): rental expense for low-value/short-term
leases rose from ₹164 Lakh to ₹361 Lakh, +120.1%). Separately, **Collection Agency Commission rose
112.3% YoY (₹1,216 Lakh → ₹2,581 Lakh)**, a cost line that tracks directly with the rising
recovery/collection effort implied by the credit-cost surge already flagged as Pass 1's #1 finding
— i.e., the P&L is absorbing higher third-party collection costs on top of the higher ECL charge,
a second, distinct cost consequence of the same asset-quality deterioration. 🟡 Watch

### 9. Gross write-offs actually declined YoY, a counterpoint to the provisioning surge (Note 50, p.252)
Not covered in Pass 1: total loans and advances written off during the year were **₹3,958 Lakh
(₹39.58 Cr), down 14.0% from ₹4,603 Lakh (₹46.03 Cr) in FY24.** This is a useful counterweight to
Pass 1's #1 and #3 findings (impairment charge +228.6%, standard-asset provisioning +792.7%): the
realised write-off rate did not rise in tandem with the provisioning build, supporting the reading
that FY25's large provisioning increase is materially a forward-looking/overlay-driven cushion
build (as management itself discloses, Note 3.6(j)) rather than a reflection of already-crystallised
losses. This nuance was absent from Pass 1's largely negative framing of the provisioning swing. 🟢

### 10. One positive credit-rating migration during the year (Note 48.19, p.239)
Not covered in Pass 1: of the nine rated instrument categories, **CRISIL Ratings upgraded the
Company's Long Term NCD (Subordinated Debt) — wait, correction: the CRISIL upgrade specifically
applies to "Long Term Non Convertible Debentures" from CRISIL AA/Positive to CRISIL AA+/Positive**
(the subordinated-debt lines carry separate India Ratings/CARE ratings at IND AA+/Stable and CARE
AA+/Stable respectively, both unchanged). This is the only rating migration disclosed in the year
(all other agency ratings — India Ratings, CARE, ICRA — held stable) and is a modest positive
counterpoint to the CRAR decline (23.46% → 21.92%) and funding-mix shift flagged elsewhere. 🟢

### 11. Short-term liability stock ratios confirm the funding-mix shift with precise metrics (Note 48.31(d), p.245, 251)
Not covered in Pass 1 in ratio form: "Other Short Term Liabilities as % of Total Liabilities" rose
from **14.62% (FY24) to 16.92% (FY25)**; as % of Total Assets, from **11.65% to 13.67%**. Commercial
Paper, a new instrument this year, separately adds 0.92% of total liabilities / 0.75% of total
assets (FY24: nil for both, first CP issuance already flagged in Pass 1). These RBI-mandated LRM
stock ratios corroborate, with an independent metric, the funding-mix shift toward short-tenor
liabilities already flagged qualitatively in Pass 1's finding #6. 🟡 Watch (reinforces Pass 1 #6,
not counted as a fully separate new risk)
