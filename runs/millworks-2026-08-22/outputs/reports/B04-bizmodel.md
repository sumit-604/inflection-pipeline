## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description

Millworks machines and assembles precision metal parts to a customer's drawing or spec, then sells
those parts to Defence, Railways, Aerospace and Semiconductor equipment makers who cannot afford
the part to be wrong (RHP p.120).

### 1B. Money flow chain, by revenue stream

**Stream 1 — Build-to-Print (BTP) / Build-to-Spec (BTS) product manufacturing (the core stream)**
[Input: customer's technical drawing or functional spec, plus raw metal — aluminium, titanium,
stainless steel, brass, polycarbonate (RHP p.125)] → [Millworks machines, fabricates, welds, EDM-cuts
or laser-cuts the part across 4 AS9100D/ISO9001:2015-certified Bengaluru units, then inspects it with
CMMs and traceability documentation (RHP p.121, p.125)] → [delivers the finished component or
sub-assembly Ex-Factory — control transfers, and revenue is recognised, at the factory gate (RHP p.256)]
→ [an OEM, Tier-1 or Tier-2 buyer in Aerospace, Defence, Railways or Semiconductor equipment
(RHP p.191)] → [pays against a purchase order or rate contract, on credit — realisation has stretched to
93.22% of FY26 revenue sitting in receivables at year end (RHP p.24)].

**Stream 2 — Engineering / job-work services (new in FY26, Rs 2,300.00 lakh, first appearance in the
revenue split; RHP p.257)**
[Input: a customer's functional or performance requirement, sometimes customer-supplied material]
→ [Millworks executes machining/fabrication as job-work, billed time-and-material or, for fixed-price
work, on percentage-of-completion (RHP p.254)] → [delivers a processed component, sub-assembly or
engineering output] → [an OEM customer — the disclosed driver of this new line is drone-component
work for Quick Pay Private Limited (RHP p.130)] → [billed and collected against the service contract].

**Note on Quick Pay** — this is not a separate revenue stream in the RHP's own classification, but it is
the single largest money flow in the company. Quick Pay Private Limited contributed 47.02% of FY26
total sales, Rs 6,992.76 lakh, buying drone structural frames, motor housings, BLDC motor stators/rotors
and mounting brackets that Quick Pay integrates into finished drone systems and sells onward
(RHP p.25, p.130). Millworks holds a Rs 5.75 crore equity stake in Quick Pay, taken by converting an
existing unsecured loan (RHP p.130, p.192 [Annexure XV: 5,332 equity shares, cost Rs 575.06 lakh]).
There are also common shareholders between the two companies, though the RHP states no director,
promoter or KMP is otherwise related to Quick Pay (RHP p.25). This is best read as a quasi-captive
customer relationship: Millworks' largest revenue source is a company it partly owns and effectively
helped bootstrap into the drone business.

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version

Think of Millworks as a very fussy, very well-certified metal workshop. A fighter-jet part maker,
a train-brake maker or a drone company hands them a drawing that says "make this exact piece,
to this exact tolerance, and prove every step on paper." Millworks cuts, machines and checks the
metal, then ships it. They do not design the end product and they do not sell to the public — they
sell to the companies that build the real thing. The catch: almost half their sales this year came from
one customer, a drone firm they themselves own a slice of, and that customer is slow to pay — the
company is owed nearly as much money as it made in sales all year. So the workshop is busy and
profitable, but a lot of its cash is still sitting in someone else's pocket.

### 2A. Five forces

No wide or durable moat is evidenced in the documents. The closest thing to a moat — certification plus
program-level switching costs — is real but narrow, and is offset by severe customer/supplier
concentration that looks more like dependency than competitive advantage.

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage

Global aerospace and defence market: $846.94 billion (2025) → $899.65 billion (2026), 6.2% CAGR,
forecast to reach $1,185.07 billion by 2030 at 7.1% CAGR (RHP p.108-109) — a maturing-but-growing
global industry. Indian defence production was Rs 1,09,556 crore in FY26 (April-December) and
defence exports were Rs 23,622 crore in FY25, up 12% YoY, with government targets of Rs 3,00,000
crore production and Rs 50,000 crore exports (RHP p.109-110). India-specific defence manufacturing
is in an early-growth phase, policy-subsidised by Atmanirbhar Bharat/Make in India. Millworks itself
is a very early-stage participant within that growth phase — 5 years old, most of its scale built in the
last 12 months, concentrated in one customer relationship.

### 3D. Unit economics — the physics of the business

- **Define one unit**: one machined/fabricated component or sub-assembly delivered against a purchase order (the RHP prices by order/line item, not a standardised "unit"; a formal average-price-per-part figure is NOT FOUND in the documents).
- **Revenue per unit**: NOT FOUND at the individual-part level. The closest available proxy is Average Order Value: Rs 23.47 lakh (FY24) → Rs 48.04 lakh (FY25) (RHP p.24); FY26 AOV NOT FOUND in the documents provided.
- **Cost per unit**: NOT FOUND at the individual-part level; the closest proxy is Cost of Materials Consumed as % of revenue, which rose sharply from 26.96% (FY24) to 49.52% (FY26) as the mix shifted to high-value electronic drone-kit content (RHP p.256).
- **Volume drivers**: capacity hours available (currently 3,83,019 hours FY26, rising to a proposed 6,71,499 hours post-capex) (RHP p.88), number of qualified customer programs, and order-book conversion.
- **Price drivers**: part complexity/tolerance, material grade (titanium/aerospace-grade vs standard steel), whether the arrangement is BTP (customer-specified, generally lower value-add) or BTS (Millworks-designed-to-spec, generally higher value-add), and sector (defence/aerospace command higher unit value than railways per the order-book table, RHP p.129).
- **Cost drivers**: raw material grade and price (aluminium/titanium/stainless/brass/polycarbonate), the increasingly electronics-heavy drone-kit bill of materials, skilled labour (CNC programmers, quality inspectors), and the single dominant supplier now at 44.05% of purchases (RHP p.31).
- **Incremental margin / operating leverage**: strongly positive historically — Total expenses as % of revenue fell from 74.68% (FY24) to 67.21% (FY26), and the company explicitly attributes this to "improved absorption of fixed and semi-fixed manufacturing overheads" as revenue scaled (RHP p.256, p.261-262). Direct expenses fell from 28.66% to 6.50% of revenue and employee costs fell from 21.77% to 5.79% of revenue over the same period (RHP p.261-262) — classic operating leverage from a young, still-underutilised fixed base. The risk: this leverage is currently riding on one customer's ramp, so it can reverse just as fast if that customer's orders slow.

| Replacement cost / asset reproduction | Not applicable | No evidence this is a scarce-capacity or infrastructure-style asset story |

**Cycle stage that matters for valuation**: Millworks is at the very early stage of its own operating
S-curve (5 years old, most scale built in the trailing 12 months), riding an early-growth phase of Indian
defence indigenisation policy. The valuation question is not "what multiple does a mature precision
manufacturer deserve" but "how much of the current growth rate and margin profile is durable once the
Quick Pay concentration either normalises or breaks." Any valuation exercise must treat FY26 as a
possibly non-representative base year.
