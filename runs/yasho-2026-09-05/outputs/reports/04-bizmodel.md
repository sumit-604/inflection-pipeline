## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description
Yasho makes specialty chemical ingredients — rubber chemicals, lubricant
additives, aroma chemicals and food/pharma-grade additives — that other
manufacturers (tyre makers, oil blenders, flavour houses, personal-care
brands) buy as inputs, mix into their own products, and pay for on
standard trade-credit terms (AR p.5, p.28-29).

### 1B. Money flow chain, by revenue stream

**Industrial Chemicals (rubber chemicals, lubricant additives, resin
stabilisers)**
[Petrochemical/organic-intermediate raw material, largely imported] →
[Yasho reacts/processes it into accelerators, antioxidants, additives,
stabilisers at Vapi/Pakhajan plants] → [Delivers packaged chemical to
tyre, auto-component, conveyor-belt, glove, condom, lubricant-blending
and resin/ink manufacturers, ~62-69% of it exported] → [Industrial B2B
buyer, largely MNC and large domestic manufacturers] → [Pays on
negotiated trade-credit terms; one buyer has gone further and pre-funded
Yasho's own capex with advances] (AR p.5, p.28-29; Inv. Pres. slide 7,
slide 12).

**Consumer Chemicals (aroma chemicals, food antioxidants, personal-care,
oral-care, agro/pharma intermediates)**
[Raw material] → [Yasho processes into flavour/fragrance ingredients,
food preservatives, personal-care and agro/pharma intermediates] →
[Delivers to F&F houses, food/FMCG, agro/pharma formulators] → [B2B
buyer] → [Trade credit] (AR p.5, p.28).

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version
Think of Yasho as the spice supplier for big factories, not the factory
itself. Tyre makers need a very specific "spice mix" (accelerators,
antioxidants) to make rubber cure correctly; oil companies need additive
"spice" to make hydraulic and engine oil work; perfume and food companies
need aroma "spice" for flavour and fragrance. Yasho cooks these spice
mixes to exact recipes in its own kitchens (4 plants) and ships them
worldwide. It buys its raw ingredients mostly from abroad, so when
ingredient prices swing, Yasho feels it first. One very large international
customer trusted Yasho's kitchen so much it is paying Yasho in advance to
build a whole new kitchen wing just for them, on a 15-year supply deal —
that is as close to a customer saying "you are our chef for life" as a
chemicals business gets (AR p.28, Inv. Pres. slide 12).

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage and company position
Global specialty chemicals: ~US$835 bn in 2026, growing ~5.5% (AR p.25-26).
Indian specialty chemicals: ~US$67 bn (2025) projected to US$93.4 bn by
2034, a 3.65% CAGR (AR p.26) — a maturing, mid-single-digit-growth
industry, not a boom sector. Against that backdrop Yasho is guiding 30-40%
company revenue growth annually for the next 2-3 years (Inv. Pres. slide
13) and an FY28 revenue target raised to >Rs1,600 cr from an AR-stated
~Rs1,500 cr FY28 target (AR p.30 vs Inv. Pres. slide 13) — i.e. growth well
above the industry rate. This gap must be closed by market-share gains,
new capacity, and specific customer wins (the MNC contract, new
geographies in Asia/Africa, Inv. Pres. slide 11), not by industry beta.
That makes Yasho's growth idiosyncratic and execution-dependent rather
than a rising-tide story.

## SECTION 3: FINANCIAL METRICS THAT MATTER FOR THIS BUSINESS MODEL

### 3A. Ignore-these / track-these

| Commonly tracked ratio | Why it misleads for Yasho right now |
|---|---|
| Trailing net profit margin, in isolation | FY26 PAT margin was only 3.04% (AR p.30) despite a 20.99% EBITDA gain, because of heavy interest cost on Rs540-558 cr of debt carried through a multi-year capex build (AR p.29, Inv. Pres. slide 17). Reading net margin alone understates underlying operating improvement; track EBITDA margin and PBT margin trend instead. |
| Trailing P/E | PAT swung +312.74% YoY in FY26 and EBITDA +127.61% YoY in Q1FY27 (AR p.30; Inv. Pres. slide 15) as utilisation crossed an inflection point — trailing earnings are not a stable base for a multiple right now. |
| Dividend yield | Irrelevant — FY26 dividend was Rs0.50/share, the Board retained the entire remaining profit under Retained Earnings and explicitly declined to transfer to General Reserve (AR p.32-33); this is a reinvestment-stage company, not an income stock. |
| Static current ratio | Current ratio was flat at 1.37 FY26 vs 1.38 FY25 (AR p.29) even as the underlying working-capital cycle was being actively restructured (190→143 days, Inv. Pres. slide 11) — the point-in-time ratio misses the more important trend. |
| Single-year ROE/RoNW level | RoNW of 5.69% FY26 (AR p.29) is depressed by leverage carried through the capex cycle and by the margin inflection happening mid-cycle; the level understates normalized earning power once capacity utilisation and the leverage ratio both settle. |
| Asset turnover, read as pure efficiency | Pakhajan land is only 25% utilised by design, with capacity built ahead of demand (Inv. Pres. slide 7) — turnover ratios are mechanically depressed during a phased capex build-out even in a well-run plan. |

### 4C. Quarterly monitoring checklist (10-15 items)

1. Revenue growth YoY and volume growth YoY — good: tracking toward the 30-40% guide; trouble: volume growth below revenue growth or below ~15%.
2. Gross margin % (revenue less material cost) — good: holding at or above ~40%; trouble: compression below high-30s%.
3. EBITDA margin — good: sustaining above ~20%; trouble: falling back toward 16-17%.
4. Capacity utilisation % — good: rising toward 80%+; trouble: flat or falling while capex continues.
5. Export revenue % of total — good: stable-to-rising near 62-69%; trouble: sharp drop (tariff or key-account loss).
6. Debt/EBITDA — good: at or below 2.5x; trouble: rising back above 2.5-3x.
7. Interest coverage ratio — good: rising trend; trouble: stalling below ~2x.
8. Working capital cycle (days) — good: holding near/below 143 days; trouble: drifting back toward 190 days.
9. Customer advances received / vendor advances paid for the MNC contract — good: on the disclosed schedule (Rs98.12 cr received, Rs70.89 cr paid as of Q1FY27); trouble: schedule slipping.
10. FY27 capex spend vs the Rs250 cr outlay — good: on pace; trouble: material under- or over-run without explanation.
11. Bank loan credit rating trend — good: maintaining/improving from the recent BBB+ to A- upgrade (Inv. Pres. slide 11); trouble: outlook revision to negative.
12. New product commercialisation / R&D pipeline updates — good: concrete new SKUs/lines named; trouble: silence on pipeline progress for multiple quarters.
13. Any disclosure of top-customer or top-5-customer revenue concentration — currently absent; a first-time disclosure (or refusal to disclose on request) is itself information.
14. Raw material cost trend commentary (sulphur-based chemistries specifically) — good: stable sourcing; trouble: renewed volatility commentary.
15. PAT margin trend relative to EBITDA margin trend — good: gap narrowing as leverage/interest normalizes; trouble: gap widening (rising interest burden outpacing operating improvement).

### 4D. Highest-value questions for management

1. What is the customer-revenue concentration (top 1, top 5, top 10 as % of FY26 revenue), and how much of the FY26-28 growth guide rests on the single MNC contract? Reassuring: the MNC contract is a modest single-digit-to-teens % of the FY28 target and the rest is broad-based. Worrying: the MNC contract plus a handful of large accounts represents most of the incremental growth, hidden by the 2,000-customer headline count.
2. Of the 42% Q1FY27 volume growth, how much came from existing-customer additional off-take versus genuinely new customers/geographies (Asia, Africa)? Reassuring: broad-based across many accounts. Worrying: concentrated in one or two large accounts, or a one-off restocking effect.
3. What is the blended realized price per tonne trend by division, separating volume-led from price/mix-led margin gains? Reassuring: price/mix stable-to-positive even as volumes surge. Worrying: price per tonne declining, with the margin gain purely a fixed-cost-absorption effect that reverses if volumes soften.
4. How much of the FY27 capex (raised from Rs125 cr to Rs250 cr) is tied to signed contracts or firm enquiries versus speculative capacity ahead of demand? Reassuring: the increase is mostly pre-committed. Worrying: a large uncommitted, speculative component.
5. What is the raw-material import-dependency ratio, and what hedging or alternate-sourcing exists specifically for the sulphur-based chemistries flagged as volatile? Reassuring: diversified, multi-geography sourcing with domestic-substitution progress. Worrying: concentrated single-region or single-supplier dependency.
6. Given the self-set covenant of Debt/EBITDA below 2.5x through a ~Rs100 cr FY27 borrowing plan, what is the covenant headroom if growth comes in at half the guided 30-40% rate? Reassuring: the covenant still holds. Worrying: breach risk if growth undershoots materially.
7. Is the working-capital cycle reduction from 190 to 143 days a structural, process-driven change (credit terms, inventory systems) or a timing/one-off effect? Reassuring: structural and sustainable. Worrying: a collection-timing effect that reverses as revenue scales further.
