## PART 2: UNPROMPTED CROSS-READ

### 2A. Demand environment

All three peers describe a broadly constructive-to-resilient demand backdrop across the Oct-2025 to Jul-2026 window, consistent in direction with Share India's stabilization narrative, but with important qualifications. SMC Global's Q2 FY26 opening remarks (SMCGLOBAL-2025-11-07) actually describe the derivatives-segment ADTO "declining sequentially" that quarter [p.2] — a genuine headwind quarter, one quarter before Share India's claimed Q3 FY26 recovery, which is consistent with a trough-then-recovery pattern rather than continuous industry-wide strength. By Q3 FY26, SMC's tone turns "broadly constructive" [SMCGLOBAL-2026-02-09, p.2] and Angel One describes "early signs of recovery" with rising average daily orders [ANGELONE-2026-01-21, p.4] — both consistent with Share India's own Q3 FY26 recovery framing. Choice International's Q3 FY26 call frames a "fundamental structural pivot" and "equitization of household savings" [CHOICEIN-2026-02-11, p.2] — the most bullish structural framing of the three, though largely narrative rather than quantified. By Q1 FY27, tone diverges: Angel One reports its strongest quarter in the set (PAT +102.1% YoY, lifetime-best client funding book [ANGELONE-2026-07-21, p.5]), while SMC Global's Q1 FY27 opening remarks are notably measured — "a relatively stable operating environment" [SMCGLOBAL-2026-07-31, p.3] — a materially cooler characterization than Share India's own "strongest quarterly performance to date" framing of the same quarter. This bears directly on the unquantified "we have performed far, far better [than peers]" claim Kamlesh Shah made in the Q1 FY27 call (flagged LOW in 05-concall.md 3A): Angel One's own Q1 FY27 print (PAT +102% YoY) was also a standout quarter, which undercuts, without formally contradicting (no named-peer comparison exists), the implication that Share India uniquely outperformed a struggling peer set that quarter.

### 2B. Pricing and input costs

Angel One directly confirms industry-wide MTF pricing pressure from "deep-pocketed" entrants (Q3 FY26, see Question 3 above) and separately discusses its own cash/intraday brokerage simplification to a flat 0.1% structure (Q2 FY26, ANGELONE-2025-10-23, p.11) — evidence of active brokerage-price competition across the sector, consistent with the general commoditization pressure Share India's transition thesis is built to escape. SMC Global discloses no MTF pricing commentary (no comparable product disclosed). On funding costs, SMC Global's Q4 FY26 opening remarks note the industry-wide shift toward NCD/CP funding diversification "in line with the RBI's intent... to reduce their dependency on bank borrowing" [SMCGLOBAL-2026-05-08, p.2] — directly parallel to Share India's own NCD/CP diversification strategy (Crisil A1+ CP, NCD programme), confirming this is an industry-wide funding-mix response to the same regulatory pressure, not a Share India-specific innovation.

### 2C. Capex cycle

This is a lone-expander-vs-industry-race question with a clear and striking answer for the physical-branch dimension specifically: Choice International is running a branch expansion at a vastly larger scale than Share India. Choice's Q1 FY27 call (CHOICEIN-2026-08-17) discloses it is "on track with our growth target of 800 branches over the next three to four years" [p.13], already at "210 to 220 branches" with a March-2027 target of "300 to 350" [p.13] — versus Share India's stated ambition of 30 branches over 2-3 years. Both companies frame physical Tier-2/3 presence as a structural counter to digital-only discount brokers, so this is directionally an industry-wide theme (physical distribution still matters), but the SCALE gap is an order of magnitude: this is a capacity race in which Choice International is racing far harder than Share India. Choice's per-branch capex is also disclosed as low (Rs 3-5 lakh per new branch, CHOICEIN-2026-02-11, p.5, "not very significant in terms of capex"), a cost-light distribution/customer-acquisition branch model, contrasted with Share India's Rs 15 Cr-MTF-book-in-8-months payback framework, which is a capital-allocation (MTF book funding), not a cost-light distribution, model. The two companies are both "expanding branches" in name but running structurally different economics under that label. Angel One, by contrast, is not expanding physical branches at all in this period; its "phygital" answer is an assisted-partner (AP/MFD) network already built out (10,000+ APs, 11,000+ MFDs, ANGELONE-2026-01-21, p.4) rather than new owned branches, and its own capex commentary centres on technology/AI and a proposed Rs 150 Cr capital infusion each into wealth management and its NBFC platform (ANGELONE-2026-04-22, p.7) rather than physical footprint. So the peer set splits on this specific capex question: one large-scale physical expander (Choice), one technology/vertical-capital expander (Angel One), and Share India sits between them at a much smaller physical-branch scale than Choice's.

### 2D. Competitive mentions

No peer transcript names Share India, mentions its ticker, or describes it in an unmistakable, identifiable way in any of the twelve transcripts searched. This is a clean silence across the full corpus, not a finding of contradiction; the peer set operates in adjacent but distinct sub-segments (Angel One is retail-digital-first; Choice is phygital broking/NBFC/advisory; SMC is a diversified broking/insurance/NBFC group with treasury-style arbitrage), and none appears to track Share India as a direct competitor worth naming to its own analysts.

### 2E. Risks peers discuss that Share India does not

- **NBFC asset quality (GNPA/NNPA).** SMC Global's Q1 FY27 call discusses elevated GNPA in its SME lending book and the legal/recovery process for large secured accounts [SMCGLOBAL-2026-07-31, p.11-12]; Choice International discloses its own NNPA figure proactively each quarter (e.g., "NNPA at 2.79%" [CHOICEIN-2025-10-24, p.3]). Share India's own NBFC-adjacent lending exposure (MTF book, Share India Cred) is never discussed in asset-quality terms (GNPA/NNPA equivalent) in any of its three primary calls — a disclosure gap by comparison with two peers that both proactively quantify credit-quality metrics for their lending books.
- **Cost-structure/peer-comparison scrutiny.** Angel One faced a direct analyst challenge (Q2 FY26) comparing its cost ratios unfavourably to a peer's IPO filing (ANGELONE-2025-10-23, p.11-12) and answered candidly that "different companies are built very differently" rather than deflecting to an external cause — a contrast worth noting against Share India's own pattern of external-blame-heavy responses to comparable pressure (05-concall.md 2B).
- **Regulatory-tenure/expiry-structure sensitivity.** Angel One was asked directly (Q2 FY26) to sensitize its profitability to a potential SEBI move reducing monthly options expiries from 8 to 1-2 [ANGELONE-2025-10-23, p.13] — a live, named regulatory tail risk to F&O-dependent broking revenue that Share India's own calls do not raise or get asked about, despite Share India's own heavy commodity/F&O ADTO dependence.
- **Regulatory restriction generating customer attrition.** Angel One's Q1 FY27 call surfaces a direct customer complaint about a "restricted basket" feature causing a client to move an SME holding to a competitor (Zerodha) [ANGELONE-2026-07-21, p.19-20] — a concrete, named instance of regulatory-compliance friction causing customer loss, a risk category (compliance-driven attrition) not discussed in any Share India call.

## PART 5: CROSS-PEER HYPOTHESIS

Reading across all three peers, a pattern emerges that none of them states directly: the sector is bifurcating along a "distribution capital intensity" axis that has nothing to do with prop-vs-client mix and everything to do with what each firm is willing to spend capital building physically. Choice International is racing to 800 low-cost branches (Rs 3-5 lakh each) explicitly to acquire Tier-2/3 clients physically while executing digitally — a volume-acquisition bet. Angel One is doing the opposite: it has stopped building physical footprint altogether, is redeploying capital into AI/technology and named-vertical capital infusions (wealth, NBFC), and is content to let market share "stagnate" (its own CFO's word, per Q4 FY26) rather than fight for it with feet on the ground. SMC Global sits in between, running its "prop" desk as a low-leverage treasury sweep rather than a capital-intensive directional book, and leaning on its insurance/NBFC diversification rather than aggressive branch or MTF-book expansion.

Share India's own strategy — a small, capital-intensive branch network (30 branches, each carrying a Rs 15 Cr MTF-book funding obligation) combined with a large, bank-guarantee-dependent prop desk — does not cleanly match any of the three peer archetypes above. It is trying to run Choice's physical-acquisition play at a much smaller scale while carrying a prop-book risk profile that looks larger and more leveraged than SMC's self-styled "treasury" arbitrage. [INFERENCE] If the sector really is bifurcating between capital-light distribution racers (Choice), capital-light technology compounders (Angel One), and capital-light treasury-style prop books (SMC), then Share India is the outlier for combining capital-intensive branch economics with a capital-intensive, bank-guarantee-dependent prop book at the same time — a combination none of the three peers is running, and one that may explain why Share India alone among the four needed a bespoke, quantified 8-month/12-month branch payback discipline (Q1 FY27) that no peer discloses needing for its own physical or digital growth spend. This is inferable only from comparing all three peers' capital-allocation choices side by side; no single peer's disclosure states it.
