## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description

Ceigall builds roads, highways, metro viaducts, tunnels and bridges for
Indian government agencies under fixed-price contracts, and increasingly
also PART-OWNS the roads it builds through 15-20 year annuity concessions
(HAM), a second and very different business bolted onto the first.

### 1B. Money-flow chain, one per revenue stream

**Stream 1: EPC (Engineering, Procurement, Construction) — 57.86% of FY26
revenue (AR, sheet 32, "Key Performance Highlights")**

[Input: cement, steel, bitumen, rented equipment (~14% of equipment cost
is rented, Inv. Pres. p.27), subcontracted labour, in-house design team]
→ [Ceigall designs and builds a road/bridge/metro-viaduct segment for a
government client under a fixed-price or item-rate contract] → [delivers
a certified, milestone-completed civil asset] → [who pays: NHAI, state
PWDs, MoRTH, UPMRC/JMRC (metro), GMADA, and similar government or
quasi-government authorities] → [how they pay: progressive milestone
billing certified by the client's engineer; cash follows certification
with a lag, and revenue recognised ahead of certified billing sits in
Contract Assets, which tripled in two years to Rs 14,132m (B02 finding,
Note 11)].

**Stream 2: HAM (Hybrid Annuity Model) — 41.68% of FY26 revenue (AR,
sheet 32)**

[Input: same construction inputs as EPC, plus Ceigall's own equity
infused into a dedicated project SPV and concession-linked debt raised
by that SPV] → [Ceigall, acting as EPC contractor to its own SPV, builds
the road; the SPV holds a 15-20 year concession from NHAI and carries NO
traffic-risk (unlike a toll or BOT road)] → [delivers a commissioned,
toll-free national highway to NHAI] → [who pays: NHAI, as grantor] → [how
they pay: TWO phases. During construction, milestone-linked payments are
booked as construction revenue on a percentage-of-completion basis,
building up the "Receivable Under Service Concession Arrangements"
(RUSCA) financial asset (Rs 14,578.90m FY26, +40.5% YoY, Note 7 consol.,
per B02). After Commercial Operation Date (COD), NHAI pays fixed
semi-annual annuity instalments (principal plus interest) over the
concession life, recognised as the RUSCA amortises]. AR sheet 19: "The
Concessionaire is compensated through annuity payments for maintaining
the asset over the agreed concession period."

IMPORTANT GAP: the AR's 41.68% figure does not split how much of that is
still construction-phase revenue (EPC-like risk) versus post-COD annuity
income (bond-like risk). Only ONE HAM asset (Malout-Abohar) had reached
COD and been monetised as of FY26 (Inv. Pres. p.24); the annuity-income
share of the 41.68% is therefore almost certainly small. NOT FOUND: a
disclosed construction-vs-annuity revenue split.

**Stream 3: O&M (Operations & Maintenance) — 0.46% of FY26 revenue (AR,
sheet 32)**

[Input: maintenance manpower, spares, safety/compliance systems] →
[Ceigall maintains a previously built EPC/HAM road for its operating
life: lighting, electrical systems, barriers, incident management] →
[delivers continued lane availability and safety compliance] → [who
pays: the same government/concession authority] → [how they pay: a fixed
periodic O&M fee under the original or a standalone contract]. Currently
immaterial in revenue terms (0.46%) but structurally the highest-
predictability stream the company has.

### 1D. Simplified business model canvas

| Regulatory moat or burden | BURDEN dominant — land acquisition, environmental clearance delays, and government payment terms are the AR's own top-listed threats (AR sheet 33 "Risk and Concerns") | MOAT once a specific concession is won (15-20 year exclusivity on that road), but no portfolio-wide exclusivity; each new concession is re-bid competitively |

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version

Imagine a contractor who builds roads for the government, paid in stages
as he finishes each stretch, like a chai-stall uncle who gets paid after
each round of tea, not before. That is Ceigall's main business, EPC, and
it is more than half its revenue. Now imagine the same uncle also agrees
to build and OWN a small stretch of road himself, using some of his own
money and some borrowed money, and instead of being paid as he pours
cement, the government promises to pay him a fixed rent for the road
twice a year for the next 15-20 years, whether or not anyone uses the
road. That second deal is HAM. The two deals need completely different
patience: the first pays fast but thin, the second pays slow but for a
very long time. Ceigall is trying to do more of the second deal while
still doing plenty of the first, and it has just sold its first
"rent-collecting" road to an outside investor to get its money back
faster, calling this the start of a repeatable cycle.

## SECTION 2: INDUSTRY DYNAMICS & COMPETITIVE POSITION

### 2A. Five forces

| Force | Assessment | Helps / Hurts / Neutral |
|---|---|---|
| Competition (count) | Indian NHAI/state EPC and HAM tenders draw multiple qualified bidders per package (L1, lowest-bidder award); the AR does not name specific competitors (NOT FOUND in the documents) | HURTS — bid price is set by competition, not by Ceigall |
| Entry barriers | Technical qualification (past project scale, net worth, bid-capacity formula) and bank-guarantee capacity gate small players from large NHAI packages; specialised structures (tunnels, hilly terrain, elevated corridors) narrow the qualified pool further (AR sheet 31, "Dominance in Specialized & Complex Structures... command higher entry barriers than standard highway EPC") | HELPS — moderate barrier that favours an incumbent with a 24-year track record |
| Supplier power | Key inputs (cement, steel, bitumen) are commodities with many domestic suppliers, but MD&A itself flags "volatility of raw material prices... can increase construction costs and impact financial stability" (AR sheet 33) as a standing risk, meaning price protection is incomplete | Mixed / slightly HURTS on cost-pass-through gaps |
| Customer power / concentration | Customers are almost exclusively government or quasi-government bodies (NHAI, state PWDs, MoRTH, metro corporations); a near-monopsony buyer set sets contract terms, payment timing, and certification pace | HURTS — extreme customer concentration on the buy side |
| Substitutes | No real substitute for physical road/bridge/tunnel construction, but the "substitute" risk is really just another qualified bidder winning the tender instead of Ceigall | Neutral — competitive intensity is the effective substitute risk |

Two confirmed, moderate-durability moats stand out: the specialised-
structure execution niche, and the asset-light cost-efficiency model.
Neither is a strong, wide moat; both are typical of a good, not great,
mid-cap contractor.

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage

The Indian road/highway EPC industry itself is MATURE and fragmented
(competitive L1 tendering has existed for two decades) but is riding a
STRUCTURAL government-capex upcycle: MoRTH's FY2026-27 budget allocation
of Rs 3.10 lakh crore is a record, up 8% YoY, and NHAI's own allocation
rose 10% (AR sheet 31, "Road Sector Budget Allocations"). Ceigall's OWN
position within that industry is a scaling mid-cap contractor attempting
to climb from pure contractor into asset ownership (HAM) and into two
brand-new verticals (renewables, T&D) that are still at the
letter-of-award / 0%-completion stage.

## SECTION 3: FINANCIAL METRICS THAT MATTER FOR THIS BUSINESS MODEL

### 3A. Ignore-these / track-these

| Commonly tracked ratio | Why MISLEADING or IRRELEVANT here |
|---|---|
| Blended consolidated P/E or EPS | Mixes EPC's cash-ish project margin with HAM's largely non-cash, leveraged concession income; a single EPS number obscures two different earnings qualities |
| Standard debtor/receivable turnover on consolidated revenue | The HAM portion's "receivable" (RUSCA) is structurally a financial asset amortising over 15-20 years, not trade credit; blending it with EPC trade receivables misstates collection risk either way |
| Asset turnover / fixed-asset turnover | Physical PP&E is only ~6% of standalone assets (AR sheet 38); the assets that actually matter (SPV equity, RUSCA) are financial, not fixed |
| Inventory days | Meaningless for the HAM annuity model (no inventory concept in a financial-asset revenue recognition); only relevant to the EPC book |
| Consolidated ROCE / ROE read as a quality signal | Both fell sharply (ROCE 31.98%→19.22%, ROE 33.57%→14.39%, FY24-26, AR sheet 10/16) largely because capital is locked up as equity in SPVs that earn nothing until COD — a mechanical artefact of the HAM ramp-up, not necessarily a quality collapse, though it must still be watched |
| Consolidated free cash flow / CFO trend read in isolation | Negative CFO in FY25 (Rs -5,155.57m) and FY26 (Rs -912.83m) is structurally the cost of funding HAM SPV construction through the parent (B03 finding); distinguishing structural funding from genuine cash-conversion failure requires the standalone-vs-consolidated split, not the consolidated number alone |
| Current ratio | HAM SPV financial assets shift between current/non-current based on concession amortisation schedules, not operating liquidity; a falling current ratio (1.77→1.42, AR sheet 32) can reflect concession mechanics as much as liquidity stress |

## SECTION 4: RISKS, VALUATION APPROACH & MONITORING

### 4A. Business-model-specific risks

| Category | Risk | First financial line item that would deteriorate |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue model | Government counterparty concentration plus fixed-price contract risk | Gross margin compression on the Cost of Construction line (already visible: standalone gross margin fell from 19.7% to 16.2%, FY24-26, AR sheet 37) |
| Margin | Raw material price volatility not fully passed through | Cost of Material Consumed as a % of revenue rising quarter over quarter |
| Balance sheet | Growing Contract Assets/RUSCA with nil impairment, plus payables that are economically reverse-factoring (B02 finding) | Further rise in Contract Assets + RUSCA as % of revenue past ~71%, or any first impairment provision |
| Execution | SPV-level project stalls (NHAI already terminated one project, Ceigall Ludhiana Rupnagar; a second SPV's auditor resigned, B02/B03) | Stagnant % completion at a named HAM SPV, or a new Emphasis-of-Matter/termination disclosure |
| Structural | Renewables and T&D are still pre-revenue, a THIRD business model (tariff-based) layered on top of two already-different engines | First renewable/T&D segment-level revenue and margin disclosure, or continued 0.0% completion status past promised timelines |

### 4B. Valuation method applicability

| Method | Applicable? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP)** | **PRIMARY** | This hybrid needs the EPC engine valued on an earnings/EV-EBITDA basis and the HAM SPV portfolio valued on a DCF/NAV basis against its concession cash flows (annuity plus interest), because a single blended multiple conflates a project-execution earnings stream with a government-annuity financial-asset stream that has a fundamentally different risk, duration and cash-conversion profile. Renewables/T&D should be carried at cost/investment or as pure optionality given the 0.0% completion status |
| **Consolidated EV/EBITDA cross-check** | **SECONDARY** | Useful only as a sanity check against comparable-sized road EPC/HAM peers, and only with the explicit caveat that it blends the two engines |
| **Standalone EPC-only P/E or EV/EBITDA (ex-HAM investment income)** | **TERTIARY** | Isolates the pure-contracting earnings power for direct benchmarking against peer EPC contractors, since consolidated EPS is distorted by SPV-level finance cost and non-cash items |
| DDM | NOT APPLICABLE | No stated, disclosed dividend policy in the documents reviewed; NOT FOUND |
| Asset-based / replacement cost | NOT APPLICABLE | The EPC engine is not asset-heavy (PP&E ~6% of standalone assets); book value materially understates the HAM concession rights and materially overstates the EPC fixed-asset base |
| Which cycle stage matters for valuation | The industry is in a capex UPCYCLE (record MoRTH/NHAI budgets); Ceigall itself is EARLY in its own HAM-to-annuity transition (one asset monetised) and PRE-REVENUE in renewables/T&D — valuation should not extrapolate the mature-cycle multiple of a pure EPC contractor onto the still-unproven annuity and new-vertical pieces |

### 4C. Quarterly monitoring checklist (12 items)

1. Order book (Rs) and book-to-bill ratio — trend and sector mix
2. Order inflow (Rs) by quarter
3. Consolidated and standalone EBITDA margin
4. Consolidated and standalone CFO, and the gap between them
5. Contract Assets + RUSCA as % of trailing revenue
6. Any impairment/provision against Contract Assets or RUSCA
7. WIP days and Creditor days (trade-off already visible in FY26)
8. Interest Service Coverage Ratio
9. Consolidated Debt/Equity, and whether reverse-factoring-type payables are separately disclosed
10. HAM SPV-level % completion for each named project, watching for stalls
11. Any new HAM monetisation transaction, WITH disclosed sale consideration and gain/loss
12. First segment-level P&L disclosure for renewables/T&D (revenue, not just LOA value)

### 4D. Highest-value questions for management

1. Can you disclose gross margin separately for the EPC and HAM segments, rather than only a blended company-level margin? (Reassures: transparent segment economics; Worries: refusal or "not tracked separately")
2. What is the sale consideration and realised gain/loss on the Malout-Abohar HAM divestment, and will future HAM monetisations disclose this upfront? (Reassures: value-accretive, transparent terms; Worries: opaque or below-book pricing)
3. What share of the disclosed 41.68% HAM revenue is post-COD annuity/interest income versus construction-phase revenue? (Reassures: a growing, disclosed annuity share; Worries: refusal to split, implying it is still almost entirely construction-phase)
4. What is the expected timeline for renewable and T&D projects to move from 0% completion to first revenue recognition? (Reassures: a specific, near-term date; Worries: vague "in due course" language)
5. Why did the group's operating cash flow remain negative in FY26 while the parent's standalone operating cash flow turned strongly positive? (Reassures: a clear, quantified SPV-funding explanation; Worries: no clear answer beyond "growth investment")
6. What is driving Contract Assets and RUSCA to a combined ~71% of one year's revenue with nil impairment provisioning? (Reassures: specific, auditable milestone-certification timing reasons; Worries: a generic answer with no quantified ageing)
7. What continuity risk does the recent leadership churn (three CEO-level changes in ~12 months, B02/B03 finding) pose to execution of the current Rs 1,85,683m order book? (Reassures: a named, settled leadership team with tenure commitments; Worries: further churn)
