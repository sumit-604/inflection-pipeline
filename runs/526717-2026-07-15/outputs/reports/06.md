### Q2. RM (PP granule) pricing: "completely pass through" via annual rate contracts, or margin lag during spikes?

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Claim | HPBL/rating agency framing: PP granule cost increases are "completely pass through" (RATING p.5), implying no margin exposure to RM spikes |
| Verdict | **PARTIALLY VERIFIED** (peers complicate the "completely" framing) |
| Peer evidence | Emmbi (Q1 FY22 call, Aug 12 2021, p.11) — analyst Deepan Shankar: "Polymer price increase has not been able to pass onto customers so is that a lag?" Makrand Appalwar (CMD): **"Yes, there is a lag, there is a lag of around 30 to 45 days."** This is a direct, named-lag admission from a comparable peer, contradicting a "completely" pass-through characterisation, at least mechanically. Kanpur (Q3 FY26 call, Feb 20 2026, p.9) — Saket Kapoor asks about RM inflation impact; Shashank Agarwal: "polymer has also gone up by about 10% to 15%. But as you know in our industry this gets passed on to the customer. So there is no inflation risk from that standpoint." This supports full pass-through in direction. But Kanpur (Q4/FY26 call, May 4 2026, p.5) shows the mechanism is not automatic: "we follow a model of procuring raw material largely against confirmed orders, which helped us to manage price risk effectively and protect margins" — i.e. margin protection is achieved through active procurement discipline against booked orders, not a contractual, instantaneous pass-through clause. The same call (p.11, Madhur Rathi Q) clarifies the pass-through is applied on a **per-kilo basis, not a percentage basis** — meaning gross-margin percentage can actually expand when RM rises (an accounting artefact of fixed absolute markup), which is a different mechanism than the "completely pass through" phrasing implies. |
| Peers silent | Neither peer directly comments on HPBL's specific "annual rate contract" structure. |
| Net read | Both peers describe RM cost recovery as broadly effective, but neither describes it as instantaneous or contractually guaranteed. Emmbi names a concrete 30-45 day lag (dated evidence, same underlying commodity dynamics); Kanpur's more recent evidence shows pass-through is achieved through active order-matched procurement, not passive full pass-through — and during the FY26 Iran-conflict RM spike (PP up ~65%, $1,000→$1,700/ton, Kanpur Q4/FY26 call p.5), Kanpur itself flags demand-side effects (customers cutting order sizes, lead times compressing from 6-8 weeks to 3-4 weeks) even while claiming margin protection. This nuances rather than fully verifies HPBL's "completely pass through" language — the mechanism exists but is neither instant nor risk-free. |

### Q3. Capacity utilisation ramp for peers' recent FIBC expansions vs HPBL's 31% FY25 utilisation on its newly expanded 24,300 MTPA base

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Claim | Tests whether 31% FY25 utilisation is an industry-typical ramp curve for newly expanded FIBC capacity, or a company-specific execution lag |
| Verdict | **CONTRADICTED** |
| Peer evidence | Emmbi: pre-COVID utilisation was 76% (Jun 2021 call, Q4FY21, p.3); post-COVID it dipped to 69-70% but recovered to 87% within two quarters (Aug 2021 call, Q1FY22, p.3: "capacity utilization was up to 87%, pre-COVID our capacity utilization was about 76% and we are looking at 11% growth"), and stood at 84% in the following quarter even as a fresh 3,000-tonne capacity addition was being brought online (Nov 2021 call, Q2FY22, p.4). Kanpur: overall manufacturing utilisation was ~75% in FY25 and 75-77% in the most recent quarter reported at the time (Aug 2025 call, Q1FY26, p.15-16), with FIBC-specific throughput at 1,350 tons/month ramping to a targeted 1,800 tons/month within "one to two years" on internal-accrual-funded capex. By Q2 FY26 (Nov 2025 call, p.12), utilisation on an 18,000-tonne base was 82% (14,500 tons produced); by Q4/FY26 (May 2026 call, p.5, p.17) it was ~83-85% even while a fresh 6,000-tonne, multi-year Unit-3 expansion was under construction (target completion May 2026). |

| Net read | Two independent peers, one in an older cycle and one contemporaneous, both operate FIBC/technical-textile capacity in the mid-70s to mid-80s percent utilisation range as a matter of course, including during periods of active capacity expansion. This is a genuine, well-evidenced contradiction of the "31% is a normal ramp-curve" reading: the peer set suggests HPBL's 31% FY25 utilisation on its expanded 24,300 MTPA base is a company-specific execution lag rather than an industry-typical early-ramp number, though the comparison is imperfect since HPBL's 31% may reflect the very first partial year after the incremental 6,000 MTPA went live (1 Apr 2025) rather than a full-year steady state. |

### Q5. Capex plans and gearing trajectories: industry-wide capex pause, or peers investing counter-cyclically while HPBL holds back?

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Claim | Tests whether HPBL's "no major capex FY27-28" stance is a sector-wide pause or a company-specific capital constraint |
| Verdict | **CONTRADICTED** |
| Peer evidence | Kanpur is actively and materially expanding through the exact period HPBL is holding capex flat. It announced an INR105 crore capex program (Nov 2025 call, Q2FY26, p.4) comprising: INR58.04cr for a greenfield needle-punch non-woven facility (automotive interiors, shoe insoles, artificial leather, carpets — targeting INR120-125cr full-capacity turnover) and INR47cr for FIBC capacity expansion at Unit 3 (+1,200 MTPA/year, +6,000 MTPA over five years, target 18,000→24,000 tons). | Net read | The peer set does not support an industry-wide capex pause. This does not by itself invalidate HPBL's stance — HPBL's higher leverage (~3x D/E per B05) is a plausible company-specific reason to hold back — but it does mean HPBL is the more cautious operator relative to at least one directly comparable, currently active peer, not a participant in a sector-wide retrenchment. |

## PART 2: UNPROMPTED CROSS-READ

**2A. Demand environment.** Kanpur's four calls (Aug 2025-May 2026) describe a consistently positive demand backdrop through most of the period: a structural "tectonic shift" of labour-intensive manufacturing from China/Far East to India (Aug 2025 call, p.9), 80-85% repeat-customer revenue (Feb 2026 call, p.4), and a stable 2-2.5 month order book (Nov 2025 call, p.9). This softens somewhat in the final two calls: order-book visibility narrowed and lead times compressed from 6-8 weeks to 3-4 weeks (May 2026 call, p.6) as customers responded to the Iran-conflict-driven PP price spike with smaller, more frequent orders — described by management as inventory-adjustment, not demand destruction ("underlying demand and consumption absolutely remains intact," May 2026 call, p.5). Emmbi's 2021-22 narrative is a COVID-recovery story (export growth of ~27-30%, China+1 tailwinds) and is too dated to speak to current demand conditions. Neither peer frames demand growth in the specific CAGR/market-size terms HPBL's AR uses (see Q1), but both are consistent with a growing, export-led FIBC opportunity — this complicates rather than confirms HPBL's specific market-size framing while broadly supporting its directional growth narrative.

**2B. Pricing and input costs.** Peer evidence (Q2 above) shows RM cost recovery is real but neither instantaneous nor risk-free — it depends on order-matched procurement discipline (Kanpur) or carries a measurable lag (Emmbi, historically). Kanpur's FY26 experience of a 65% PP price spike (Iran conflict, $1,000→$1,700/ton, May 2026 call, p.5) with an accompanying near-term order-book softening is a live illustration that "pass-through" coexists with real short-term margin and demand risk, which complicates HPBL's undifferentiated "completely pass through" framing.

**2C. Capex cycle.** This is not an industry-wide capacity race — it is closer to a **single active expander (Kanpur) against a cautious HPBL**, with Emmbi (dated evidence) also modestly expanding rather than pausing. No peer in this set describes a sector-wide capex freeze; if anything, the peer evidence points to HPBL being the more capital-conservative operator in its immediate comparison set, consistent with (and perhaps explained by) its higher leverage per B05.

**2D. Competitive mentions.** No peer transcript in either set mentions HCP Plastene, "Plastene," or an unmistakable description of the company by name. `peer_mentions_of_company` is empty — this is a genuine silence, not a search failure; all 8 transcripts were read in full.

**2E. Risks peers discuss that HPBL's available filings do not (per B05, HPBL's FY25 AR Risk & Concerns section is "entirely generic").** Peers surface several specific, named risks largely absent from HPBL's disclosed materials: (i) explicit US tariff exposure and scenario planning (10%→25%→50%→18% progression tracked across three Kanpur calls, Aug 2025-May 2026); (ii) a formal, weekly-reviewed forex hedging policy (May 2026 call, p.13: "reviewed on a weekly basis every Saturday on our net exposures in dollar, euro and pound"); (iii) skilled-labour/manpower availability as a structural capacity constraint requiring in-house training schools (Nov 2025 call, p.12); (iv) recurring geopolitical supply-chain shocks named as a risk category in their own right — Red Sea crisis, Russia-Ukraine gas crisis, Iran conflict (May 2026 call, p.13); (v) RM price volatility risk with a defined "new normal" price band ($1,200-1,350/ton, May 2026 call, p.8). None of these appear as named, specific risks in HPBL's available filings per B05's characterisation of its risk disclosure as content-free.

## PART 4: TRIANGULATION SUMMARY

- Claims verified: 0 of 5 (fully VERIFIED, requiring two independent corroborating peers on direction and magnitude)
- Claims partially verified: 2 of 5 (Q2 RM pass-through mechanics; Q4 margin differential/competitive pressure)
- Claims contradicted: 2 of 5 (Q3 utilisation ramp; Q5 capex-pause framing) — **priority items for synthesis**
- Claims unverifiable: 1 of 5 (Q1 market-size/import-share figures)

**Single most consequential contradiction.** Q3: both independent peers (one dated, one contemporaneous) operate FIBC/technical-textile capacity at 75-90% utilisation as a matter of course, including during active expansion phases, with no peer evidence of anything resembling a sustained 31% floor on newly commissioned capacity. This makes HPBL's 31% FY25 utilisation on its newly expanded 24,300 MTPA base look like a company-specific execution lag rather than an industry-typical early-ramp number — a material qualifier on the utilisation-ramp trigger (priority 2 in B05) that synthesis should weight heavily, while noting the imperfect comparability (HPBL's 31% may reflect only a partial first year post-expansion).

**Single strongest independent confirmation.** Q4: Kanpur's own product-mix data cleanly shows a ~5-6pp EBITDA margin gap between its higher-value FIBC output (~12.5-13.5%) and its commodity fabric/multifilament output (~7%), plus an explicit, named account of Bangladesh/Vietnam competitive pricing pressure in the UK market and the structural EU-FTA offset — this is real, specific, single-peer support for the mechanism (if not the exact magnitude) behind HPBL's forward 7-9% margin-band claim.

**Overall assessment.** The peer set neither uniformly supports nor uniformly undermines HPBL's narrative — it complicates two of the three central operating claims and offers partial support to a third. On demand and margin-differentiation logic, the peer evidence (Kanpur especially) is broadly consistent with HPBL's direction, though it does not corroborate HPBL's specific market-size figures and shows RM pass-through to be a managed, not automatic, mechanism carrying real short-term risk. On the two claims most load-bearing for the medium-term thesis — capacity utilisation ramp and the "no major capex" capital-discipline stance — the peer evidence points the other way: comparable peers ramp faster and are currently expanding rather than pausing, which reframes HPBL's low utilisation as more plausibly company-specific and its capex restraint as a leverage-driven choice rather than a sector-wide pattern. Neither peer names HPBL directly, so this triangulation rests entirely on inference from independent peer behaviour, not on any peer's stated view of HPBL itself.
