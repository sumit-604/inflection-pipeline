### LBF-1 Customer shift (government 86.98% to 55.22%)

FACT. P.150: FY26 kits for 95 Mobile CSI vehicles went "through local dealers and established vehicle manufacturers", revenue Rs 20.36 Cr (2,035.74 lakh). That is 43.0% of non-government revenue and 19.3% of total revenue (computed).
FACT. Mobile CSI vehicle revenue fell from Rs 16.41 Cr to Rs 10.57 Cr (P.150, P.24). The kit revenue Rs 20.36 Cr exceeds the whole Mobile CSI line, so it sits in another segment. P.150 does not say which one.
FACT. Management's own reason: the channel shifts "the investment in vehicles" to local partners and cuts working capital (P.150).
NOT FOUND. Names of dealers, builders or any top-10 customer. Buyer class of this revenue. End-user state. Collections by buyer class. Every top customer is "Top Customer N" (P.160).
Cross-fact. Top Customer 3 FY26 (Rs 9.25 Cr, 8.75%) equals the rental line (Rs 9.25 Cr, P.24, P.160). Top Customer 6 FY26 (Rs 5.41 Cr) equals Assam state revenue (P.159). So single customers equal full state totals in some cases.

[INFERENCE 1] If the kits sit in Physical Evidence (Rs 39.11 Cr), that segment's 2.10x growth rests on Rs 20.36 Cr of dealer-channel kit revenue. The remainder is Rs 18.75 Cr, flat on FY25 Rs 18.59 Cr (+0.9%). This holds only if all kits are booked there. The filing does not say.

Two readings and the separating observation.
- Reading A: the non-government label is a billing artefact. Dealers and vehicle builders are billed instead of state police. The end buyer is still government. The government share fell because of channel, not customer mix. Collections risk then still sits with government payers behind a dealer.
- Reading B: the dealers and builders are real private intermediaries who pay faster. The prospectus says private buyers pay 30% to 50% at delivery (P.92, P.97).
- Separator: the receivables ageing by named buyer, or named dealers and end-user states for the 95 vehicles. The filings give neither. Customer advances are not visible either: the FY26 rise in other current liabilities (Rs 2.00 Cr to Rs 5.21 Cr) is credited to March GST, not advances (P.98).

Status: OPEN on names. The mechanism is now in the filing but absent from MD&A.

### LBF-2 Receivable days basis (83 / 89 / 74 vs 144 / 111 / 79)

FACT (computed). Year-end receivables over revenue: FY24 1,193.58 / 3,018.33 x 365 = 144.3 days. FY25 1,979.05 / 6,502.69 x 365 = 111.1. FY26 2,288.07 / 10,571.28 x 365 = 79.0. The prospectus prints the same ratio as 39.54%, 30.43%, 21.64% (P.91), which equals 144, 111 and 79 days.
FACT (computed). The stated 89 and 74 reproduce on AVERAGE receivables: FY25 (1,193.58 + 1,979.05) / 2 = 1,586.32, x 365 / 6,502.69 = 89.0. FY26 (1,979.05 + 2,288.07) / 2 = 2,133.56, x 365 / 10,571.28 = 73.7. FY24 83 cannot be tested: opening FY24 receivables are NOT FOUND in these sections.
FACT (computed). The other two day counts also reproduce on averages. Inventory on cost of sales: FY25 44.2, FY26 42.6 (stated 44, 43). Creditors on purchases: FY25 50.9, FY26 43.7 (stated 51, 44). Cost of sales = purchases plus change in inventory: FY26 7,998.16 - 287.08 = 7,711.08; FY25 4,905.05 - 427.90 = 4,477.15 (P.264).
Verdict. The prospectus headers say "Revenue from Operations", "Cost of sales", "Purchases" (P.91). They do not say "average". The same page prints the year-end ratio next to the average-basis days. Neither number is false. The table does not tell the reader which basis applies. The improvement is real on both bases: 111 to 79 days at year end, 89 to 74 on average.
Flag for later stages: year-end days are the right basis for a balance sheet that grew 62.6% in revenue within the year, and Q4 loading raises year-end receivables. The company admits Q4 FY25 was 51% of sales (P.92) and March concentration in FY26 (P.98). It also says the business "is not seasonal" (P.270).
