## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description

Venus Remedies makes and sells injectable medicines (mostly antibiotics and cancer drugs) to hospitals in India and to distributors and government health programmes in 100+ countries, and runs a small in-house drug research unit that is trying to invent new antibiotics for infections that current antibiotics can no longer treat (AR p.25, p.53).

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version

Think of Venus Remedies like a trusted local pharmacy that also runs a small factory in the back. Most of what the factory makes are injections doctors use for serious infections and cancer, the kind of medicine a hospital cannot do without, not the kind a patient skips buying. Almost 4 out of every 5 rupees Venus earns comes from selling these injections to hospitals and governments in other countries, not from India. The company also spends a little money each year, about 18 crore rupees, trying to invent brand-new antibiotics for infections that old antibiotics can no longer beat, the way a shopkeeper might set aside a little capital every year to test a new product line, knowing most experiments fail but one success could matter a lot. This year, for the first time in over ten years, Venus paid its shareholders a dividend, a signal that the shop is finally generating more cash than it needs to reinvest.

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage and Venus's position

The global sterile/generic injectables industry is in a growth phase (generic injectables CAGR 10.35% to 2035; generic sterile injectables CAGR 9.29% to 2035, AR p.43-44), driven by rising chronic disease and hospital-based care, not a mature or declining category. Within that growing industry, Venus is a mid-scale, specialised player pivoting from a volume/tender-led export model toward a margin-led, higher-value-therapy model: gross contribution on exports rose from 72% to 77% in one year and low-margin commodity products are being actively deprioritised (AR p.54). This is a company-level maturation move inside a still-growing industry, not evidence the industry itself is maturing.

### 3D. Unit economics — the physics of the business

- **Define one unit:** one saleable injectable dose (vial/ampoule/pre-filled syringe) shipped to a hospital, distributor, or government buyer.
- **Revenue per unit:** NOT FOUND, check investor presentation or concall. The AR discloses monthly production volume (record 1 Crore+ units in a single month, AR p.56) and a long-term production target (Venus Industrial Park designed for 100 Crore units/year against a "near-term operating range" of ~10 Crore units/year, AR p.58) but does not disclose average selling price per unit or annual unit volume shipped. A rough back-of-envelope (Rs769.60 Cr revenue against a ~10 Crore units/year operating range implies an average realisation in the tens of rupees per unit) is an analyst approximation only, not a company-disclosed figure, and should not be treated as anchored.
- **Cost per unit:** NOT FOUND, check investor presentation or concall (no per-unit cost disclosed; only aggregate cost of materials consumed, Rs387.95 Cr against Rs768.73 Cr standalone revenue, 50.5% of revenue, is available, AR standalone P&L).
- **Volume drivers:** new marketing-authorisation activations (511+ active of 1,100+ granted, 90 new grants in FY26), tender wins (Tier 1/2 government channels), ESI/CGHS/DGAFMS hospital additions, private-hospital penetration, and manufacturing throughput gains ("same-energy capacity growth" to 1 Crore+ units/month) (AR p.50-58).
- **Price drivers:** product mix shift toward oncology/AMR specialty molecules over commodity antibiotics; gross-contribution floors applied to new export commitments; selective tender participation filtered by margin rather than volume; NPPA/DPCO caps apply only within the <20-23% domestic price-controlled slice (AR p.52, p.54).
- **Cost drivers:** API price volatility (platinum-linked oncology inputs specifically flagged in FY26), freight/logistics volatility, currency movements on ~77-80% export revenue, and employee costs (Rs84.85 Cr, 11.0% of standalone revenue, workforce grew to 1,472) (AR p.54, p.63, standalone P&L).
- **Incremental margin / operating leverage:** strong and directly evidenced. FY26 revenue grew 17.9% (consolidated) while EBITDA nearly doubled (+89%, Rs84.48 Cr to Rs159.86 Cr) and PAT grew 127% (Rs45.31 Cr to Rs102.79 Cr), on record output achieved "without major new production-line investments" (AR p.56, p.68). This is the clearest evidence in the AR of positive operating leverage: fixed manufacturing and compliance infrastructure absorbing a materially higher volume at a falling cost per unit ("same-energy capacity growth").

| Method | Applicable? | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| PE-based relative/comps valuation | **PRIMARY** | Company is now solidly profitable (standalone PAT Rs99.31 Cr, consolidated PAT Rs102.79 Cr, FY26), debt-free, and the earnings base has just stepped up materially; a peer-relative earnings multiple is the standard entry point for a specialty/branded generic exporter, provided the framework's Section 1B destination-PE governs the exit multiple per pipeline rules |
| DCF (discounted cash flow) | **SECONDARY** | Cash conversion is strong and directly evidenced (operating cash flow Rs155 Cr exceeded EBITDA for the second consecutive year, AR p.68) and the company is debt-free, both supportive of a DCF cross-check; the main DCF risk is the short track record of the current margin level (record year, no multi-year history at this profitability), so DCF should be a cross-check on the PE-based primary, not the sole anchor |
| Pipeline/optionality (rNPV-style) value for the R&D pipeline | **TERTIARY** | VRP-034 is only entering Phase 1 and most candidates remain preclinical; a formal risk-adjusted NPV is premature at this stage of clinical development and should be tracked as an optionality overlay, not embedded in the core valuation, until at least Phase 2 data exists |
| EV/Sales | Not applicable as primary | Margin is inflecting rapidly (EBITDA margin nearly doubled in one year), which makes a sales multiple a poor read on intrinsic value right now; useful only as a sanity check against PE/DCF |
| Dividend discount model | Not applicable | Dividend was resumed only in FY26 after "more than a decade" of no payout (AR p.68); no dividend track record exists to model a growth/discount rate against |
| Asset-based / replacement value | Not applicable | Not a distressed, asset-heavy, or holding-company situation; the value driver is earnings and regulatory-capital optionality, not net asset value |
| Sum-of-the-parts | Not applicable at this stage | Single reporting segment (AR Note 45); no disclosed sub-business economics (domestic vs export vs CMO vs R&D) granular enough to value separately with confidence |

Cycle stage that matters for valuation: the company is at an **earnings inflection point** (consolidated PAT +127% YoY, EBITDA margin +781bps to 20.44%, first dividend in a decade), not mid-cycle steady state and not a downcycle trough. Valuation work in Stage 11 should explicitly test how much of the FY26 step-up is durable (product-mix shift, gross-contribution discipline) versus a one-year base effect, before applying any peer multiple.
