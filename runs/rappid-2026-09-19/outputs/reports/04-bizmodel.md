## SECTION 1: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PLAIN ENGLISH

### 1A. One-line description
Rappid designs, casts, machines, assembles, tests and certifies industrial and marine
valves (15mm-600mm, ferrous and non-ferrous metals) at one Palghar factory, selling
build-to-order to industrial plants and, increasingly, to Indian shipyards building navy
and commercial vessels (AR p.20-21; Inv. Pres. slide 7-8).

### 1B. Money flow chain, by revenue stream

**Stream 1: Industrial valves (67% of FY26 revenue, Inv. Pres. slide 11)**
[Metal billets/castings/bars: carbon steel, stainless steel, brass, bronze, duplex steel
purchased from foundries] → [Rappid CNC-machines, assembles, pressure-tests and
certifies the valve to the customer's spec] → [ships to an oil & gas, chemical, ethanol,
water-treatment, EPC or power-sector buyer as a component of their process piping] →
[buyer is the EPC contractor, OEM or end-industry plant] → [paid against purchase order
terms; advances vary by customer, PSU tenders carry no price-escalation clause once
awarded (Jun-2026 concall, line 246)].

**Stream 2: Marine valves (33% of FY26 revenue, Inv. Pres. slide 11; this splits across
two AR industry-vertical lines: "Marine" 8.34% and "Shipbuilding & Repair" 24.51%,
combined 32.85%, AR p.24)**
[Same metal inputs, but type-approved alloys for saline resistance] → [Rappid
manufactures to marine classification-society specification (ClassNK Japan, DNV, Marine
Systems Research Institute) and type-approves each valve design before it can be
quoted] → [delivers to Indian shipyards, PSU (Mazagon Dock, Garden Reach, Cochin
Shipyard, Udupi, Hindustan Shipyard) and private (L&T Shipbuilding), for installation on
naval and commercial vessels] → [buyer is the shipyard or a systems integrator such as
BHEL, Shree Refrigerations or Muller-BBM Acoustic on a specific naval programme
(Fleet Support Ship, FSS)] → [paid on long-cycle contracts, 1-3 years execution (AR
p.27); PSU tenders again carry no escalation clause (concall line 246), some private
orders carry 20-30% advance (concall line 1144)].

**Stream 3: Valve automation / actuators (embedded in both streams above, no separate
% disclosed)**
[Rappid buys actuators from an OEM] → [integrates the actuator with its own valve body
to deliver a complete automated flow-control unit] → [same industrial or marine
customer] → [paid as part of the composite valve-plus-actuator order] (Inv. Pres.
slide 12).

### 1E. The chai-stall-uncle version
Think of a valve as a tap, but built for a factory pipe or a ship's hull instead of your
kitchen sink. Rappid makes these taps to exact specification: for a chemical plant, a
brewery, or a warship. Two-thirds of what they sell today still goes to ordinary
factories. But the growing one-third goes to shipyards building navy and commercial
ships, where a tap failure at sea can sink the ship, so the buyer will only accept a tap
that has passed years of testing and holds a foreign classification society's stamp of
approval. That stamp is hard to get, which is why not many Indian companies can sell
there. The catch: shipyards, especially government ones, agree on a price up front and
never move it, even if the metal Rappid buys gets more expensive mid-order. So Rappid
sometimes chooses to hold finished valves in its warehouse rather than deliver at a
loss, which is exactly what happened in the second half of FY26.

### 2D. Industry lifecycle stage
The global valves market (USD 82.9 Bn in 2025, projected USD 136.0 Bn by 2034, 5.7%
CAGR, IMARC via AR p.22) and the Indian industrial valves market (USD 3.4 Bn to
USD 6.3 Bn, 6.64% CAGR, same source) are both in a mature, steadily growing phase, not
an early or a declining one. Rappid's OWN position within this mature industry is an
early-stage mix-shift: its marine/shipbuilding revenue share rose from 0.5% (FY23) to
32.85% (FY26, AR p.24), i.e. the company is climbing into a younger, faster-growing
sub-segment (Indian naval shipbuilding, backed by a stated Rs 2.3 lakh crore naval
modernisation pipeline and 75% domestic-procurement reservation, Inv. Pres. slide 27)
inside an otherwise mature parent industry.

### 3D. Unit economics — the physics of the business
Define one unit: a single valve (or valve-plus-actuator assembly), size range
15mm-600mm across seven product families (ball, gate, globe, butterfly, check, double
block, strainer) plus marine-specific variants (Inv. Pres. slide 13, 16).

- Revenue per unit: NOT FOUND. The AR and Inv. Pres. disclose revenue by product TYPE
  (Rupees and % of total, AR p.24) and by industry VERTICAL, never a volume count or an
  average selling price per valve. A 15mm valve and a 600mm marine globe valve for the
  FSS programme sit at entirely different price points, so a single blended
  revenue-per-unit figure would not be meaningful even if disclosed.
- Cost per unit: NOT FOUND at the unit level; only company-wide material cost as % of
  revenue (70.9% FY26, AR p.26) is disclosed.
- Volume drivers: order-book conversion (Rs 40 Cr executable at Q1 FY27), shipyard
  tender win-rate (management cites ~Rs 40-50 Cr bid, ~Rs 8-10 Cr won in the PSU channel
  as of the Jun-2026 call, concall line 1196), and capacity headroom (management states
  the plant supports up to ~Rs 120 Cr revenue without further expansion, concall
  line 206).
- Price drivers: type-approval/certification status (gates which tenders Rappid can even
  bid), customer channel (PSU tenders fixed-price with no escalation clause vs private/
  export orders where price revision is sometimes negotiable, concall lines 246, 400),
  and product complexity/size (larger, exotic-alloy, marine-certified valves command
  higher realisation, qualitatively evidenced by the NAB Globe Valve development for the
  FSS programme, AR p.30-31/Inv. Pres. slide 21, but not quantified).
- Cost drivers: input metal price (copper and non-ferrous alloys, the single largest
  named cost and margin risk, AR p.23, p.26), working-capital carrying cost (short-term
  borrowing more than doubled to fund inventory and receivables, AR p.26), and employee
  cost (rose from 3.9% to 5.3% of revenue, AR p.26, as the company scales organisational
  capacity).
- Incremental margin and operating leverage: management's own framing implies operating
  leverage IS present on well-priced orders (EBITDA margin rose from 19.05% to 19.36%
  even as revenue grew only 2.1%, AR p.31) but is being suppressed by roughly Rs 22 Cr
  of carried, low-margin orders that must still be executed (concall lines 681-688,
  "these 22 crores will probably have some hit on profit margin"). The physics: Rappid
  can flex margin UP by selectively bidding profitable orders (as it did in H2 FY26,
  choosing to hold Rs 10-12 Cr of orders rather than accept thin margins), but cannot
  flex COST down mid-contract on fixed-price PSU awards if input prices move against it.

## SECTION 4: RISKS, VALUATION APPROACH & MONITORING

### 4A. Business-model-specific risks

| Category | Risk | First financial line item to deteriorate |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue model | PSU fixed-price tenders carry no escalation clause; a commodity spike forces a choice between margin erosion or holding delivery (already occurred H2 FY26, AR p.23; concall line 246) | Revenue from operations, quarter-over-quarter, diverging from the executable order book |

A slowdown in these sectors... could adversely affect overall business performance", AR p.23); certification/regulatory dependency also named | Marine + Shipbuilding & Repair revenue % of total, and its trend if the naval order pipeline slows |

### 4B. Valuation method applicability

| Method | Applicable? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| P/E on trailing or forward earnings | SECONDARY | Usable as a cross-check once the FY26 order-timing distortion (H1 +46.9% / H2 -24.9%, AR p.25-26) is normalised; not a clean standalone signal given the single-year growth deceleration |
| EV/EBITDA | Not primary | Reasonable for a manufacturer but does not distinguish the marine-mix-shift re-rating thesis from the base industrial business; use only as a sanity check |
| DCF | Not applicable at this evidence stage | Cash flow has been negative every year FY23-FY26; a DCF built on projected CFO recovery would be forecasting the very thing not yet evidenced, contrary to the Never-anchor-to-hope rule |
| Sum-of-the-parts (industrial vs marine) | PRIMARY | The two revenue streams have genuinely different economics (fixed-price/long-cycle/certification-gated marine vs shorter-cycle, more competitive industrial); the framework's Section 1B destination-PE approach (applied per stream, then blended by mix) is the most defensible way to value a business mid-transition on the Quality Ladder |
| Order-book/revenue-visibility multiple | TERTIARY, supporting only | The executable order book (Rs 40 Cr at Q1 FY27, vs Rs 53 Cr FY26 revenue) is a useful cross-check on near-term revenue but is not itself a valuation method; feed it into the SOTP forward-revenue assumption |
| Asset-based/NAV | Not applicable | Rappid is an operating manufacturer, not an asset-holding or distressed entity; PPE is not the driver of value here |
| Comparable-company multiples (peer set) | SECONDARY, if peer data becomes available | KSB, Quest Flow Controls, Atam Valves named in company memory as candidate peers; not independently validated this stage (peer financials not loaded) |

PRIMARY method: sum-of-the-parts by revenue stream (industrial vs marine/naval), with
each stream's exit multiple governed exclusively by Section 1B. SECONDARY cross-check:
trailing/forward P/E and comparable-company multiples once peer data is available.
TERTIARY, supporting only: order-book-to-revenue visibility ratio, feeding the forward
revenue assumption, never used as a standalone multiple. Cycle stage that matters for
valuation: the marine/naval sub-cycle (India's naval modernisation programme, Inv. Pres.
slide 27) is early-and-rising; the base industrial-valve cycle is mature and steady. The
blended multiple should reflect the REVENUE MIX weighting between these two cycle
positions, not a single company-wide cycle read.

### 4C. Quarterly/half-yearly monitoring checklist

1. Executable order book (Rs Cr) and its QoQ change — good: rising faster than trailing
   revenue; trouble: flat or falling for two consecutive updates.
2. Marine + Shipbuilding & Repair revenue % of total — good: continuing to rise or hold
   above 30%; trouble: reverting toward the FY23 base (0.5%).
3. Revenue from operations vs "Sales" as separately labelled in business updates — good:
   the company clarifies which figure is which each time; trouble: the terms are used
   interchangeably again without clarification (see the 09-Jul/10-Jul-2026 revision,
   Section 1B note).
4. CFO for the half-year/year — good: turns positive or CFO/PAT recovers above ~0.7x;
   trouble: a fifth consecutive negative year.
5. Trade receivable days / turnover — good: turnover recovers toward 2.7x+ (FY24 level);
   trouble: turnover falls further below 2.0x.
6. Inventory days / turnover — good: turnover recovers toward 2.7x+ (FY25 level);
   trouble: further decline, or inventory value growing faster than revenue again.
7. Short-term borrowings balance — good: growth decelerates relative to revenue growth;
   trouble: continues doubling-pattern seen in FY26.
8. Rs 22 Cr low-margin carried-order book (concall line 681) — good: this backlog is
   worked through and margin recovers to or above 19%; trouble: EBITDA margin falls
   toward the "12%" level the CMD flagged as undesirable (concall line 571).
9. Rs 10-12 Cr of orders held back for price revision (AR p.23) — good: converted to
   delivered revenue at protected margin; trouble: remains undelivered for multiple
   quarters (working-capital and inventory-valuation risk).
10. FY27 growth delivery vs "closing to 50% or more" guidance (concall line 416) — good:
    quarterly delivery tracks toward that range; trouble: repeat of the FY26 H1/H2
    guidance-vs-delivery gap (guided 50% CAGR FY26-27 per concall line 192-193, actual
    FY26 delivery +2.1%).
11. Repeat-customer % reconciliation — good: company discloses a single, consistent
    figure with a defined denominator; trouble: continues to state two different
    numbers (60% vs 75%) without reconciliation.
12. Debt-Equity ratio — good: stabilises or falls back toward 0.19x-0.25x; trouble:
    continues rising past 0.35x without a matching CFO recovery.
13. New certifications/type-approvals obtained — good: incremental approvals widen the
    addressable marine/export market; trouble: none added for several quarters while
    marine ambitions are still stated.

### 4D. Highest-value questions for management

1. What is the FY27 actual naval-specific revenue (or a defined proxy) versus
   commercial shipbuilding within the combined Marine + Shipbuilding & Repair line?
   Reassuring answer: a disclosed split showing naval revenue growing in line with the
   FSS order intake. Worrying answer: continued refusal or inability to disaggregate.
2. Of the Rs 40 Cr executable order book at Q1 FY27, how much sits on PSU fixed-price
   terms with no escalation clause, and how much on private/export terms with some price
   flexibility? Reassuring: a rising private/export share. Worrying: an unchanged or
   rising PSU fixed-price share with commodity prices still volatile.
3. What concrete plan, with a date, will turn CFO positive, given four consecutive years
   of negative CFO? Reassuring: a specific working-capital target (e.g. receivable days
   below 120) with a stated timeline. Worrying: a repeat of the generic "monitoring
   inventory and receivables" mitigation language already used in the AR (p.24).
4. Reconcile the 60% (Inv. Pres.) versus ~75% (Jun-2026 concall) repeat-customer figures.
   Reassuring: a clear, consistently applied definition going forward. Worrying: the
   discrepancy persists or widens.
5. How much of the Rs 22 Cr low-margin carried-order backlog (cited on the Jun-2026 call)
   remains unexecuted as of the most recent quarter, and what margin is it actually
   printing? Reassuring: the backlog is shrinking and its margin drag is quantified.

6. Given "nobody likes to be at the 12% margin point" (concall line 571), what specific
   mechanism (order selection, pricing, cost) will lift EBITDA margin sustainably above
   the FY26 19.36% level, and by when? Reassuring: a named, measurable lever. Worrying:

7. Now that the entire IPO acquisitions budget (Rs 4 Cr) and unspent capex (Rs 3.65 Cr)
   has been redirected to working capital (AR p.27), what is the company's updated
   inorganic-growth strategy, if any? Reassuring: a clear statement that organic
   marine-mix growth is now the sole strategy. Worrying: continued reference to
   acquisitions without funding earmarked for them.

### Archetype classification (CLAUDE.md Archetype Library)
Rappid best fits TWO archetypes simultaneously, split by revenue stream, which is itself
the central business-model fact this run must carry forward:
- **Build-to-spec component maker** (industrial-valve stream, 67% of revenue): customer
  capex/opex cycle-driven demand, no design-win pipeline disclosed beyond repeat orders,
  input-cost pass-through attempted but constrained on fixed-price awards.
- **Order-book business (EPC/defence/capital goods variant)** (marine/naval stream, 33%
  of revenue): order inflow and executable order book are the key forward indicators,
  book-to-bill visible via the quarterly business updates, execution pace matters over a
  1-3 year cycle, and margin on backlog is explicitly a live issue (the Rs 22 Cr
  low-margin carried orders, concall line 681).
Neither archetype alone describes the company; the transition thesis (Quality Ladder
Part B1, out of scope for this stage) is precisely the shift of revenue weight FROM the
first archetype TOWARD the second.
