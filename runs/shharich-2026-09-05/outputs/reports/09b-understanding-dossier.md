## SECTION 2: MENTAL MODEL DECLARATION

### PART A — THE FROM STATE (the anchor, not the model)

**A1. ARCHETYPE (per line).**
- *H-Acid manufacturing* (78.5% of FY26 revenue, B04): **Commodity converter** (Section 1B v3.7 Amendment 17 binds). Margin is the spread between H-Acid realisation and the naphthalene-chain/sulphur input basket, not price power. The company is a price-taker against more than ten listed Indian competitors, with no patent, brand, or cost-curve edge demonstrated (B01 M2/M9 both score below peer median; B04 pricing_power "price-taker").
- *Traded H-Acid resale* (18.2% of FY26 revenue, new in FY26, B04): fits no archetype in the CLAUDE.md library. It is a pass-through at roughly 1.03% gross margin (B02, Note 30 versus Note 27) with no processing step, no moat test scored, and no stated strategic rationale. It is treated here as a dilutive add-on to the headline growth number, not a business line with its own economics.

**A2. THE SIMPLE ANALOGY.** Shree Hari buys naphthalene-chain and sulphur-family chemicals, reacts them into H-Acid, and sells the H-Acid to dye makers who turn it into the colour that ends up in dyed cotton, silk, wool, nylon and acrylic. The company earns the gap between what it pays for those inputs and what it can charge for H-Acid, a gap that moves with global chemical prices, not with any special hold the company has over its customers. In FY26 the underlying business grew only a little. Most of the year's headline growth came from a new sideline: buying finished H-Acid from someone else and reselling it at almost no profit, which inflated the revenue line without inflating the underlying business. At the same time, the promoter family is funding a second plant through a wholly owned subsidiary, meant to add H-Acid and other chemical volume beyond the single Mahad site. That second plant has been under construction for more than two years and has produced nothing yet.

### PART B — THE TRANSITION (the model)

**Transition line: H-Acid manufacturing (dominant, 78.5% of FY26 revenue).** The trading line is not itself declared a transition line; it is tracked as a dilution risk against the manufacturing line's own growth read (Part C1).

**B2. **B5. **B6. THE TRANSITION FALSIFIER.** Either of two prints kills the transition thesis specifically (as distinct from the business, see C3): (a) organic manufactured H-Acid growth stays below 5-10% for a second consecutive year while headline growth is again carried by a trading or spread effect (B02, B04 must_track_metrics); or (b) Shakambhari Dyechem reports a third consecutive year of Nil turnover with its CWIP ageing further, unaccompanied by any disclosed capacity or commissioning date (B07 catalysts_12m). ## SECTION 3: BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING NARRATIVE

Shree Hari Chemicals Export makes H-Acid, a key intermediate dyers need to produce reactive and acid dyes for cotton, silk, wool, nylon and acrylic fibres, at a single plant in Mahad, Maharashtra it has run since 1987 (B04). Manufactured H-Acid made up 78.5% of FY26 revenue. A new, near-zero-margin line reselling H-Acid bought from others added a further 18.2% (B04). Customers are B2B dye and textile-chemical makers in India and in four export markets the annual report counts but never names (B03, B09). They can switch suppliers on price, since H-Acid is a globally traded commodity with more than ten listed Indian competitors and no patent or brand lock-in holding them to this one plant (B01, B04). The company discloses no top-customer or top-product concentration figure beyond its single-product, single-segment classification (B02, B03). Present demand comes from steady domestic dyeing and textile-chemical use plus a sudden FY26 export step-change: export revenue moved from zero to 46.4% of sales, with no driver the company names (B02, B03). Testing whether that step-change is a durable new market or a one-year pass-through is exactly what six downstream indicators can do from outside the company's own filings: India's H-Acid trade volumes, CHEMEXCIL's export bulletins, a global H-Acid spot-price index, the status of any China anti-dumping case, peer dye-manufacturer sourcing disclosures, and any US tariff action reaching Indian dye-intermediate exports (B09). Demand should grow if the wholly owned subsidiary Shakambhari Dyechem starts producing dye intermediates and other chemicals on capacity the annual report never quantifies, and if the company's own H-Acid manufacturing volume actually rises, rather than FY26's growth staying trading-line and spread driven (B03, B04, B07). The externally verifiable signal for the second half of that question already exists in peer transcripts: they confirm a real global H-Acid price spike from February 2026, but show the sector turning price-led with volume sacrificed in the exact window covering this company's own Q1 FY27 inflection (B06). The competitive advantage, where it exists, sits in the H-Acid manufacturing line's customer stickiness and capital efficiency: revenue grew every year while receivable days fell, and asset turnover stayed high relative to capital employed, both scored at full marks in the Gate 0 moat scan (B01). That line's advantage does not extend to cost position, scale, or brand: operating margin runs well below its two comparable peers, market capitalisation is the smallest of the four companies compared, and the gross-margin proxy sits below the peer median (B01). The trading line carries no moat at all; it is a pass-through at roughly 1% gross margin with no scored strength in the moat framework (B04). The forward-looking Emerging Moat scan finds almost nothing building beneath either line: only captive solar power scores as a moderate, documented category, while the subsidiary that should anchor any new capability has sat non-operational for more than two years with aged, uncapitalised construction-in-progress (B07).

## SECTION 5: PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY

1. Shree Hari Chemicals Export makes H-Acid, a chemical used to make dyes for textiles, at a factory in Mahad, Maharashtra.
2. The company has run this factory since 1987 and describes itself as one of India's largest H-Acid makers.
3. It also resells H-Acid bought from others, a low-margin trading line that started in FY26.
4. Buyers are dye and textile-chemical makers in India and abroad, in a business-to-business model.
5. Demand exists because dyers need H-Acid to make reactive and acid dyes; the company sells to four export countries, unnamed, and to domestic customers.
6. Demand may grow if a new subsidiary, Shakambhari Dyechem, starts making more chemicals, and if the company's own H-Acid volume rises.
7. The corpus cannot yet prove that growth is happening: real H-Acid manufacturing revenue grew only about 5% in FY26, while the 31% headline growth came mostly from the new trading line.
8. The moat sits in customer stickiness and capital efficiency: customers keep buying, and payment days improved even as revenue grew.
9. The moat does not sit in cost position, scale, or brand; on all three the company scores below its named peers.
10. The mental model is a climb from a spread-driven commodity price-taker toward a steadier, cost-advantaged converter, funded by promoter-family capital, not yet proven.
11. The evidence read is fragile: several outside facts, H-Acid prices, sulphur costs, and the subsidiary's start-up all have to go right, and most of them come only from the company's own filings, not from outside sources.
12. The corpus could not establish actual production volume or capacity utilisation anywhere in the 181-page annual report, despite a stated 270-tonne installed capacity.
13. The corpus could not establish who buys most of the exports, or in which four countries, since the report never names them.
14. The two biggest open questions on the transition are whether next year's manufactured H-Acid growth clears 10-15%, and whether the subsidiary ever starts producing revenue after two-plus stalled years.
