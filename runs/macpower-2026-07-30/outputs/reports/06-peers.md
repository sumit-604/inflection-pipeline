## PART 2: UNPROMPTED CROSS-READ

**2A. Demand environment.** JYOTICNC and KLBRENG both describe a strong, broad-based Indian capex upcycle: JYOTICNC capacity-constrained with >20% CAGR CNC consumption growth over three years and an order book (Rs4,732cr, FY26 close) growing despite turning away business it cannot deliver within customer-acceptable lead times; KLBRENG's inquiry pipeline has doubled from Rs2,000cr to Rs4,000cr over 24 months with 47–50% FY26 revenue growth. ADOR, by contrast, describes a much more muted environment — revenue growth of only ~3–5% across FY25–FY26, volumes "flattish," and (Q4 FY26 call) capex conditions "slightly weaker than expected." The split is informative: strength is concentrated in specialised/high-precision capital equipment (CNC, process/heat-recovery), not in ADOR's more commoditised welding-consumables base. None of the three peers speak specifically to demand at the entry/mid-tier CNC machine segment that is Macpower's core market — JYOTICNC's growth is increasingly aerospace/defence and high-end weighted, which may not read straight across to Macpower's customer base.

**2B. Pricing and input costs.** None of the three peers describe genuine pricing power as the source of margin gains. JYOTICNC's blended EBITDA margin holds near 25% even as per-machine realisation and per-machine margin have *declined* (average realisation down to ~Rs34–36 lakh/machine, machine-level margin down from ~Rs13 lakh to ~Rs10.85 lakh) as machine count and entry/mid-tier mix rise — margin resilience here is a scale/cost story, not a pricing-power story. KLBRENG explicitly manages input-cost risk by booking material immediately on order receipt rather than relying on pass-through pricing, and cites steel-price moves as the main source of quarter-to-quarter gross-margin variance. ADOR ties its own margin path to cost discipline against a backdrop of import/unorganised-sector price competition. Net: the peer set complicates rather than supports a pure "pricing power" reading of margin expansion — mix and cost management, not realisation growth, appear to be doing the work across the sector, consistent with (but not proof of) Macpower's own mix-shift framing.

**2C. Capex cycle.** This is a genuine, industry-wide capacity build among the two capital-equipment-adjacent peers, not a lone-expander situation: JYOTICNC is executing a ~Rs450cr capex programme nearly tripling machine capacity (6,000→16,000/year), funded partly by ~Rs300cr of new debt; KLBRENG is running parallel brownfield expansions (Kilburn standalone + ME Energy) targeting Rs800cr–1,000cr revenue by FY27/28. ADOR, in the same period, is holding capex to ~Rs30–40cr/year of largely maintenance/small debottlenecking spend with no major greenfield addition. Read together: this looks like a genuine capacity race concentrated in precision/high-value capital equipment (machine tools, process equipment) riding PLI/defence/import-substitution tailwinds, while a more mature, commoditised capital-goods peer (ADOR) sits out the race. This is closer to "industry-wide capacity race" than "lone Macpower expander," at least among the machine-tool/capital-equipment peers — a supportive read for Macpower's own capex decisions.

**2D. Competitive mentions of Macpower.** None. Across all 12 transcripts (8 quarters x ADOR/KLBRENG unrelated segments, plus JYOTICNC's four calls), no management team or sell-side analyst names Macpower or gives an unmistakable description of it. Given JYOTICNC's scale (order book alone ~8–10x Macpower's revenue), this silence is unsurprising and not informative in either direction — it does not indicate obscurity beyond what scale alone would predict.

**2E. Risks peers discuss that Macpower's own materials do not.**
- **Export-control/geopolitical compliance risk (JYOTICNC, Q4 FY26 call):** an ongoing French regulatory investigation into JYOTICNC's Huron subsidiary over potential re-export of CNC machines toward Russia via third-country channels; the Huron general manager suspended pending the probe; Rs67cr of revenue deferred (not written off) pending export-licence resolution; management states the investigation "could take very longer time, 2 years, 3 years, 5 years, we don't know." This is a live compliance/geopolitical risk specific to the CNC machine-tool export trade that Macpower's materials do not address.
- **Import-substitution failing to materialise (JYOTICNC):** "imports are surging" even as the domestic market grows — the risk that faster-growing imports permanently capture share ahead of domestic capacity, rather than domestic players closing the gap, is a real counter-scenario to the import-substitution thesis that Macpower's own framing does not name.
- **Shipping/logistics disruption (KLBRENG, Q4 FY26 call):** Middle East conflict disrupted international shipping routes and industrial-gas availability, delaying export dispatches by "2 to 3 months" — a live supply-chain/geopolitical risk not referenced in Macpower's materials.
- **Manpower/talent scarcity (KLBRENG, Q1 FY26 call):** blue-collar and white-collar talent retention named as an explicit ongoing risk to growth execution across the capital-goods sector.
- **Structural import/unorganised-sector price competition (ADOR):** ~65% of ADOR's welding-equipment segment is import-served (largely Chinese) — a reminder that "import-heavy market" can as easily be read as a standing competitive threat as a substitution opportunity, a framing Macpower's growth narrative may understate.

## PART 4: TRIANGULATION SUMMARY

- **Claims verified:** 0 of 6 fully VERIFIED (strict two-peer, direction-and-magnitude bar not met by any claim)
- **Partially verified:** 4 of 6 (Q1 industry growth, Q2 import-component dependency, Q4 approval-delay mechanism, Q6 margin-mechanism)
- **Unverifiable:** 1 of 6 (Q3 market-share reconciliation)
- **Claims contradicted:** 1 of 6 (Q5 defence tender-bid conversion) — priority item for synthesis

**Most consequential contradiction:** JYOTICNC's aerospace/defence order intake (27–46% of quarterly order intake, >Rs800cr executed in FY26, Rs180cr in a single quarter from Indian ordnance factories) is an order of magnitude larger and faster-growing than the modest, slow-converting Rs300–400cr bid-pipeline picture Macpower paints of itself. Either Macpower is genuinely under-realising a tender pool that a scale peer is capturing well, or — more likely — the two companies are bidding into different sub-segments of "defence" (Jyoti's blend of private/global aerospace primes and Indian ordnance vs. Macpower's narrower PSU-tender exposure). Synthesis should not treat this as a clean like-for-like comparison, but should flag the scale gap explicitly.

**Strongest independent confirmation:** JYOTICNC's unprompted statement that Indian CNC consumption has grown ">20% CAGR" over the last three years, paired with its own capacity-constrained order book and "imports are surging" commentary, sits squarely inside Macpower's claimed 15–30% growth band — delivered by the sector's largest listed domestic player, with no reason to inflate a growth number on behalf of a much smaller proxy company.

**Overall:** The peer set **complicates** more than it flatly supports or undercuts Macpower's narrative. It independently corroborates the core macro setup — genuine double-digit CNC demand growth, a structurally import-heavy market, and a real mix/backward-integration mechanism for margin expansion that recurs (at different absolute levels) across all three peers. But it complicates two specific pillars: the defence-tender-conversion story looks structurally smaller and slower for Macpower than the scale peer's own tender-pool experience, and the import-component-lead-time-driven-inventory-build causal chain is only partially corroborated (import dependency is real; the specific lead-time/inventory mechanism is not confirmed by any peer). No peer statement outright refutes Macpower's transition thesis, but conviction on the defence-tender pillar and the precise IMTMA/market-share figures should be tempered accordingly.

## PART 5: CROSS-PEER HYPOTHESIS

JYOTICNC and KLBRENG — both riding a genuine Indian capital-goods capex upcycle — are demand-unconstrained but *capacity*-constrained: both describe turning away or delaying orders rather than competing on price, and both are mid-way through large capacity expansions specifically to capture demand they say they are currently leaving on the table. ADOR, operating in a more commoditised, consumables-weighted segment of the same broad "capital goods" universe, shows the opposite pattern: flat-to-low-single-digit growth, minimal capex, and explicit competitive pressure from unorganised/Chinese imports on price.

**Hypothesis:** the current Indian capital-goods upcycle is bifurcating along a technical-differentiation axis, not a generic "capex cycle" axis. Growth, margin, and capacity investment are concentrating in technically differentiated, IP-protected equipment tiers (precision CNC machine tools, specialised process/heat-recovery equipment) where domestic supply is structurally short of demand — hence JYOTICNC's "imports are surging" alongside 20%+ demand growth, and KLBRENG's doubling inquiry pipeline. Meanwhile the commoditised/consumables tier of the same broad industrial-equipment universe (ADOR's core welding business) is not participating in an equivalent boom and instead competes on cost against unorganised and import supply. This is testable and specific to Macpower: if Macpower's own entry/mid-tier machine backlog and realisations show the pricing pressure and modest growth characteristic of ADOR's experience — rather than the capacity-constrained, price-agnostic order books of JYOTICNC/KLBRENG — that would validate the bifurcation and suggest Macpower sits closer to the commoditised end of the CNC spectrum than its own premium-mix-shift narrative implies, making its stated margin-expansion path structurally harder to achieve than peer disclosures on their own would suggest. No individual peer states this bifurcation; it only emerges from comparing capacity-utilisation and growth behaviour across all three peer sets side by side.
